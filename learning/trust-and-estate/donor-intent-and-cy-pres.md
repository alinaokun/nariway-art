# Donor intent and cy-près — what a restricted gift is *for* when the institution changes or disappears

*Study note, not legal advice. Confirm the governing state law and the specific gift instrument before relying on any of this in a real matter. Cross-links: [[executor-authority-over-art]], [[curriculum]], [[sources]].*

The question this note tracks: when someone makes a **restricted** gift to an institution (a scholarship fund, a named building, a collection held "for public display") and the institution later merges, changes its mission, or **ceases to exist**, what happens to the gift? Does it follow the institution's assets to a successor, get redirected by a court, or return to the donor's estate?

## The doctrines in play

- **Donor intent.** A restricted gift is governed by the donor's stated charitable purpose, captured in the gift instrument (deed of gift, pledge agreement, will, trust). The tighter and more specific the restriction, the more it constrains what the recipient — or a court — may later do.
- **Cy-près** (French, "as near as possible"). An equitable doctrine for charitable gifts. When the original purpose becomes **impossible, impracticable, or illegal**, a court may redirect the gift to a purpose *as near as possible* to the donor's original intent, rather than let the gift fail. Cy-près keeps the money charitable; it does **not** default to returning the money to the donor.
- **Reversion / gift-over.** The cleanest donor protection. If the instrument says the gift **returns to the donor (or the donor's estate/foundation)** should the purpose fail or the institution dissolve, that reverter can defeat cy-près, because the donor specified what happens on failure. Absent such a clause, courts lean toward cy-près over reversion.
- **Standing.** Historically, once a charitable gift is complete, the **state attorney general** — not the donor — enforces the charitable purpose, and donors (and heirs) often **lack standing** to sue. Modern statutes and gift agreements increasingly reserve **donor enforcement rights**; a reverter or an express standing clause is how a donor keeps a seat at the table.
- **UPMIFA** (Uniform Prudent Management of Institutional Funds Act). Governs how an institution manages, spends, and **modifies or releases restrictions** on institutional endowment funds. It sets the process (donor consent where available; otherwise court approval with AG notice) for changing a restriction that has become impracticable.

## Live anchor case — Hamilton Family Charitable Trust v. the University of the Arts endowment

A textbook instance of the "what is the gift *for* the moment the institution stops existing" question.

- **The institution.** The University of the Arts (Philadelphia) **closed abruptly in 2024**, leaving a roughly **$77M endowment** and no school.
- **The donor.** **Dorrance "Dodo" Hill Hamilton** (Campbell Soup heiress, longtime UArts board member and its primary benefactor; **died 2017**). Gifts from the **Hamilton Family Charitable Trust** make up **more than half** of the endowment; the Trust puts its share at about **$37M** (it funded Hamilton Hall, among other things).
- **The fight.** UArts petitioned Philadelphia's **Orphans' Court** for a **cy-près** ruling to distribute the endowment among a group of universities (roughly a dozen), scholarships to former UArts students first, then broadly to arts education; the court ruled favorably on that petition in **September 2024**. **Temple University** was a leading candidate to absorb UArts/its funds. The **Hamilton Trust intervened and countersued to claw its ~$37M back**, arguing Hamilton never intended her money to flow to a **taxpayer-funded public university**, and that redirecting it there defeats her intent rather than approximating it. As of 2026 the matter remains **unresolved** (headed toward trial or settlement).
- **Why it matters here.** The case turns on exactly the levers above: whether Hamilton's gifts carried any **reverter** or purpose language narrow enough to defeat cy-près, whether the **Trust has standing** to reclaim a completed gift, and whether "as near as possible" can include a public university the donor arguably would have rejected. It is the sharpest current illustration of why the **gift instrument's failure/dissolution clause** is the whole ballgame.

## The Nariway relevance

For an estate with a **significant art collection** promised or given to an institution, the same failure-mode applies: if the museum deaccessions, merges, or closes, what happens to a collection given "for public display" depends entirely on the **instrument** — a specific-purpose restriction, a reverter to the estate or a family foundation, a gift-over to a named alternative institution, or nothing at all (in which case cy-près and the AG decide). This is precisely the kind of downstream question an estate attorney structuring an art gift should price in **at the drafting stage**, and a concrete reason donor-intent instruments belong in the conversation before a collection moves. See also the deaccession and donor-intent cases surfaced through the Signals **Law & Estates** beat (e.g. the Brauer/Valparaiso and Fisk/Stieglitz disputes already in the collections corpus).
