# The personal representative's authority and duties over estate art

*Trust & estate learning note. Follows the [[curriculum]] discipline: separate the law from common practice from Nariway's role, cite authorities, flag what needs a lawyer. Not legal advice.*

**In one line:** Once appointed, an executor or administrator can usually take custody of and sell a decedent's art without going back to court, but only within strict fiduciary duties, and not if a particular piece was left to a named person.

## Why it matters to a Nariway matter
When a collection enters an estate, the first two questions are who controls it and what that person is allowed to do with it. The personal representative (PR), an executor named in the will or an administrator appointed by the court, holds that authority. Nariway works alongside the PR, so the shape and the limits of that authority are the ground floor of every matter.

## The law
- **Authority to possess and sell (general / UPC).** Under the Uniform Probate Code, a PR has "the same power over title that an absolute owner would have," held in trust for those interested, and it "may be exercised without notice, hearing, or order of court" (UPC §3-711), including the power to "dispose of an asset, including personal property, at public or private sale" (UPC §3-715), plus a right to possession of the property (UPC §3-709). Caveats: the UPC is a model that many states modified or never adopted; **supervised administrations and many non-UPC states require court approval to sell**; and a will can restrict the power.
- **New York (does not follow the UPC).** A fiduciary's powers come from **EPTL §11-1.1**, which authorizes selling estate property "at public or private sale, on such terms as in the opinion of the fiduciary will be most advantageous." New York estates run through **Surrogate's Court** under the **SCPA**, and where a will withholds a needed power the fiduciary can seek the Surrogate's approval. Note: EPTL 11-1.1(b)(5) **expressly excludes property specifically disposed of by the will** from the automatic power to take and sell (see below).
- **New Jersey (modified UPC).** **N.J.S.A. 3B:14-23** gives a fiduciary, unless the will or a court provides otherwise, the power to "dispose of an asset, including personal property, at public or private sale" without court approval, vesting on appointment.
- **The duties that constrain all of this.** The power is exercised subject to fiduciary duties. **Loyalty / no self-dealing:** the PR cannot buy the art personally, or sell to an insider, without beneficiary consent or court approval, and the UPC makes conflicted transactions voidable (§3-713). **Care / prudence:** administer diligently, safeguard the art, get fair value, judged by the prudent-person standard on process not hindsight (§3-703). **Impartiality:** treat beneficiaries evenly.
- **The specific-bequest limit, and ademption.** If the will leaves a particular, identified artwork to a named beneficiary, the PR generally **cannot sell it**; it is earmarked for that person, reachable only if other assets cannot cover debts and taxes, and even then last in the order of abatement. Relatedly, **ademption**: if a specifically bequeathed piece is no longer in the estate at death, the gift usually fails. These rules vary by state.
- **Valuation and appraisal duties.** The PR must inventory assets at date-of-death fair market value (UPC §3-706) and may hire a qualified appraiser (§3-707). For a **taxable / filing estate**, art is reported on **Form 706, Schedule F**, and **Treas. Reg. §20.2031-6(b)** requires that art totaling **more than $3,000** be supported by an **expert appraisal under oath** filed with the return, with the executor's statement on the appraiser's competence and disinterestedness. On audit, the IRS **must refer** any single artwork claimed at **$50,000 or more** to Art Appraisal Services (IRM 4.48.2.3), which **generally** takes items above **~$150,000** to the **Art Advisory Panel**. Keep those three figures distinct. Note too that the defined term "qualified appraisal" (IRC §170) technically governs charitable deductions, while the estate-tax requirement is the Reg. 20.2031-6 "expert appraisal under oath."

## Common practice
To meet those duties and build a defensible record, a careful PR and counsel usually get an independent, USPAP-compliant appraisal before selling, confirm the piece is not specifically bequeathed or otherwise restricted, compare sale channels (auction, private sale, dealer) on net proceeds, timing, and confidentiality, avoid any self-interest and seek consent or court approval if an insider wants to buy, keep beneficiaries informed and treat them evenly, and document the appraisal, the offers, the rationale, and the terms. None of this is a statutory checklist. It is what prudence looks like.

## Nariway's role, and the boundary
This is the moment Nariway exists for. Nariway helps the PR **organize** the collection and its records, **assemble and compare** competing proposals on a common basis, **track** open items and deadlines, and **document** and **reconcile** what was decided and what happened. Nariway does **not** advise on the PR's legal authority, does not opine on value, and does not make the fiduciary's decision. Whether a sale needs court approval, whether a bequest is specific, whether a transaction risks self-dealing, all go to counsel. Nariway's independence, paid only by the estate with no transaction commissions, is what lets it stand cleanly beside the fiduciary here.

## Confirm with counsel / open questions
- Whether the governing state, or a supervised administration, requires court approval to sell (the UPC "no court order" default is not universal).
- Whether a given gift is "specific" (and so unsellable) versus general or residuary, plus the state's ademption and abatement rules.
- The exact enacted statute numbers in the governing state, since states renumber the UPC.
- Self-dealing remedies vary, from a strict "no further inquiry" rule to a fairness defense.
