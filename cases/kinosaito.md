# KinoSaito

One-line: painter and avant-garde theater designer Kikuo Saito bought a derelict former Catholic school in the Hudson Valley with his wife Mikiko Ino in 2013 as a future studio; after his 2016 death, and per his own wishes, Ino converted it into a public interdisciplinary art center that opened in 2021 — a small permanent collection of his own work rotating alongside changing exhibitions and residencies for living artists, run entirely by the founder's widow.

*(Public projection fields are in the frontmatter above, per [[case-template]]. The prose below is the internal research record.)*

**Why coded now:** the third of the three artist-foundation-adjacent leads named in [[candidate-universe]]'s "Artist-endowed foundation candidates" list. Distinct from every other case in that cluster (Graves, Resnick/Passlof, Frankenthaler, Rothko, Lichtenstein, Artists' Legacy): not a grant-making foundation at all, but a physical public building with a rotating permanent collection, adaptively reused from a closed institution (a 1891-1991 parish school), and run day-to-day by the founder's surviving spouse rather than a professional board.

## Narrative (sourced)

Kikuo Saito, a Japanese-American abstract painter and longtime scenic/costume designer for avant-garde theater (including work with Robert Wilson), lived and worked in SoHo before he and his wife Mikiko Ino began looking outside the city for more studio space [secondary: [kinosaito.org/general-info](https://www.kinosaito.org/general-info)]. In 2013 they bought the former St. Patrick's Catholic School in Verplanck, NY — a building that had served the parish from 1891 until it closed in 1991 — intending it purely as Saito's private studio [secondary: same]. Saito died in 2016, three years after the purchase and before any public conversion had begun.

What happened next is the case's central fact: rather than sell the property or leave it as a private estate, Ino — "in accordance with his wishes," per the foundation's own account — reconceived the school as a public, multidisciplinary art center, renovating it into two galleries, a multipurpose performance space, resident-artist studios, a classroom, a library, and a garden. KinoSaito opened to the public in 2021 [secondary: [BroadwayWorld](https://www.broadwayworld.com/rockland/article/KinoSaito-Art-Center-Opens-in-Lower-Hudson-Valley-20210720)]. Its programming deliberately pairs the founder's own legacy with living artists' work: Gallery 1 shows a quarterly-rotating selection from the "Kikuo Saito Permanent Collection" of his paintings, works on paper, and theater designs, while the center's other spaces host changing exhibitions and a resident-artist program for artists working today [secondary: [kinosaito.org](https://www.kinosaito.org/)]. Beyond KinoSaito itself, Saito's work sits in the permanent collections of MoMA, the Whitney, the Nasher Art Museum, the Aldrich Contemporary Art Museum, and the Edmonton Art Gallery — an independent marker of art-historical standing that helps explain why a dedicated public center for a single artist's legacy was viable at all [secondary: search synthesis, kikuosaito.com].

Financially, KinoSaito is small and thinly capitalized: 2024 revenue of $430K against expenses of $417K left only a modest surplus, and total assets of $173K represent well under half a year's operating budget — a genuine funding-gap signal, though not yet a crisis, for an institution only five years into public operation. Ino, Treasurer Sarah Strauss, and Secretary Joshua Cohen are all reported as uncompensated, consistent with a founder-family-and-close-associates governance structure still finding its financial footing rather than one yet tested by any succession event of its own.

This case sits closest, within this dataset, to the founder-spouse-driven adaptive-reuse pattern (compare [[kreeger-museum]], a widow-led house-museum conversion) but with two distinguishing features: the building had a prior institutional life of its own (a century as a parish school, not built as a private residence), and the mission was designed from reconception to serve living artists alongside the founder's own legacy, rather than being a pure single-artist shrine.
