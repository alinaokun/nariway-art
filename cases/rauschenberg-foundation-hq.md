## Narrative
Where [[rubin-museum]] closed a public-facing museum from strength and [[cam-raleigh]] is selling its building from acknowledged weakness, the Rauschenberg Foundation is neither — it never operated as a public museum in the first place, and its balance sheet ($515M+ net assets, ~32x its own annual expenses) shows no financial strain of any kind. What it is divesting is *personal real estate the artist happened to own*: his home and studio in Manhattan, and a 22-acre residential/studio compound on Captiva Island, Florida, both accumulated during his lifetime rather than acquired for public exhibition. The Captiva sale ($45M to a resort operator) and the still-unpriced Lafayette Street listing read as portfolio management by a well-run grantmaking foundation, not an institutional retreat — the clearest test yet in the sample of the question [[rubin-museum]]'s file poses explicitly: is the building or the collection/mission the thing that "was the legacy"? Here the answer is unambiguous: the Foundation's actual work (artist grants, archives, licensing) continues undisturbed; only real estate that had become a costly, non-mission-critical asset is being sold.

This sharpens rather than duplicates the "building vs. collection" comparison set: CAM Raleigh (distressed, no endowment, no founder), Rubin (strategic, well-capitalized, but the building *was* the entire public-facing institution), and Rauschenberg (strategic, extremely well-capitalized, and the building was never the institution's public program at all — a fourth, cleanest-yet position on the same spectrum). Also worth flagging for the schema: `primary_friction`'s controlled vocabulary has no value for "voluntary real-estate optimization from a position of financial strength" — the closest fits (`none-documented`, `funding-gap`) both misdescribe it.

## The 2011-2016 trustee litigation (added 2026-09-11, sixtieth run — this run's Part D disconfirmation instrument)
This case's file previously covered only the 2026 real-estate divestment. A dedicated search this run for a genuine internal-governance stress test on an artist-endowed foundation surfaced a far more serious episode in the Foundation's own history, unrelated to the real-estate story above but belonging on the same case record. In 2011, three of the Trustees of Robert Rauschenberg's Revocable Trust — Bennet Grutman (his accountant), Darryl Pottorf (his companion and the will's executor), and Bill Goldston (his longtime publishing partner) — sued the newly formed Robert Rauschenberg Foundation for what they argued was fair compensation for their work administering the artist's estate after his 2008 death, seeking as much as **$60 million**. Christopher Rauschenberg, the artist's son and the Foundation's board chairman, contested the claim directly, arguing reasonable compensation was closer to $375,000 split among the three. A Florida circuit judge sided substantially with the trustees, awarding **$24.6 million** (later reported as ~$25 million) to be split evenly among them; the Foundation's board, over Christopher Rauschenberg's own stated reservations about further litigation, voted to appeal. Florida's Second District Court of Appeals unanimously upheld the award in January 2016, five years after the suit began [secondary — Artnet News, Center for Art Law, Gulfshore Life].

This is a genuine stress test of a different kind than the H8 founder-constraint cluster tracks: not a will's substantive terms being challenged, but a **direct, adversarial, multi-year lawsuit between the foundation's own founding insiders** (the artist's own hand-picked estate trustees, on one side, against the artist's own son and the board he chaired, on the other) over tens of millions of dollars — arguably a sharper internal-conflict test than [[helen-frankenthaler-foundation]]'s dismissed-for-standing dispute, since here the claim was litigated to a final, binding, eight-figure judgment against the Foundation, not dismissed on a threshold ground. We now have a clean before/after: the litigation ran 2011-2016; today, a decade after the appeal concluded, the Foundation holds $515M+ in net assets, ~32x its own annual expenses, and continues active, robust grantmaking (~$11.6M in disbursements, 71.5% of expenses, in its most recent filing found) — showing no lasting financial or programmatic damage from a $24.6M forced payout to its own founding trustees. **This complicates a reading this evidence base has not yet stated outright but has come close to implying through the Lichtenstein and Frankenthaler entries: that severe, adversarial internal conflict inside an artist-endowed foundation is itself a durability risk.** Rauschenberg shows that even a real, court-decided, eight-figure internal fight — not a dismissed nuisance claim — can resolve, pay out, and leave the institution's long-term financial and programmatic health apparently undamaged, provided the foundation's balance sheet is large enough to absorb the award and its post-litigation governance (Christopher Rauschenberg remained chairman throughout and after) stays intact.
