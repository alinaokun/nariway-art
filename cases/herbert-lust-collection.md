One-line: Herbert Lust, an "accidental collector" who assembled deep personal holdings of Giacometti, Calder, Indiana, and modern photography over roughly a century of living among the artists he collected, died in Greenwich, Connecticut in May 2026 at age 99; he had already given hundreds of works to the Hirshhorn Museum and the Tel Aviv Museum of Art during his lifetime, and his estate's remaining ~300 works (estimated ~$60 million) are now being actively courted by both Christie's and Sotheby's for a 2026 sale.

## Why in the sample

This is the sample's cleanest **hybrid disperse case with a documented two-stage split**: a deliberate, self-directed lifetime pattern of museum gifts (donate-existing-museum-scattered), followed immediately at death by a market-driven auction-house consignment fight (sell-auction) for whatever remained undonated. Unlike Ganz or de la Cruz (posthumous sale only) or Vogel/Souls Grown Deep (dispersal only, no sale), Lust ran both tracks himself, in sequence, over one long life — donating what he chose to place, while apparently keeping several hundred works uncommitted for his estate to dispose of. It is also this sample's most direct evidence for the "great estate rush" pattern named in [[market-intelligence]]: within roughly three months of his death, two of the world's two largest auction houses are reported in active, named competition for the consignment, with Sotheby's described as advantaged by "years cultivating Lust and his collection" — a market relationship, not a coordinated advisory one, filling the pre-decision vacuum H7A describes.

## Narrative (sourced)

Herbert Lust died in Greenwich, Connecticut on May 12, 2026, at age 99, described by ARTnews in his own obituary headline as "the accidental collector" — a former investment banker who gravitated toward artists he came to know personally, amassing deep holdings of Alberto Giacometti, Alexander Calder, and Robert Indiana alongside a substantial photography collection (Berenice Abbott, Henri Cartier-Bresson, Eugène Atget, and others) over roughly a century of collecting [source: ARTnews, 2026; confidence: secondary]. Well before his death, Lust had already placed significant parts of his holdings with museums: more than 200 photographs went to the Hirshhorn Museum and Sculpture Garden in Washington, and over 200 works by Giacometti went to the Tel Aviv Museum of Art [source: ARTnews; confidence: secondary]. This lifetime pattern — donating some of the collection's deepest single-artist holdings to two different institutions on two different continents, in two different mediums — is itself notable evidence against a simple "collector hoards, estate disperses" reading; Lust was actively curating his own legacy across multiple institutions decades before his death.

What remained at his death is now the subject of active competition between the art trade's two dominant auction houses: Christie's and Sotheby's are both reported pursuing the estate's roughly 300 remaining works, collectively estimated at approximately $60 million, with the top lot expected to be a major Giacometti sculpture [source: ARTnews, Robb Report, 2026; confidence: secondary]. ARTnews reports Sotheby's may hold an advantage from "years cultivating Lust and his collection" — language that reads, in this dataset's terms, as exactly the kind of standing commercial relationship H7A's disconfirmation research found filling the pre-decision vacuum: not a credentialed independent advisor, but the auction house itself, positioned by a long prior relationship to become the de facto decision-shaping party once a collector dies without one named.
