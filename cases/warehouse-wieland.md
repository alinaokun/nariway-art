# The Warehouse (Wieland Collection)

One-line: Atlanta homebuilder John Wieland (founder of John Wieland Homes and Neighborhoods) and his wife Sue spent four decades assembling a 400-plus-work contemporary art collection, converted a 37,000 sq ft furniture warehouse to house it in 2010, and opened it to the public as a free contemporary art space, The Warehouse, in 2024 after a second renovation.

**Why in the sample:** a living-founder standalone-museum case built by a real-estate/homebuilding fortune rather than a finance or tech one, and a genuinely slow-burn version of the pathway — a private storage-and-display building held privately for 14 years (2010-2024) before opening to the public, distinct from cases that opened to the public immediately at founding. Also a small-foundation disclosure case: the Warehouse Wieland Art Foundation's own reported net assets ($200,000) are far too small to represent the collection's actual market value, the same "the collection is not on the foundation's balance sheet" gap already flagged for [[kinsey]], [[thoma]], and [[gochman-family-collection]].

## Narrative
John Wieland, founder and chairman of John Wieland Homes and Neighborhoods, and his wife Sue began collecting contemporary art together in the 1980s. In 2010 they bought a 37,000 sq ft former furniture warehouse in Atlanta and converted it into private galleries, storage, and offices to bring the growing collection — which now spans painting, photography, works on paper, sculpture, installation, ceramics, textile, and video/film by more than 250 national and international artists — under one roof. The building functioned as a private, invitation-only space for over a decade before a 2023 expansion and renovation prepared it for public access; The Warehouse opened to the public in 2024. The collection itself is held by the Susan Wieland Trust and described as a promised gift to the Warehouse Wieland Art Foundation, a small family-trusteed 501(c)(3) (EIN 86-3496129) whose own reported financials ($200,000 total assets, under $2,000 in annual revenue/expenses) are far too small to reflect the collection's actual value — evidence the Foundation functions as a governance/promised-gift vehicle rather than the collection's own balance sheet, the same pattern this sample has already flagged for touring and loan-based collections where the art and the filing entity are legally distinct.
