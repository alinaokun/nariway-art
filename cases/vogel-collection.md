## Narrative
Herbert Vogel worked as a New York postal clerk; Dorothy Vogel worked as a reference librarian at the Brooklyn Public Library. From the early 1960s they built one of the most significant collections of minimalist and conceptual American art anywhere — reported between 4,000 and 5,000 works over roughly five decades — funded entirely out of Herbert's salary while the couple lived on Dorothy's. No family fortune, no advisor, no foundation, no endowment: the collecting itself was the entire capital structure, an extreme data point against every other case in the sample, nearly all of which start from significant personal wealth.

Facing the practical problem of an apartment that could no longer hold what they'd bought, the Vogels first partnered with the National Gallery of Art in 1992, placing roughly 1,100 works there as gifts and promised gifts. As the collection kept growing, they and the National Gallery designed something structurally distinct from every other disposition in the sample: rather than concentrate the remaining collection in one museum, or found their own, they distributed exactly 50 works to one selected institution in every US state, executed in 2008–09 with National Endowment for the Arts and Institute of Museum and Library Services support. By the time Herbert died in 2012, the defining act of disposition was already three years complete; Dorothy lived another 13 years as the collection's public face, dying in November 2025.

This is the sample's cleanest test of the "distinctiveness over scale" and "eponymous museum losing share" framings from a different angle than the living-founder cohort (Broad, Kinsey, Cisneros): the Vogels never had the wealth to build a standalone institution even if they'd wanted one, and their solution — total intentional dispersal into 51 pre-existing institutions, timed to finish before either founder's death — reads as the most durable disposition mechanism in the entire dataset by construction, even though it produces almost nothing codeable on the endowment/net-assets axis. It is a strong argument that the report's "governance beats endowment" finding generalizes even to the case with the smallest endowment (zero) in the sample.
