## Narrative
John G. Johnson was, by his own era's account, the nation's leading corporate lawyer — his Philadelphia practice grew alongside the Gilded Age's industrial titans, and he spent much of his considerable income during summer trips to Europe buying paintings, mostly Early Netherlandish, Italian Renaissance, and Dutch/Flemish Baroque work, guided by his own connoisseurship rather than fashionable taste. He died in 1917 having assembled roughly 1,300 paintings, and his will left the entire collection to the City of Philadelphia on the explicit condition that it remain in, and be shown from, his own house at 510 South Broad Street — a founder-designed house-museum constraint of exactly the kind [[case-template]]'s H8 cluster tracks.

The constraint was tested almost immediately. The house's storage did not meet the city's own fire-prevention code, delaying any public opening for six years; when the house museum finally opened in November 1923, only about 275 of the collection's works could be safely displayed on its first two floors, with the rest crowded into upper floors or rented storage elsewhere. As early as 1919, and formally by 1921, a petition went before the Philadelphia Orphans' Court asking to relocate the collection and sell the Broad Street property outright, arguing that the renovation the house needed to display the collection safely would cost more than the city could afford. The court **dismissed the petition** — a direct, documented instance of a "keep it in the house" constraint surviving a legal challenge, the same enforceability question this evidence base has tracked at Gardner, Soane, and the Wallace Collection. The city instead proceeded with whatever renovation its limited budget allowed, and the house-museum form continued.

What makes this case a genuinely distinct addition to the H8 cluster is what happened next. By 1933, the Depression had made the deteriorating property an unsustainable burden, and rather than a second round of litigation, "an informal proposition to display the collection at the Philadelphia Museum of Art was...agreed upon by the City and Trustee of the collection" — no court order, no reported objection, simply a negotiated administrative agreement between the two parties bound by the will. The collection moved into PMA's own building, where it remains today, still catalogued and exhibited as the named "John G. Johnson Collection," now marking over a century of continuous, if institutionally discontinuous, public display. This is a fourth distinct H8 constraint-erosion mechanism alongside Barnes's court override, Star of Hope's quiet non-enforcement, and the Wallace Collection's reinterpretation-of-ambiguous-language: a **will-based constraint that survived a direct legal challenge, then was set aside decades later by ordinary administrative agreement between the bound parties themselves, once practical and financial conditions made the original form genuinely unworkable** — no court needed to force the outcome a second time, because no party with standing to enforce the original condition objected.

The case also closes a live research-program thread: H9's note on professional (law-firm, corporate) collecting, prompted by Ian Silverstein's Center for Art Law essay. Johnson is this dataset's first individual lawyer-collector whose personal, not firm, collection reached a museum — a reminder that "professional-services collecting" includes both the institutional collections firms build collectively (Allens, the modern law-firm collections named in this run's own ecosystem research) and a much older pattern of individual professionals personally amassing museum-grade holdings.
