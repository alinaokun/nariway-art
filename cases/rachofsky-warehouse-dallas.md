# The Rachofsky Collection / The Warehouse Dallas Art Foundation

One-line: A living Dallas collector couple made an irrevocable "whatever's left" bequest to an existing museum in 2005 — deliberately preserving their own freedom to keep collecting, editing, and selling for the rest of their lives — while running a separate, growing private exhibition nonprofit alongside it, a two-track model distinct from every other living-founder case in the sample.

**Why in the sample:** a genuinely different H8 data point — a founder deliberately choosing the *loosest* possible binding constraint (a bequest of "whatever remains," explicitly preserving lifetime editing rights) rather than the tight, permanent restrictions Barnes or the Stieglitz gift impose; a `university-partnership`-adjacent gift to an *existing* museum that stops short of founding anything new for the core collection, run in parallel with a genuinely founded (if informal-then-formalized) private foundation for a second, overlapping body of work; and a rare multi-family coordinated pledge (five families, one museum, 2005) that prefigures the Art Omi "shared-campus" taxonomy question on a smaller, simpler scale.

## Narrative (sourced)

In 2005, Cindy and Howard Rachofsky joined four other prominent Dallas collectors, Rusty and Deedie Rose and Robert and Marguerite Hoffman, in announcing an irrevocable bequest of their combined collections to the Dallas Museum of Art, at the time estimated at more than $300 million and reported to include the Rachofskys' own Richard Meier-designed house [secondary: [Artsy](https://www.artsy.net/article/artsy-editorial-collector-helped-build-dallass-texas-sized-art-scene); [Harvard Club Dallas](https://hcdallas.clubs.harvard.edu/article.html?aid=944)]. The mechanism was deliberately loose: rather than freezing the collection at the moment of the pledge, the bequest transfers "whatever is in the collection at the time of the second to pass away" between Cindy and Howard, explicitly leaving them free to continue buying, selling, and refining the collection for the rest of their lives [secondary: papercitymag.com interview, cited in search synthesis]. This coordinated, five-family, single-recipient-museum structure is a distinct shape from every other multi-party pledge in the sample; it is not a shared-campus model like the taxonomy-flagged Art Omi Pavilions (each collector there controls a separate structure), but five independent collections converging on one existing institution through one negotiated agreement.

Separately, in 2012 Howard Rachofsky and fellow collector Vernon Faulconer opened The Warehouse, an adapted 18,000 sq ft industrial building in Dallas holding art storage, a library, and exhibition space, showing works from their own collections alongside pieces acquired jointly with, and loaned by, the Dallas Museum of Art [secondary: [Artnet News](https://news.artnet.com/market/rachofsky-house-dallas-for-sale-2635863)]. In late 2024, Rachofsky and a newer Dallas collector, Thomas Hartland-Mackie, relaunched the effort as a formal joint nonprofit, The Warehouse Dallas Art Foundation, continuing free, appointment-based public exhibitions at the same Inwood Road address [secondary: [ARTnews](https://www.artnews.com/art-news/news/collectors-howard-rachofsky-and-thomas-hartland-mackie-launch-the-warehouse-dallas-art-foundation-1234722381/); [Glasstire](https://glasstire.com/2024/11/07/collectors-howard-rachofsky-and-thomas-hartland-mackie-establish-the-warehouse-dallas-art-foundation/)].

**A live open contradiction worth flagging, not resolving prematurely.** An October 2025 Dallas Morning News report describes the Rachofskys' Richard Meier-designed home going on the open market for $23 million [secondary: [Dallas Morning News](https://www.dallasnews.com/business/real-estate/2025/10/31/iconic-rachofsky-house-to-hit-open-market-for-23m-2/)], which appears in tension with 2005 reporting that the house itself was part of the promised DMA gift. This run's searches could not confirm whether the house was formally removed from the bequest terms, was always intended for eventual private sale separate from the art pledge, or whether the DMA gift's "whatever remains" mechanism was always understood to exclude real property. Coded as an open gap rather than resolved either way.
