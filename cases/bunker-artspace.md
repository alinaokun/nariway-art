# The Bunker Artspace (Beth Rudin DeWoody Collection)

One-line: New York real-estate heiress and collector Beth Rudin DeWoody converted a 1920s Art Deco former toy factory/WWII armory in West Palm Beach into a private, invitation-only exhibition space (opened December 2017) for roughly 500 works at a time from a personal holding reported at 10,000+ works — with no nonprofit or foundation wrapper located, a genuinely institution-free public-access model.

*(Public projection fields are in the frontmatter above, per [[case-template]]. The prose below is the internal research record.)*

**Why in the sample:** the sample's clearest US instance of a private collection given public-facing life with **no institutional entity at all** — not a foundation (unlike [[sammlung-boros]] or [[feuerle-collection]], the sample's Berlin appointment-only comparators, both of which carry a legal foundation/nonprofit wrapper), not a hospitality business (unlike [[casabianca]]), simply a personal space DeWoody runs herself, opened by choice specifically as private/by-invitation from the outset rather than pursuing museum status. Tests H3 (a standalone-museum alternative that deliberately avoids the nonprofit-museum apparatus) and the H4 anti-blue-chip/overlooked-artist thesis directly, since DeWoody's stated curatorial premise favors emerging and underrepresented artists over market-blue-chip names.

## Narrative (sourced)

Beth Rudin DeWoody, an heir to the Rudin family's roughly $5 billion New York real-estate fortune (Rudin Management, 15M+ sq ft of Manhattan commercial and residential property) [secondary: [Cine Net Worth](https://www.cinenetworth.com/rudin-family-net-worth/); [Forbes profile](https://www.forbes.com/profile/rudin/)], has built a personal art collection reported at more than 10,000 works spanning the 20th and 21st centuries. In December 2017 she opened the Bunker Artspace in West Palm Beach inside a 1920s Art Deco building that had served first as a toy factory, then as a World War II-era armory — two floors of gallery space, an art-book library, and storage, showing roughly 500 works at a time in rotating, season-by-season presentations drawn "exclusively from the BRD Collection" [secondary: [Galerie Magazine](https://galeriemagazine.com/inside-beth-rudin-dewoodys-art-filled-bunker-in-south-florida/)]. The venue is explicitly **not zoned as a museum** and shows work "by invitation" and scheduled private tour rather than open public admission [secondary: multiple sources]. No nonprofit registration, 501(c)(3) status, or EIN was located in this run's search — a genuinely different disclosure shape from this dataset's other appointment-only private collections, [[sammlung-boros]] and [[feuerle-collection]], both of which carry documented (if thin) foundation wrappers in Germany. DeWoody chairs the broader Rudin Family Foundations (the family's general philanthropic vehicle) and sits on the boards of the Whitney Museum, the Hammer Museum, and others, but no source connects a specific foundation entity to the Bunker itself [secondary: [Palm Beach Illustrated](https://www.palmbeachillustrated.com/beth-rudin-dewoody-on-her-state-of-the-art-space/)]. Programming continues actively into 2026: a December 2025 queer-themed exhibition and February 2026 coverage both describe an ongoing, evolving curatorial program, often co-curated with her daughter Kyle DeWoody [secondary: [Cultured Magazine, Dec 2025](https://www.culturedmag.com/article/2025/12/04/art-bunker-artspace-queer-exhibition/); [Rhonda's Escape, Feb 2026](http://www.rhondasescape.com/2026/02/the-bunker-artspace.html)]. As a living-founder, `founder-sole`, no-financial-disclosure case, this is not yet testable for survival, but it is a clean data point on H3: a wealthy collector with every resource to found a formal museum instead deliberately chose the lightest-weight model available — a private space with no public zoning, no board, and no filed disclosure — suggesting "founds a standalone museum" and "gives a collection genuine, if narrow, public life" are not the same choice at all, even for someone who could easily afford either.
