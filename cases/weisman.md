## Narrative
Frederick Rand Weisman (1912–1994) made his fortune in the produce and automobile-distribution businesses — he ran Val Vita/Hunt Foods and later built Mid-Atlantic Toyota into one of the first and largest Toyota distributorships in the United States — before art collecting became the pursuit that made him internationally known. He and his first wife, Marcia Simon Weisman (sister of collector Norton Simon), began collecting in the late 1940s, assembling a major postwar American and European collection; when they divorced in 1979 they divided the collection between them and each went on to separate philanthropic paths — Marcia toward LACMA, the National Gallery, and as a founding figure of MOCA, Fred toward building an institution bearing his own name. In 1982 he purchased a 1920s Gordon B. Kaufmann-designed Mediterranean Revival villa on Carolwood Drive in the Holmby Hills section of Los Angeles as a showcase for his collection, and with his second wife, art conservator and curator Billie Milam (a former Getty Museum conservator), continued to build the collection through the 1980s and early 1990s, adding a contemporary annex designed by Franklin D. Israel in 1991 to house larger-scale works. Weisman also gave his name — and money — to two university art museums that are legally and operationally separate from the Los Angeles foundation: the Frederick R. Weisman Art Museum at the University of Minnesota (a Frank Gehry-designed building realized with a pledge reported at $3 million, opened 1993) and the Frederick R. Weisman Museum of Art at Pepperdine University (built around a $1.5 million 1992 gift and long-term loans from his collection). Neither of those university museums is governed by, or reports its finances through, the Frederick R. Weisman Art Foundation covered here.

Weisman died on September 11, 1994, and control of the Los Angeles foundation passed to Billie Milam Weisman, who has served as its President and Director for more than three decades since — one of the longest single-successor tenures in this report's sample. Under her direction the Foundation has kept the collection displayed largely as a domestic "living with art" environment rather than converting the house into a conventional white-cube museum, opens it to the public only through docent-led tours by appointment, and runs an active program lending works to museums worldwide alongside continued publishing and exhibition activity. On paper the Foundation is financially strong: 990-PF data attributed to EIN 95-4442307 shows net assets over $105 million against annual operating expenses of roughly $3.2 million — a large cushion, though the figure was sourced via third-party aggregators rather than a direct read of the filing in this research pass (see caveat above), and it is not established whether that net-assets figure includes the carrying value of the art collection itself or reflects investment/cash assets held separately from it.

The central governance question this report cares about — whether Billie Milam Weisman's succession was locked in by a legal instrument before Weisman's death, or arrived informally through her position as his widow and long-time collaborator — could not be resolved from public web sources in this pass. No will, trust document, or Foundation bylaws text was located. A separate, much smaller Weisman vehicle, the Frederick R. Weisman Philanthropic Foundation (EIN 95-4442308, net assets reported around $1.5 million), also run by Billie Milam Weisman, exists for broader arts/education/social-service grantmaking and should not be confused with the art-collection foundation profiled here; the two are disambiguated by EIN in this file to avoid the kind of entity-conflation risk flagged in the Thoma case.
