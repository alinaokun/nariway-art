## Narrative
Inhotim gives this sample its first South American case, and a governance pattern this dataset has now seen twice, with an instructive difference between the two instances. Like [[dia-art-foundation]] (founder-sole from 1974, crisis in 1985, resolved by a forced board handoff while the controlling founder — Heiner Friedrich — was still living, not at his death), Inhotim was founder-sole from its 2004 founding and hit a governance-threatening crisis while Paz was still alive: a 2016-17 attempt to settle his separate mining company's tax debt using 20 of the art park's own site-specific works, an arrangement a Minas Gerais tax court declared "illegal and null and void" in October 2020. Where the two cases diverge is the *mechanism* of resolution. Dia's 1985 handoff reads as an externally forced correction, a board imposed on a founder who had lost the confidence of his own institution's stakeholders. Inhotim's 2022 resolution reads, in every source located this run, as **Paz's own voluntary choice**: rather than a court or a board stripping him of control, he donated his entire remaining personal collection, all galleries, and the land itself to the independent nonprofit institute, restructuring its governance into a 20-to-30-member, multi-stakeholder Deliberative Council with outside business figures (Eugênio Mattar of Localiza) joining as vice-president, and installing the institute's first outside, non-founder CEO (Paula Azevedo) in January 2024. Both cases end in the same place this sample's Pattern 14 predicts, independent governance surviving a founder-sole crisis without the institution collapsing, but Inhotim tests whether that governance conversion holds up when it is chosen rather than imposed, and while distress (the tax dispute, and the founder's own later 2024 sale of his mining company) continued in the background even after the art institute's own governance was formally settled.

This case also surfaces a genuine research uncertainty this sample's discipline requires naming rather than smoothing over: the vault's own working notes, going into this run, described Inhotim as a case of the founder having "lost control" through legal/financial distress, implying something closer to a forced seizure or bankruptcy-driven loss of the institute itself. This run's research found no evidence of a personal bankruptcy or judicial-recovery (*recuperação judicial*) filing by Paz specifically, in the Brazilian press sources searched. What the sources do document is the 2020 court ruling voiding the art-for-tax-debt settlement (protecting the institute's collection from his corporate debts, not seizing it), followed two years later by his own voluntary total donation, and, separately, the 2024 arm's-length sale of Itaminas (the mining company, not the art institute) to outside buyers. The prior framing may have conflated three genuinely distinct events into one "loss of control" narrative; this run's finding is that the actual mechanism looks more like Dia's than like Terra's or de la Cruz's forced-liquidation pattern, a correction worth carrying forward rather than repeating uncritically.

Finally, Paz's post-2022 second act sharpens the case further. Rather than retiring from collecting, he has, per 2025-2026 Brazilian and trade press, been building a new, personally-owned museum directly adjacent to Inhotim's own preservation land, explicitly modeled on the Inhotim template, with a reported ~R$1 billion invested, ~3,000 works reassembled since 2022, and a first-phase opening planned for September 2027. Read together with the main case, Inhotim becomes a rare instance in this sample of a founder relinquishing institutional control over one collection while immediately rebuilding a second, personally-controlled one from scratch, a live test of whether "permanence requires surrendering control" (H8) describes a founder's *institution* or a founder's *temperament*, since Paz appears to have accepted the former without abandoning the latter.
