## Narrative

Robert Fleming & Co., a Dundee-founded Scottish merchant bank, began buying Scottish art in 1968 to decorate its new London offices, with purchasing decisions given to a single bank director, David Donald, under one simple brief: Scottish artists, or Scottish subjects by any artist. The collection grew for over three decades as an ordinary, if unusually coherent, corporate asset. When the bank itself was sold to Chase Manhattan (later JPMorgan) in 2000, the founding Fleming family was unwilling to let the collection pass to the acquirer as an incidental corporate asset — so the family bought the entire collection back at market value and vested it in a newly created independent charity, the Fleming-Wyfold Art Foundation, whose stated mission is to promote understanding of Scottish art and creativity outside Scotland.

The Foundation opened a dedicated public gallery on Berkeley Square in central London in 2002, and ran it for roughly thirteen years. By 2015 the gallery's endowment had run down, and the Foundation closed the building rather than continue operating at a deficit. Under a new director, James Knox, it reinvented itself as a "Museum without Walls": rather than maintaining a fixed venue, the Foundation now places its 600+-work collection on tour — exhibitions such as *Scottish Colourists* and *Scottish Women Artists* travel to partner venues, and individual works go on long-term loan to public institutions, a model that keeps the collection publicly visible without the fixed cost of a standing gallery.

This case sits at an unusual intersection of two of this dataset's recurring mechanisms. It is a corporate-collection rescue at the moment of a parent's sale — a distinct fourth shape alongside this dataset's other M&A-triggered corporate outcomes (bankruptcy liquidation at [[lehman-brothers-collection]] and [[enron-art-collection]]; absorption into an acquirer at [[credit-suisse-ubs-collection]]; a government buyout at [[westlb-portigon-collection]]) — where the founding family itself intervened to keep the collection independent rather than let a change of corporate ownership dictate its fate. And it is also a "building vs. collection" case: the family's rescue succeeded completely at preserving the *collection* (it has never been dispersed, and has kept growing since 2000), but even a maximally motivated, family-funded, mission-driven foundation could not sustain a *public gallery* past its own endowment's natural life.
