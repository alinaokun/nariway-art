# The Wallace Collection

One-line: Lady Wallace's 1897 bequest bound the nation to keep her family's collection "together" and unmixed forever, later given statutory force by the Museums and Galleries Act 1992; the no-acquisitions half of that restriction has held completely for 128 years, but the no-loans half — long read as part of the same prohibition — was formally reinterpreted and lifted in 2019, making this run's Part D disconfirmation instrument: a statute-backed constraint, the strongest enforcement type this evidence base has found (per [[soane-museum]]), that nonetheless partially loosened without repeal, court challenge, or crisis.

*(Public projection fields are in the frontmatter above, per [[case-template]] — the single machine source the export reads. The prose below is the internal research record.)*

**Why in the sample — this run's Part D disconfirmation instrument.** [[what-we-now-believe]]'s most recent H8 entries (Gardner, then Soane) built toward a refined claim: enforcement-mechanism *type*, not constraint scope, predicts durability, with statutory mandate (Soane, backed by a private Act of Parliament and 189 years unmodified) reading as the strongest of three tested types, ahead of forfeiture-to-a-beneficiary (Gardner) and court-enforceable private indenture (Barnes, the one type that failed). Soane's own entry named the explicit next test: a fourth enforcement-mechanism instance, ideally a jurisdiction test or a second statutory case, to check whether "statute is stronger" reflects the mechanism itself or merely Soane's own luck and elapsed time. The Wallace Collection is a second UK statutory-mandate case — and it complicates rather than confirms the "statute holds best" reading Soane alone suggested.

## Narrative (sourced)

Sir Richard Wallace inherited and greatly expanded a collection of French 18th-century paintings, furniture, porcelain, and arms and armour assembled across several generations by the Hertford family; on the death of his widow, Lady Julie-Amélie Wallace, in 1897, her will bequeathed the entire collection to the British nation on the explicit condition that it be "kept together" and "unmixed with other objects of art" [secondary: wallacecollection.org; UK Parliament Hansard, "Bequest To Nation By Lady Wallace," 1897-03-01]. A Treasury Minute of July 28, 1897 appointed the first Board of Trustees, acting on the authority of a Declaration of Trust dated July 27, 1899; the museum opened to the public at Hertford House — the Wallace family's own London residence — in 1900 [secondary: Wallace Collection Annual Report and Accounts]. The Wallace Collection has operated as an executive non-departmental public body ever since, funded through Government Grant-in-Aid via the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport, an unusually direct state-institutional governance shape compared with this dataset's more common independent-charity or founder-sole structures [secondary]. The Museums and Galleries Act 1992 placed Lady Wallace's "kept together, unmixed" condition on modern statutory footing, explicitly barring the museum from adding to its permanent collection — a restriction that has now held, unmodified, for 128 years [secondary: Museums and Galleries Act 1992; wallacecollection.org].

The same original condition was, for 122 years, also read as an absolute bar on lending works — no comparable statutory language specifically named loans, but institutional practice treated "kept together" as prohibiting temporary removal of any kind [secondary: The Art Newspaper, "Where there's a will there's a way: the Wallace Collection lifts loan restrictions"]. In 2019, the Wallace Collection secured a Section 105 order under the Charities Act 2011, formally reinterpreting Lady Wallace's 1897 language as not, in fact, a prohibition on lending — since the will's text does not specifically mention loans — and authorizing the museum to borrow and lend objects on the same basis as other national museums [secondary]. This reversed more than a century of practice without a court challenge, a crisis, or a legislative repeal: the mechanism was a charity-law reinterpretation process, initiated by the institution's own trustees rather than imposed on them.

We read this as a genuine complication of the H8 refinement Soane's entry proposed. Soane showed a private Act of Parliament holding completely for 189 years with no documented loosening of any kind. Wallace is a second UK statutory-mandate case, arguably an even stronger instrument (a public Treasury Minute and a general Act of Parliament, rather than Soane's own bespoke private Act) — yet one of its two component restrictions loosened after 122 years, through a formal but non-adversarial legal process most of this evidence base's other constraint cases have not shown (Barnes required a contested court battle; Star of Hope simply went unenforced). This suggests "statute" is not a single enforcement type with one durability profile: a statute's specific *wording* still matters enormously — the no-acquisitions restriction survived because the 1992 Act states it explicitly, while the no-loans reading survived only as an *inference* from "kept together" that a later, differently-motivated set of trustees successfully re-argued. The disconfirmation is real but narrow: it does not touch the "enforceability beats scope" core claim (both Wallace restrictions remain enforced by an active legal mechanism, unlike Star of Hope's toothless oversight), but it shows that even the strongest-tested enforcement type can loosen through *reinterpretation* of ambiguous language, a failure mode distinct from all three mechanisms this evidence base has coded before (court override, quiet non-enforcement, or — until now — no loosening at all).
