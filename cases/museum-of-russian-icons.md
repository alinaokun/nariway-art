# Museum of Russian Icons (now the Icon Museum and Study Center)

One-line: a plastics-manufacturing executive's icons bought on Russia business trips from 1989 became a Massachusetts museum in 2006, was AAM-accredited in 2016, survived his 2021 death under a standing board of trustees, and was renamed in 2023 to reflect an expanded scholarly mission — a clean, single-thesis, non-marquee survived-founder case.

## Narrative (sourced)

Gordon Lankton, longtime chairman and CEO of the injection-molding company Nypro, bought his first Russian icon at an open-air market in Moscow in 1989 while on a business trip, and kept collecting for the next fifteen-plus years until the holding — now widely described as the largest collection of Russian icons in North America and one of the largest outside Russia itself — became a public museum in his hometown of Clinton, Massachusetts, opening in October 2006 [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icon_Museum_and_Study_Center)]. The museum built out a scholarly arm, The Center for Icon Studies, in 2012, and earned accreditation from the American Alliance of Museums in 2016 — a credential that itself requires, among other things, a functioning independent governing board, which is the strongest available evidence (short of the founding bylaws, not located this run) that the institution was not run as founder-sole even during Lankton's lifetime [secondary]. Lankton died on 7 March 2021 at 89; the Board of Trustees announced his death and the museum continued operating without interruption [secondary: [BroadwayWorld](https://www.broadwayworld.com/boston/article/The-Board-Of-Trustees-Of-The-Museum-Of-Russian-Icons-Announces-The-Death-Of-Founder-Gordon-B-Lankton-20210308)]. In October 2023, the institution renamed itself the Icon Museum and Study Center, explicitly to reflect a broadened mission beyond Russian icons specifically to Eastern Christian sacred art more generally, growing out of a strategic-planning process [secondary: [WBJ](https://wbjournal.com/article/the-museum-of-russian-icons-rebrands-to-match-expanded-mission/)]. That rebrand is itself a mild `coherence_drifted` signal — the institution chose, five years after its founder's death, to widen a thesis he had made narrowly Russian — but the core collecting focus (icons) held.
