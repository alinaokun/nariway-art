## Narrative
Bank of America's *Art in our Communities* program is the sample's cleanest example of a corporate collection choosing public life entirely through other institutions' walls rather than its own — a structurally different bet from every other corporate case coded so far. UBS and JPMorgan Chase keep the art largely inside their own offices, with UBS adding one dedicated public gallery; Deutsche Bank built and is now closing a dedicated venue; this case never built one at all, treating "loan complete exhibitions to museums, at no cost" as the whole public-access strategy from the outset. That design choice sidesteps the exact real-estate cost pressure this sample's Pattern 5 (di Rosa, CAM Raleigh, Rubin, Rauschenberg, Deutsche Bank) keeps surfacing: there is no building to become a liability, because there never was one.

The program's own framing makes the mechanism explicit: a free, fully curated exhibition (with wall labels and educational materials supplied) lets a receiving museum redirect its own limited exhibition budget elsewhere, a genuine subsidy to the receiving institution rather than merely a display opportunity for the lender. This is a fifth distinct shape for the corporate sub-typology this sample already tracks (forced liquidation / voluntary retreat / unaffected continuity / distressed absorption / anticipatory monetization, per [[report-dataset]] Pattern 8, now extended once more): call it the **standing subsidy model**, corporate art deployed as an ongoing act of institutional support rather than owned, displayed, or divested.

The collection's own founding narrative shows the same self-history compression this sample has now documented in three separate corporate cases (UBS, Credit Suisse→UBS, and here): "50+ years" is a vague, marketing-friendly rounding that obscures a real, better-documented predecessor lineage (the National Exchange Bank of Chicago's 1967 photography-collecting mandate under Nancy Newhall), itself just one of many legacy institutions absorbed into the modern Bank of America through decades of mergers (NationsBank, BankAmerica, and others). The pattern first named for UBS/Credit Suisse — a bank's collection history compresses a genuinely layered, multi-merger past into one clean founding date — now holds across a third, independent corporate case.
