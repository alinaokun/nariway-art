## Narrative (sourced)

Scott Price, who had worked at Microsoft and at Bonvolo Real Estate Investments, bought a forested property east of Coupeville on Whidbey Island in 2008 intending to build a house there for his family [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_Sculpture_Forest)]. When the family ultimately built elsewhere, Price faced an ordinary real-estate choice: sell the land, which he expected would be subdivided and its century-old forest cleared, or find another use for it. Inspired by travel experiences combining nature with discovering sculpture inside a forested setting, he spent seven years saving before converting the property into a free public sculpture park, which opened October 23, 2020, with two curated trail loops, "Nature Nurtured" (nature-inspired work) and "Whimsy Way" (playful work) [secondary: [Pacific Northwest Sculptors](https://pnwsculptors.org/scott-price-price-sculpture-forest/)]. He placed a permanent conservation easement on the land with the Whidbey Camano Land Trust, protecting it from clearcutting or residential development regardless of what happens to the sculpture park itself as an operating institution [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Price_Sculpture_Forest)].

For this dataset, Price Sculpture Forest is useful precisely because of its modest scale: at roughly $221,000 in total assets against Chinati's $25M or Storm King's much larger base, it is a reminder that this report's `found-art-park` successes are not uniformly well-capitalized institutions, several are simply small, still-forming nonprofits running close to break-even. Its governance is `mixed` rather than confirmed sole control, echoing this dataset's own [[weisman]] finding that a founder who nominally leads a board is not the same as a founder with no board at all. Its most distinctive feature for the H8 founder-constraint question is the conservation easement itself: unlike every prior constraint this dataset has coded (Barnes's display rules, the Fisk/O'Keeffe sale restriction, Star of Hope's unenforced restoration mandate), the easement does not bind the *institution's* governance or programming at all, it binds the *land*, permanently, through a third-party land trust rather than the nonprofit's own board. Even if Price Sculpture Forest as an organization someday folded, the forest itself could not be cleared or developed, a durability mechanism entirely outside this dataset's standard endowment-ratio or board-governance framework, and one worth watching for in future small, land-based cases.
