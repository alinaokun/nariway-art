# Fernando Botero donation to Banco de la República (Museo Botero)

One-line: An artist donated both his own work and his personal collection of international masters directly to his country's central bank, which readily accepted the gift intact and built a dedicated free museum around it the same year — coded specifically as this run's Part D disconfirmation instrument against the belief that "the receiving end is closing," and this dataset's most direct H2 disconfirmation to date.

*(Public projection fields are in the frontmatter above, per [[case-template]]. The prose below is the internal research record.)*

**Why in the sample:** closes Colombia's gap in this run's Mexico/Colombia/Chile sweep and gives this dataset a second `origin: artist` intact-donation case (after Judd's and Bourgeois's own foundation-based models) with a genuinely distinct receiving institution — not a conventional art museum board, but a national central bank acting under a specific statutory cultural mandate (Law 31 of 1992). Most importantly for Part D: this is a clean, well-documented instance of an institution not merely accepting an intact gift but actively building a dedicated new museum around it within the same year, directly testing the "museums are closing the door on intact gifts" reading H2 has mostly accumulated evidence for.

## Narrative (sourced)

Fernando Botero, Colombia's best-known living artist at the time, donated 208 works to Banco de la República — the country's central bank — in 2000: 123 of his own paintings and sculptures, plus 85 works from his personal collection of international modern masters, including Picasso, Monet, Renoir, Dalí, Giacometti, Beckmann, Freud, Calder, and Bacon, spanning from Camille Corot's pre-1862 *Gitane au tambourin* to a 1998 Miquel Barceló oil [secondary: Banco de la República's own Red Cultural encyclopedia]. Colombian press described it as "the greatest gift that a Colombian has ever made to his country" [secondary]. Banco de la República, which had operated a national cultural network — libraries, gold museums, and art museums — under a reaffirmed statutory mandate (Law 31 of 1992) since long before the gift, opened the Museo Botero the same year in a colonial-era house in Bogotá's La Candelaria historic district, a building that had served as the archbishop's office until 1955 [secondary]. The museum offers free admission every day except Tuesday and sits within the bank's broader Red Cultural network alongside the Gold Museum, the Luis Ángel Arango Library, and the Miguel Urrutia Art Museum [secondary: Banco de la República]. No governance dispute, funding crisis, or deaccessioning controversy was located in the 23+ years since the gift, including the 3+ years since Botero's own 2023 death — the institution simply absorbed the collection into its existing cultural-operations structure and has run it without interruption since.
