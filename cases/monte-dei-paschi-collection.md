# Banca Monte dei Paschi di Siena — Historical Art Collection

One-line: the world's oldest bank (founded 1472) has held an institutional art collection — Sienese School paintings, sculpture, historical documents — for over 500 years, including through a 2013–2017 near-collapse and state bailout that liquidated nothing from the collection, the sharpest available contrast to [[lehman-brothers-collection]]'s complete art liquidation on its parent's 2008 bankruptcy.

**Why in the sample:** a corporate collection with no individual founder and the longest institutional tenure of any case in this dataset by an order of magnitude. Coded primarily as a **disconfirmation instrument**: it tests whether "a distressed/failed parent liquidates its art collection" (the mechanism [[lehman-brothers-collection]] cleanly demonstrates) generalizes, or whether it is contingent on the specific legal event (Chapter 11 liquidation to satisfy creditors) rather than financial distress as such. See [[what-we-now-believe]] for this run's disconfirmation entry.

## Narrative
Monte dei Paschi di Siena began in 1472 as a *monte di pietà* — a charitable pawnshop lending at low interest to Sienese citizens in financial difficulty — and took its present name in 1624 when the Grand Duke of Tuscany pledged the revenues of the state pastures (*paschi*) of the Maremma as collateral. It is widely described as the world's oldest bank still operating under its original name, and it began accumulating art and historical documents as an institutional holding not long after founding — one of the earliest non-royal, non-ecclesiastical corporate art collections anywhere, predating the modern "corporate art collection" concept (usually dated to Chase Manhattan's 1959 Art at Work program) by nearly 500 years. The collection centers on the Sienese School — Jacopo della Quercia, Pietro Lorenzetti, Sano di Pietro, Domenico Beccafumi, Sassetta — spanning the 13th through 19th centuries, still displayed in the bank's original headquarters, Palazzo Salimbeni, and periodically loaned to public exhibitions such as a 2023 show at Siena's Santa Maria della Scala.

In 1995 the bank spun off Fondazione Monte dei Paschi di Siena as a separate philanthropic banking foundation, the standard Italian structure (per the 1990 Amato banking law) that split commercial banking activity from the charitable/foundation activity historically bundled into Italy's old savings banks — a structural parallel worth noting against the U.S. private-operating-foundation model this dataset otherwise tracks. The foundation's own reported assets collapsed from €6.38 billion (2007) to €543.8 million (2020), reflecting the bank's near-catastrophic 2013–2017 crisis: Monte dei Paschi required a state bailout, and the Italian government took a 68% ownership stake in 2017 — one of the largest bank rescues in European history. Yet nothing in the sources found for this case suggests the *art collection itself* was liquidated, pledged, or even seriously discussed as a distress asset during that crisis. Since 2023, Italy has progressively sold down its stake (to roughly 11.7% by late 2024, further reduced through 2025) as part of EU-mandated reprivatization, and the bank has returned to majority-private ownership — with the historical art collection reportedly undisturbed throughout.

This is the report's cleanest available contrast to [[lehman-brothers-collection]]: two corporate art collections, two severe parent-institution financial crises, opposite art-collection outcomes. The likely mechanism is legal rather than cultural — Lehman's Chapter 11 filing put the collection into a bankruptcy estate that a creditors' committee was obligated to liquidate for cash, while MPS's rescue was a recapitalization (state equity injection, later resold) that never triggered an asset-liquidation process reaching the art. That distinction — insolvency-with-liquidation versus recapitalization-without-liquidation — is itself a finding worth carrying into the tax & legal structures ecosystem work, not just the case dataset.
