# Star of Hope Foundation (Robert Indiana) — Vinalhaven, Maine, USA

*Coded to [[case-template]]. WebFetch re-tested this run against projects.propublica.org and re-confirmed `EGRESS_BLOCKED` (see [[report-dataset]] for the running count); coded from WebSearch-snippet synthesis of press and the Center for Art Law's own case review, tagged `secondary` throughout. Chosen for Part D: this is a rare instance of a founder's constraints being fully DOCUMENTED (a will directing specific restoration and public-access terms) yet not honored by the successor board years later with no judicial modification, unlike Barnes (whose constraints were changed only via court process) — direct new evidence for the H8 evidence base.*

**Why in the sample:** an artist-founded foundation, not a museum, set up two years before the founder's death specifically to execute his stated wishes; the wishes were nonetheless not carried out, for reasons the Foundation itself frames as financial/programmatic rather than as a legal reversal. Distinct from Barnes (control judicially modified over decades of litigation) and Terra (a governance vacuum that produced dissolution) — here governance was never vacant (the will created the Foundation and, eventually, a full board), yet the documented intent was still set aside by ordinary board vote.

## Narrative
Robert Indiana bought the Star of Hope Lodge, a former Odd Fellows meeting hall, on the island of Vinalhaven, Maine, in the 1970s for roughly $10,000, and lived and worked there for four decades until his death on May 19, 2018, at 89 [secondary: Bangor Daily News, ARTnews]. Two years before he died, in 2016, he established the Star of Hope Foundation as the vehicle for his legacy, and his will directed that the Lodge specifically be restored "to museum quality" and opened to the public for visits, classes, and lectures [secondary: ARTnews]. His death set off years of estate litigation — including a dispute with the Morgan Art Foundation over rights to reproduce his best-known work, *LOVE* — that the Center for Art Law's own case review treated as a live, contested succession well into the early 2020s [secondary: Center for Art Law, "Is There HOPE for the Estate of Robert Indiana?"]. By the time the Foundation announced a "fully-formed Board of Directors" under executive chairman Lawrence J. Sterrs and a continued funding relationship with longtime dealer Simon Salama-Caro's family, most of the estate disputes had been resolved and the Foundation could begin setting a multiyear strategic plan [secondary: robertindiana.com].

That plan, announced in August 2026, is not the museum the will described. The Foundation is instead selling both the Lodge ($1.4M asking) and an adjacent property ($210K), with board chair Emily Lane stating the proceeds will go "directly into artistic and cultural initiatives on Vinalhaven" rather than into restoring and opening the house [secondary: Portland Press Herald]. No party has reported a legal challenge to this decision as of this run. This is a genuinely distinct H8 data point from Barnes: at Barnes, binding restrictions were tested and modified through decades of court proceedings before the collection could be moved; here, a specific, unambiguous written directive was simply not carried out by a legitimately governed successor board, with no litigation reported to enforce it. Whether that reflects the will's restoration directive being non-binding (aspirational language, not an enforceable trust term) or simply undisputed because no party with standing has objected is not resolved by any source located this run.
