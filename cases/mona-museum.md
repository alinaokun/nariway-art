## Narrative
MONA is the sample's first Australian case and its clearest example of a collector deliberately declining the nonprofit-museum form altogether — a genuinely distinct governance type from every other found-standalone-museum case coded so far (McNay, Menil, Phillips, Crystal Bridges, Glenstone), all of which are 501(c)(3)-equivalent structures with boards, however founder-dominated. Walsh's structure — a set of personally-owned holding entities, an art collection held in a separate "Family Art Group," a museum building held via a unit trust, none of it a registered charity — is a live rejection of the charitable-foundation default, made explicit in his own public statements about not wanting tax subsidies.

The financial profile this produces is unlike anything else in the sample. Where the dataset's usual durability metric (net-assets-to-opex, however flagged as an upper bound) assumes an accumulating balance sheet, MONA runs a large, planned, and publicly disclosed annual **operating loss** — AU$408 million cumulative — that is covered indefinitely by dividend income from Walsh's other businesses rather than by any fund attached to the museum itself. It is "thriving" by every visible measure (330,000 visitors a year, continued expansion, global reputation as a cultural and tourism landmark that transformed Hobart) while being, in the report's own vocabulary, financially unmeasurable by the standard ratio — a genuinely new entry for the report's data-availability finding (Pattern 4), and a sharp illustration that governance and money can point in *opposite* directions: MONA's governance is the most concentrated, least institutionally durable in the sample (one person, no board, no succession plan), yet its outcome is unambiguously thriving, precisely because durability here depends on the founder's continued personal choice and unrelated income, not on any structure that would survive him.
