# National Gallery of Art (Andrew W. Mellon founding gift)

One-line: A financier built and endowed a national art museum, insisted it never bear his own name, died five months after Congress accepted the gift and before it opened, and the institution has spent the next 89 years absorbing other collectors' intact gifts on top of his — this dataset's fourth, and most durable, test of whether a *government* receiving-institution type accepts collections more readily than an ordinary museum board.

*(Public projection fields are in the frontmatter above, per [[case-template]]. The prose below is the internal research record.)*

**Why in the sample — this run's Part D disconfirmation instrument:** [[what-we-now-believe]]'s most recent entry (the thirty-second pass, [[elliott-collection-university-of-iowa]]) named its own explicit next test: "a second institution-type example, ideally outside Latin America" after [[botero-donation-banco-de-la-republica]] (a central bank) and the Elliott case (a public university) both showed a receiving institution *with an independent, non-art funding base and a pre-existing statutory or civic cultural mandate* accepting an intact gift readily, in contrast to the "closing door" pattern H2 documents for ordinary single-revenue-stream museum boards. This case tests a third, and the most consequential, institution type named on that list but never yet coded: a national government, acting through its own legislature. It also does something none of the prior three did: it shows the SAME institution-type repeating the readily-accepted pattern for at least five more donors after the founding one, not just once.

## Narrative (sourced)

In December 1936, financier and former Treasury Secretary Andrew W. Mellon wrote to President Franklin D. Roosevelt offering to build and endow an art gallery for the United States in Washington, DC, and to donate his own collection as its nucleus [source: presidency.ucsb.edu]. Roosevelt recommended acceptance; on March 24, 1937, the 75th Congress approved a joint resolution establishing the National Gallery of Art [source: nga.gov/about-us/our-history]. Mellon died five months later, in August 1937, before ground was even broken on the building he had commissioned from architect John Russell Pope; the National Gallery of Art opened to the public in March 1941 [secondary: nga.gov]. Mellon's own condition — that the museum never bear his name, and be called simply "The National Gallery of Art" — is credited with making it easier, not harder, for other major collectors to give: because no single donor's name dominated the institution, the Kress brothers (Italian Renaissance painting and sculpture, beginning even before the museum opened), Joseph and P.A.B. Widener (1942), Lessing J. Rosenwald (prints and drawings), and Chester Dale (19th- and 20th-century French painting, bequeathed) each gave major intact collections into the same unified institution over the following decades, and Mellon's own son Paul later funded and opened the museum's East Building expansion in 1978 [source: nga.gov/about-us/our-history; philanthropyroundtable.org]. The institution is legally a federal establishment (commonly described as an independent bureau associated with the Smithsonian Institution, though functioning as its own government entity under 20 U.S.C. § 71), governed by a statutory Board of Trustees combining permanent ex officio government-office seats with private citizen trustees — a public-private hybrid found nowhere else in this dataset, and one this run's Part D disconfirmation hunt specifically targeted after [[botero-donation-banco-de-la-republica]] and [[elliott-collection-university-of-iowa]] both showed non-conventional receiving institutions (a central bank, a public university) accepting intact gifts unusually readily.
