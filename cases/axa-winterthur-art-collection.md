## Narrative

AXA Switzerland's art collection began in the 1920s under Winterthur Insurance, one of the country's largest insurers, acquiring Swiss modern art for display in its own offices — decades before "corporate collecting" became a recognized category. The collection survived two changes of parent ownership with no interruption: Credit Suisse acquired Winterthur Insurance in 1996 (later divesting the insurance arm again), and AXA acquired it outright in 2006, continuing to grow and display the roughly 1,300-work collection under the AXA Switzerland name. Unlike [[nestle-art-collection]], no dedicated governing foundation was located this run — the collection appears to remain a direct corporate asset managed by company staff, primarily furnishing representative rooms and public-facing office spaces, with a modern-and-contemporary Swiss core (Max Bill, Franz Gertsch, Silvie Fleury, John M. Armleder) and an international minority (Warhol, Franz West, John Chamberlain). A small dedicated public venue, the "AXA Exhibition Wall" in Winterthur's Superblock, shows rotating exhibitions of young contemporary art free of charge.

The case is coded specifically for a distress event none of this dataset's other "quiet" corporate collections ([[wesfarmers-art-collection]], [[td-bank-art-collection]], [[nestle-art-collection]]) have shown: between 2007 and 2015, the collection's own curator — a lawyer and art historian entrusted with its stewardship — systematically sold 165 works belonging to both the company and individual AXA clients through a dealer and at auction houses, pocketing roughly CHF 1.1 million. He was caught after a sticker placed by an AXA employee on one piece surfaced at auction. A Winterthur district court convicted him of embezzlement in June 2019, sentencing him to 39 months; Zurich's cantonal high court upheld both the conviction and the sentence on appeal. No source located this run reports any reduction in the collection's scope, public programming, or corporate commitment following the scandal — AXA's own current materials describe an active, ~1,300-work collection continuing to serve the same purpose it always has.

This makes AXA a useful, if modest, addition to this evidence base's governance-durability discussion from a new angle: not founder-death succession (Terra), not board litigation (Rauschenberg), but sustained *internal theft by an insider entrusted with custodial authority* — arguably the closest corporate-collection analogue yet coded to the kind of insider betrayal this dataset's H8 founder-constraint cluster has mostly examined through wills and trustee disputes. The collection's apparent full recovery, absorbed quietly by a large corporate parent with no public reckoning beyond the criminal proceeding itself, is consistent with this evidence base's emerging read (see [[what-we-now-believe]]'s Rauschenberg entry) that an institution's capacity to absorb even a real, adjudicated insider betrayal may depend more on the size and stability of its backing than on the severity of the betrayal itself.
