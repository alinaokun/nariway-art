# Pyramid Hill Sculpture Park and Museum

One-line: Retired attorney Harry T. Wilks bought Ohio farmland in 1987 to build a glass-pyramid house, kept acquiring parcels, and in 1997 created a nonprofit sculpture park (now ~300+ acres, ~70 monumental works, plus an Ancient Sculpture Museum) explicitly to shield the land from development; it has run 12 years past his 2014 death under a hired executive director.

**Why in the sample:** the *smallest-scale* found-art-park and the clearest land-preservation motive — value framed as protecting the landscape, not the art market. Tests H7A (a solo founder who installed no family successor) and H8 (a nonprofit built as a development firewall).

## Narrative (sourced)

Harry T. Wilks, a retired attorney, bought land in the hills of Hamilton, Ohio in 1987 to build the Pyramid House — a residence topped with a glass pyramid — then kept buying adjacent parcels, clearing space and adding roads, trails and small lakes [secondary: [pyramidhill.org/about](https://www.pyramidhill.org/about); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid_Hill_Sculpture_Park_and_Museum)]. His explicit motive was preservation: to keep the assembled landscape out of private development, he created a nonprofit sculpture park in 1997 (opened to the public in 1996), which frames the case's value as landscape protection rather than art-market value (H4) [secondary]. The collection grew to roughly 70 monumental outdoor sculptures, and in 2016–17 Wilks's institution added indoor galleries and an Ancient Sculpture Museum of Greek, Roman, Etruscan and Egyptian antiquities — a coherence drift from the outdoor-modern thesis [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid_Hill_Sculpture_Park_and_Museum)]. Wilks was a solo decision-owner who installed no family successor; after his 2014 death the board ran a national search and seated Sean FitzGibbons as executive director in 2019, the succession event that makes this `survived_founder = yes` [secondary]. Financially it is the smallest of the four: FY2023 net assets of $11.38M against $1.55M of expenses give a net-assets-to-opex cushion near 7.3x, but the operating base is thin and some years (e.g. FY2022) ran deficits, so the outcome is coded **stable**, not thriving [primary, [990 via ProPublica](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/311439692)]. As with the other parks the 7.3x is an **upper bound** — net assets are dominated by the land and buildings, and the true endowment (Schedule D) has not been pulled.
