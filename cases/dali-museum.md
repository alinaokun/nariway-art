One-line: A. Reynolds Morse (1914–2000) and Eleanor Reese Morse (1912–2010) built the world's most comprehensive private Salvador Dalí collection over ~40 years, gave it whole to a new St. Petersburg nonprofit (opened 1982 in an adapted warehouse; purpose-built $30M+ building 2011), which is thriving and well-capitalized (~$124M net assets) two decades after the founders' deaths.

## Narrative

A. Reynolds Morse and Eleanor Reese Morse — an Ohio industrialist couple — bought their first Salvador Dalí painting in March 1943 after seeing a 1942 retrospective, beginning a roughly 40-year friendship with the artist and the most comprehensive private Dalí collection in the world [source: https://thedali.org/about-the-museum/mission-history/]. By the mid-1970s they decided to donate the entire collection intact rather than disperse it; a Wall Street Journal article ("U.S. Art World Dillydallies Over Dalí") galvanized St. Petersburg, Florida civic leaders to compete for it, and the collection — after years on display near Cleveland — opened in a rehabilitated downtown marine warehouse in March 1982 [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Dal%C3%AD_Museum]. The pathway is the purest tight-single-thesis case in the sample: one artist, every period and medium, roughly 2,400 works today [source: https://thedali.org/about-the-museum/mission-history/]. It is also a clear survive-the-founder success — Reynolds Morse died in 2000, Eleanor in 2010, and the institution not only continued but opened a purpose-built, hurricane-rated building (reportedly $30M+) in January 2011, after both founders' deaths [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Dal%C3%AD_Museum]. The finances are strong: FY2025 (year ending June 30, 2025) shows $22.4M revenue against $12.6M expenses and net assets of $123.8M — a net-assets-to-opex ratio near 9.8x, an upper bound that includes the new building and does not isolate a true endowment [source: https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/592015192]. The coded outcome is thriving/strong, with the standard caveat that endowment coverage cannot be separated from real-property value until Schedule D is pulled.
