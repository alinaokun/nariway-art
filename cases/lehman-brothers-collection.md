## Narrative
The Lehman Brothers art collection is not a story of taste, philanthropy, or succession — it is the cleanest corporate-failure dispersal in the sample, useful to the report precisely because every other variable is held constant except the parent entity's solvency. The core of the collection was originally built for Neuberger Berman, an independent money-management firm, by curator I. Michael Danoff, assembling contemporary works by artists including Damien Hirst, Takashi Murakami, Richard Prince, and Julie Mehretu to decorate corporate offices. Lehman Brothers acquired Neuberger Berman in 2003, and the art came with the acquisition; when Lehman collapsed, Neuberger Berman's management bought the asset-management firm back out of the bankruptcy estate, but the art itself stayed behind with Lehman, becoming part of the assets available to satisfy the roughly $613 billion owed to creditors after the September 15, 2008 Chapter 11 filing — the largest bankruptcy in U.S. history.

Two years later, almost to the day, the collection was liquidated across three geographically and institutionally separated auctions: Sotheby's New York on September 25, 2010 sold 118 works for $12.3 million, above the high pre-sale estimate; Christie's London followed four days later, on September 29, with a sale of artwork and ephemera fetching £1,631,238 (about $2.57 million); a third, smaller sale at Freeman's in Philadelphia on November 7, 2010 focused on the contemporary-art holdings specifically, though this session's sources did not isolate its exact total. At least one contemporaneous summary put the combined total near $16 million, a figure that does not fully reconcile against the two confirmed sale totals and is flagged here as an approximate, unverified aggregate rather than a checked sum.

There is no governance chapter to this case in the usual sense: no board succession, no donor intent, no keep-together clause, because a corporate office collection carries none of those by design. The "decision owner" was, functionally, the bankruptcy process itself — the estate and its creditors' committee, executing through Sotheby's and Christie's as auction-house agents — a decision-making structure the current template vocabulary has no clean value for, alongside the Michener case's civic-founding gap. For the report, Lehman is most valuable as a contrast case: it demonstrates what "distress" looks like with zero collecting-taste or family-conflict content at all, purely a balance-sheet event at the parent-company level cascading down to liquidate an unrelated cultural asset. It sits usefully alongside the sample's other funding-driven failures (Corcoran, di Rosa) as the corporate-sector version of the same underlying mechanism — an asset's cultural life ending because its financial host failed, not because anyone stopped caring about the art.
