# Sid Richardson Museum — a small single-thesis collection kept alive inside a grantmaking foundation

*Coded to [[case-template]]. Report-grade, sourced. A quiet, 65+-year survivor: a tight Western-art collection assembled by a Texas oilman who died in 1959, kept as a free public museum in downtown Fort Worth, funded not by its own endowment but as a standing program of the family foundation he chartered twelve years before his death. Governance was locked in ahead of the founder's death via the 1947 foundation and his will's residuary bequest — the case's central interest for the report's survival-vs-governance thesis. Gap-honest: no separate 990 filer for the museum was found — its finances are inseparable from the parent foundation's, and several figures (exact collection count, exact founding-endowment dollar amount) are contested or unlocated and coded `unknown`.*

## Narrative
Sid W. Richardson (1891–1959), born in Athens, Texas, built one of the largest fortunes in mid-century America in West Texas oil — popular estimates ran as high as $800 million — and became known as the "bachelor billionaire." In the early 1940s he began building a collection of Western American art, commissioning New York dealer Bertram M. Newhouse of Newhouse Galleries to acquire works, with the collection ultimately concentrated on Frederic Remington and Charles M. Russell. In 1947, at his friend Amon Carter's urging, Richardson chartered the Sid W. Richardson Foundation to systematize his giving to Texas churches, hospitals, and schools — twelve years before his death. When he died of a heart attack at his St. Joseph's Island compound on September 30, 1959, his will (naming nephew Perry R. Bass, John B. Connally, and Howell E. Smith as independent executors) left the residue of his estate to the Foundation as residuary legatee; the estate's complex oil-lease and securities holdings, plus tax claims, delayed the Foundation's full use of the funds until 1962.

The art collection itself did not become a standalone public museum immediately. It was first shown on long-term loan at the Amon Carter Museum starting January 1964, then opened as its own institution — the Sid Richardson Collection of Western Art — in a purpose-built replica of an 1895 structure in Fort Worth's Sundance Square on November 23, 1982, twenty-three years after Richardson's death. The museum underwent a 14-month renovation beginning in 2005, reopening in 2006 with roughly 30% more space, a new façade, and (per available secondary sources) the current "Sid Richardson Museum" name. It has been free to the public throughout, drawing a reported 50,000+ visitors annually from all 50 states and over 50 countries.

Structurally, the museum was never separately incorporated: it is, and appears to have remained, a program of the Sid W. Richardson Foundation — "funding for the museum is entirely generated by" the Foundation, per the Texas State Historical Association's handbook entry. That Foundation is today chaired by Edward P. Bass, with his brothers Sid R. and Lee M. Bass as vice presidents — grandnephews of the founder, sons of Perry Bass, who himself became a director after Richardson's death and ran the Foundation until his own death in 2006. Valleau Wilkie Jr. led the Foundation from 1973 to 2011; Pete Geren is the current president and CEO. The governance line from founder to today is unbroken family control layered over professional staff leadership — the opposite of an independent, arm's-length board, but one whose succession mechanism (the 1947 charter plus the 1959 will) was fixed before the founder's death rather than negotiated after it. On the Foundation's most recent available Form 990-PF (accessed via ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer), the Foundation reports $519.7M in net assets, $526.8M in total assets against $7.1M in liabilities, $44.6M in total revenue, and $27.6M in total expenses — figures that describe the Foundation's entire grantmaking operation, not the museum in isolation; no museum-specific budget, endowment, or headcount figure was located.

The case reads as a "quiet stable survivor": a tight, single-thesis regional collection, deliberately not built as a trophy institution, kept alive for 65+ years post-founder-death not through its own endowment but by being folded into a much larger, financially healthy family foundation whose governance was locked in by legal instrument before the founder died. That embedding is simultaneously the source of its durability (a large, professionally run parent with no apparent succession crisis) and the source of this case file's principal data gap (no independent museum-level financial statement to test against the report's endowment-to-opex durability metric).
