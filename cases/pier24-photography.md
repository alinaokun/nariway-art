# Pier 24 Photography

One-line: Andrew and Mary Pilara built one of the world's largest dedicated photography collections and gave it a free, 28,000-square-foot waterfront home in 2010; after a five-year rent standoff with the Port of San Francisco, they closed it in July 2025 and are auctioning the 4,000+-work collection to seed a new grantmaking foundation in health care, education and the arts — a deliberate sale chosen from strength, not a distress liquidation.

**Why in the sample:** the sixth data point in this dataset's "building vs. collection" comparison set ([[cam-raleigh]], [[rauschenberg-foundation-hq]], [[deutsche-bank-collection]], and others) — real estate, not curatorial failure or public disinterest, ended a thriving institution — and a rare case of a fully *planned*, publicly announced sale used to fund a deliberate mission pivot, worth reading directly against this dataset's "sell wins by default, not by design" finding: this sale was clearly by design.

## Narrative (sourced)

Andrew Pilara, a former investment banker, became a serious photography collector after a 2003 Diane Arbus retrospective, and with his wife Mary built the Pilara Family Collection into one of the most extensive surveys of 20th-century and postwar photography anywhere — eventually 4,000 to 5,000+ works [secondary: Wikipedia; pier24.org]. In 2010 they opened Pier 24 Photography on a former Port of San Francisco pier, free and by appointment, becoming reportedly the largest exhibition space in the world dedicated solely to photography [secondary: pier24.org]. The institution ran successfully for fifteen years, but the Port of San Francisco, its landlord, sought to roughly triple the rent at lease renewal; a five-year negotiation failed to produce new terms, and the Pilara Foundation announced closure, effective when the lease expired in July 2025 [secondary: [SF Chronicle](https://www.sfchronicle.com/sf/article/pier-24-photography-closure-17747049.php); [SF Standard](https://sfstandard.com/2023/01/27/famed-pier-24-photography-museum-to-close/)]. Rather than donate or disperse the collection to museums, the Foundation chose to auction it, with proceeds funding a deliberate pivot toward grantmaking in health care research, education, and the arts [secondary: [pier24.org](https://pier24.org/pilara-foundation-changing-philanthropic-focus/)]. This is a genuinely useful contrast case for this dataset's evolving reading of "sell wins by default, not by design" ([[what-we-now-believe]]): unlike [[herbert-lust-collection]]'s post-death scramble or the standard estate-driven auction pattern, this sale was announced years in advance, by living founders, as a considered reallocation of capital toward a new mission — a sale that is unambiguously *by design*, not a default outcome of nobody coordinating the decision. It joins [[cam-raleigh]], [[rauschenberg-foundation-hq]], and [[deutsche-bank-collection]] as a sixth instance of this dataset's recurring "the building's economics failed, not the collection's" pattern — here at the scale of a whole freestanding museum rather than a single headquarters or gallery space.
