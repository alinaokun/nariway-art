# Jules Strauss Collection (heirs' 2022 sale)

One-line: a collector's holdings, already scattered by Nazi-era forced sales and looting, resurface generations later as jointly-owned property of 18 living heirs — and default to auction not because any heir wanted to sell, but because French civil-law co-ownership (*indivision successorale*) leaves no single decision-owner able to keep a recovered work once inheritance has fragmented it across that many descendants. A distinct, civil-law-specific instance of the "unowned decision" H7A already tracks, and a fifth enforcement-mechanism shape for H8, alongside the UK/US will- and statute-based constraints this dataset has coded (Barnes, Gardner, Soane, Wallace, Star of Hope).

*(Public projection fields are in the frontmatter above, per [[case-template]] — a `record`-depth page: the founding collection's full scope and fate are not reconstructable in detail, so only the sourced, restitution-driven 2022 event is recorded.)*

**Why in the sample.** This dataset's H8 governance-and-constraint cases have so far been drawn entirely from UK/US instruments: Barnes's private indenture (court-modified), Gardner's board-vesting will, Soane's private Act of Parliament, the Wallace Collection's statutory bequest, Star of Hope's unenforced will. Candidate-universe.md's own open-threads note has flagged, across several runs, the need for "a fifth H8 enforcement-mechanism instance outside the UK/US common-law tradition." French civil-law *indivision* — where an asset inherited by multiple heirs is, absent unanimous agreement otherwise, jointly and equally owned by all of them, with any one able to force a sale (a *licitation*) if consensus fails — is structurally unlike any mechanism already coded: it is not a document a founder wrote to bind the future, but a default-by-operation-of-law that acts on a family's holdings whether or not anyone chose it, and it strengthens with every generation that passes without an active decision, exactly the "unowned decision" mechanism H1 and H7A track, but produced by statute rather than absence of advice.

## Narrative (sourced)

Jules Strauss, a Frankfurt-born banker who made his life in Paris, built an extensive collection ranging from antiquities to the Impressionists [secondary: Forward, "The Nazis looted more than 500 of their paintings"]. After the 1940 German occupation of France, Strauss was forced to sell major works, including "Portrait of a Lady as Pomona" (then attributed to Nicolas de Largillière), which he had owned since 1928; the German Reichsbank in Berlin acquired it in 1941 [secondary]. The painting passed through East German state custody after the war, was transferred to the National Gallery in Berlin in 1953, and was later moved to and displayed at the Staatliche Kunstsammlungen Dresden [secondary]. Strauss himself avoided deportation but died of ill health in occupied Paris in 1943 [secondary].

Decades later, Strauss's great-granddaughter, the writer Pauline Baer de Perignon, undertook a personal investigation that led to the painting's identification and, following an official finding that the 1941 sale was a forced one, its restitution by the Dresden museum [secondary: The Times of Israel; Lady Margaret Hall]. Baer de Perignon has described the restitution as conditioned on the family agreeing to resell the work to the museum, "a second forced sale," though that specific resale to Dresden was not the outcome that ultimately occurred [secondary]. By the time of restitution, the painting was jointly owned by 18 living Strauss descendants, making it, in Baer de Perignon's own words, "shared property" she "knew all along... did not belong to me" alone [secondary]. With no single heir positioned to buy out 17 others or to unilaterally decide the work's fate, the family consigned it to Sotheby's New York, where it sold in January 2022 for an estimated $1–1.5 million [secondary].

We read this case as illustrating a mechanism this dataset had not yet coded explicitly: French civil-law *indivision successorale* (undivided co-ownership among heirs) functions as a structural default toward sale, independent of any single heir's preference, wealth, or advisory relationship. It differs from the "unmanaged, so it drifts toward sale" pattern H1 already tracks (e.g. [[herbert-lust-collection]]) in a specific way — H1's cases involve one or few decision-makers who simply never got around to planning; here, the *number* of legal co-owners itself is the mechanism, growing every generation an asset goes unaddressed, until unanimous retention becomes practically impossible regardless of anyone's intent. It is also a fifth, distinct H8 enforcement-mechanism shape: not a constraint a founder wrote to bind the future (Barnes, Gardner, Soane, Wallace), but the complete absence of one, with civil law's own default rule filling the vacuum exactly the way this evidence base's "founder's disease" mechanism predicts an institutional vacuum would.
