## Narrative
Betye Saar, the assemblage artist renowned for reframing racist and sexist imagery in works like *The Liberation of Aunt Jemima* (1972), began collecting Black dolls in the late 1960s in Los Angeles — a pursuit she connected to growing up in the 1920s and 1930s without ever owning one herself [secondary: [The New York Historical](https://www.nyhistory.org/exhibitions/betye-saars-black-dolls)]. Over roughly six decades the collection grew past 100 objects, ranging from rag dolls handmade by Black women for both Black and white children in their care, to mass-produced toys and tourist souvenirs, some born directly of racist caricature — material that doubled as a working archive feeding her own assemblage practice. The collection includes *Hoo Doo Woman* (1974), the only doll in it that Saar made herself [secondary: Hyperallergic].

In 2026, The New York Historical announced a landmark promised gift of the collection and mounted the exhibition "Betye Saar's Black Dolls" (May 8–October 4, 2026), timed to Saar's 100th birthday, showing 26 of the dolls alongside 14 related watercolors and assemblages [secondary: The New York Historical's own press materials]. Saar died at her Los Angeles home on July 26, 2026 — four days before the milestone birthday the show was built around — leaving the promised gift's final legal completion unresolved as of this run.
