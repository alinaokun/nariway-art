## Narrative

Adrian Jones, a British collector, began acquiring Vietnamese art in 1987 during an extended visit to the country, continuing across further trips through 2003. Rather than assembling the collection casually, Jones reviewed the several hundred works he had by then gathered and sought in-depth advice from more than 40 Vietnamese individuals — artists, historians, and cultural figures — before formally establishing it as the Witness Collection in 2003. The collection has since grown to roughly 2,000 paintings and drawings by more than 120 Vietnamese artists, spanning folk, academic, and propaganda/socialist-realist traditions through cubism, expressionism, and abstraction from the 1920s to the 2000s — one of the largest private collections of Vietnamese art in the world.

In 2009, Jones formalized a governance structure, establishing Asiarta Foundation in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, as an independent nonprofit to conserve and document the collection, taking over artist-support and research projects Jones had already been running informally since 2002. This cross-border structure — a collection built around one country's art, stewarded by a foundation based in a different country entirely, led by a collector who is a citizen of neither — is a genuinely new shape for this dataset, distinct from every other `traveling-program` case coded so far (e.g., [[schnitzer-family-foundation]]'s domestically-based US lending program). The collection is not currently housed in any dedicated museum; part of it is visible at Saigon Domaine in Ho Chi Minh City, which hosts two galleries with rotating exhibitions, while the foundation's own stated plan is a further series of touring exhibitions, a documentary film, and books on Vietnamese art, "before finally finding a permanent home for the collection" — an explicitly still-open institutional question, coded here as `outcome_category: emerging` rather than any settled outcome.

This case offers a useful contrast to this dataset's more typical `found-standalone-museum` collector cases: nearly 40 years of sustained, methodical, advisor-consulted collecting has produced real institutional infrastructure (a dedicated foundation, conservation and documentation capacity, touring programming) without the collector ever building — or, on current evidence, even planning — a museum of his own. It is a live test of whether the "build a museum" pathway is in fact necessary for a large, coherent, publicly-engaged collection to achieve real institutional durability, or whether a touring/foundation model can substitute for it indefinitely.
