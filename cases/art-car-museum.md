# Art Car Museum (Harithas Collection)

One-line: Ann and James Harithas opened Houston's Art Car Museum in 1998 to house the "art car" movement Ann had helped launch with a 1984 exhibition; when both died within two years of each other (2021, 2023) with no foundation or succession instrument ever established, the museum closed permanently in 2024, and their family then donated $1.25M to fund a new dedicated space for the collection at the Orange Show Center for Visionary Art, an existing related nonprofit where one of their children sits on the board.

**Why in the sample:** a clean, contemporary confirming case for H6/H8's founder-sole/no-succession-plan collapse mechanism (the same "founder's disease" pattern named for [[terra]]) — a genuine nonprofit legal wrapper existed, but no dedicated foundation, endowment, or written succession instruction was ever created for what happened after both founders died. Also a genuinely new coda this dataset had not shown before: unlike Terra's forced grantmaking pivot or Corcoran's court-ordered dissolution, the rescue here was voluntary, informal, and fast — a family gift to a related nonprofit, closing within months rather than years, with a family board seat at the receiving institution (a fresh, low-friction instance of the H2 insider-exception pattern on the receiving side; see Gaps below).

## Narrative
Ann Harithas curated "Collision" at Houston's Lawndale Art Center in 1984, an exhibition that launched both the city's annual Art Car Parade and Houston's status as a hub of the international art car movement. In February 1998 she and her husband James Harithas — a museum professional who had previously directed the Corcoran Gallery of Art (see [[corcoran-gallery]]), the Everson Museum of Art, the Contemporary Arts Museum Houston, and later Houston's own Station Museum of Contemporary Art — opened the Art Car Museum, a free, not-for-profit venue nicknamed "Garage Mahal," to house Ann's growing collection of art cars and support the broader movement. The museum operated for nearly 26 years. Ann Harithas died in 2021; a small family trust funded roughly two more years of programs she had already planned. James Harithas died in 2023. Per Glasstire's direct reporting, the couple had "disliked bureaucracy" enough that they never established a dedicated foundation or left specific instructions for their heirs on how to keep the museum operating in perpetuity — despite the museum itself holding standard nonprofit status. With no succession structure in place, the museum's leadership announced its permanent closure in early 2024, effective that April. The collection did not go into limbo for long: the Harithas family — children Thomas "Will" Robinson, Madeline Merrill, Molly Kemp, and Stephanie Loeffler — donated $1.25 million through the family's South Texas Charitable Foundation to the Orange Show Center for Visionary Art, an existing Houston nonprofit devoted to folk and visionary art, to build a roughly 5,000-square-foot dedicated space for the art car collection as part of that institution's own campus expansion. Robinson sits on the Orange Show Center's board, a family—receiving-institution tie broadly consistent with this dataset's H2 insider-exception pattern, though the shape here (a family funding a *rescue* into an institution one of its own members already sits on the board of, after the founders' own institution failed) is distinct from every prior H2 case, which involve a living collector's own gift decision, not a posthumous family-led salvage.
