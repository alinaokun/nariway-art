# Lessing J. Rosenwald Collection → National Gallery of Art / Library of Congress

One-line: A retired Sears chairman pledged his entire 28,000+-item print, drawing, and rare-book collection to two federal institutions in 1943 — decades before his death — explicitly because he distrusted a conventional museum's (Philadelphia Museum of Art's) ability to fund its upkeep, choosing instead two institutions with independent, non-art funding bases; this run's Part D disconfirmation instrument testing whether the "receiving-institution-type" refinement of H2 extends to a national library.

*(Public projection fields are in the frontmatter above, per [[case-template]] — the single machine source the export reads. The prose below is the internal research record.)*

**Why in the sample — this run's Part D disconfirmation instrument.** [[what-we-now-believe]]'s chain of H2 "receiving-institution-type" entries (Botero/Banco de la República → Elliott/University of Iowa → Mellon/National Gallery of Art) has tested a central bank, a public university, and a national art museum, each showing that an institution with an independent, non-art funding base absorbs an intact gift more readily than a conventional single-revenue museum board. The Mellon entry's own "next test" line named "a national library... to see whether 'independent funding base plus organizational capacity' generalizes even further." This run searched directly for that instance and found Rosenwald — and it goes further than a simple institution-type test: Rosenwald's own recorded reasoning names the *mechanism* explicitly. His gift was directed away from the Philadelphia Museum of Art specifically **because of concern over that museum's operating deficit** [secondary: The Philadelphia Award biographical sourcing], and toward the National Gallery of Art and Library of Congress instead — both federally funded institutions with funding bases entirely independent of ticket revenue or a single city's philanthropic capacity. This is the first case in this evidence base where a donor's own stated reasoning, not just an outcome pattern inferred after the fact, tracks the "conventional museum vs. independent-funding-base institution" distinction the last four disconfirmation passes have been building from the outside.

## Narrative (sourced)

Lessing J. Rosenwald, eldest son of Sears, Roebuck chairman Julius Rosenwald, rose from stock boy to Sears chairman himself before retiring at 49 to devote the rest of his life to collecting rare books and prints [secondary: Wikipedia, Britannica]. He built his collection at his own private repository, Alverthorpe Gallery, on his estate in Jenkintown, Pennsylvania, assembling comprehensive surveys of Albrecht Dürer's and Rembrandt's prints, one of the largest American holdings of William Blake material, and one of the country's largest gatherings of 15th- and 16th-century German woodcuts and engravings [secondary: NGA's 75th-anniversary retrospective; the dedicated Blake/An Illustrated Quarterly checklist of Blake material in the Rosenwald Collection]. On March 17, 1943 — with World War II still underway and decades before his death — Rosenwald announced he was "collecting for the nation," pledging his entire collection to two different federal institutions split by medium: prints and drawings to the National Gallery of Art, and rare books (2,600 titles) plus a 5,000-volume reference library to the Library of Congress [secondary: NGA.gov; LOC.gov "About this Collection"]. He retained physical custody at Alverthorpe for the rest of his life, periodically transferring selections to Washington for exhibition and continuing to add to both halves of the collection until he died in 1979, at which point the remainder was physically transferred and the collection numbered more than 28,000 items combined [secondary: LOC finding aid; NGA sourcing]. Biographical sourcing states directly that Rosenwald's choice of recipient was deliberate: he willed his collection to the National Gallery of Art and Library of Congress "because of his concern over the Philadelphia Museum of Art's operating deficit, and his conviction that his collections would reach the public" [secondary: The Philadelphia Award, Rosenwald's own award-citation biography]. Both institutions still hold, catalogue, and actively exhibit the collection: NGA describes the 1943 gift as immediately catapulting its prints-and-drawings department "into one of the most exceptional in the country," and both institutions' own collection pages continue to cite "Rosenwald Collection" as a named, intact holding 47 years after his death — the same durable-attribution pattern already coded for the Kress, Widener, Dale, and Mellon gifts absorbed into the National Gallery of Art (see [[national-gallery-of-art-mellon]]).

We read this as strengthening, not merely repeating, the receiving-institution-type chain: it is the first case where the *donor's own stated reasoning* — an explicit comparison between a financially strained conventional museum and two institutions with independent federal funding — matches the mechanism this evidence base had previously only inferred from outcome patterns across Botero, Elliott, and Mellon. It also extends the tested institution-type roster to a **national library** for the first time, closing the specific gap the Mellon entry named. The attempted disconfirmation (whether a national library would behave differently from a central bank, university, or national art museum, and readily absorb an intact gift) failed to break the pattern: LOC's acceptance was, per available sourcing, as unconditional and as durable as NGA's own.
