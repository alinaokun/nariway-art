## Narrative
David Lloyd Kreeger (1909–1990), the son of Eastern European immigrants, entered the insurance industry in 1948 as one of the investors who purchased GEICO, eventually serving as its chairman. He and his wife Carmen began collecting nineteenth- and twentieth-century paintings and sculpture in 1959, working with major museum curators as advisors, and by the early 1960s had commissioned Philip Johnson to design a house on Foxhall Road that would serve two purposes across two timeframes: their residence while they lived, and — by explicit design brief — a public art museum afterward. Johnson's 1968 building used a modular system of cube-like galleries that could function as living space or exhibition space, a structure built to survive the conversion the Kreegers intended for it from the start. In that sense the succession plan here predates almost every other case in the sample: it was locked into the architecture itself, twenty-two years before David Kreeger's 1990 death.

David Kreeger died in 1990; Carmen Kreeger continued living in the house for two more years before moving out in 1992 to complete its conversion into a museum, which opened to the public on June 1, 1994 under founding director Judy A. Greenberg. Carmen Kreeger remained active with the museum until her own death in 2003. Governance since has run through the founders' children — Peter L. Kreeger and Carol Kreeger Ingall — joined by outside trustee Robert E. Davis and counsel Jay W. Freedman, a small, family-anchored board the museum itself describes as maintained specifically to protect the founders' original vision. The Kreegers did not want the collection actively bought into or deaccessioned; the museum has followed that wish, permitting only board-approved donations, consistent with more recent AAM/AAMD guidelines.

Financially, the museum runs on a structure that is durable but not self-contained: roughly half its operating budget, about $500,000 a year, comes as a discretionary annual grant from the separately governed David Lloyd Kreeger Foundation (a private operating foundation, tax-exempt since 1962), with the remainder from earned income, memberships, and gifts. That split funding model — a museum leaning on a related but legally distinct foundation's year-to-year generosity rather than its own locked endowment — is itself a durability signal worth tracking at the report's 50-case scale: succession by architecture and family board continuity has kept the doors open for 35 years, but the museum's own balance sheet could not be independently verified this session, and its dependence on a sister entity's discretion is a different risk profile than an institution funded from its own restricted corpus.

The Kreeger's most notable brush with external scrutiny came in late 2015, when the Senate Finance Committee, under Sen. Orrin Hatch, sent inquiry letters to eleven private art museums nationwide — including both DC-area private museums, Kreeger and Glenstone — asking for attendance, loan, and acquisition data, part of a broader congressional look at whether private museums deliver public benefit commensurate with the tax advantages their donors receive. Coverage at the time characterized Kreeger as an "above-board" museum that was caught in a dragnet aimed at more aggressive tax-shelter structures elsewhere, not a museum accused of wrongdoing itself — but the episode is a useful data point for the report's governance chapter on how private museums are perceived externally, independent of whether any one institution is at fault.
