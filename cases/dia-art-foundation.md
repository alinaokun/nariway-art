## Narrative
Dia Art Foundation is the sample's sharpest test yet of Pattern 1's governance-beats-endowment claim, precisely because it runs the experiment on *itself*, twice, on two different clocks. From 1974 to 1985 Dia was governance-control **founder-sole** in the most literal sense this sample has seen: one person's fortune (Philippa de Menil's Schlumberger-heiress wealth) funded the entire institution with no independent capital base, no diversified donor pool, and, per the New York Attorney General's own scrutiny at the time, apparently insufficient internal financial controls. That combination produced exactly the kind of governance vacuum Pattern 1 associates with failure — except here the trigger was not a founder's *death* (the pattern's usual clock) but a *funding-and-regulatory crisis while the controlling founder was still alive*. Heiner Friedrich, himself a founder, was ousted from his own institution in January 1985; the Foundation took on Citibank debt secured against stock; and it sold 18 major works (including pieces by Warhol, Twombly, and Judd) at Sotheby's that November to cover its position. This is a genuine near-death the dataset should hold up alongside Terra's *actual* death, not as a repeat of the same mechanism but as a variant: **founder-sole control can produce a survivable crisis rather than a fatal one, if the crisis triggers a real governance handoff before assets are fully exhausted** — Dia's board expanded to seven members and brought in outside leadership (Ashton Hawkins) in the same reorganization that resolved the debt, rather than dissolving. Forty-one years later the Foundation looks, by every figure found this run, comparatively strong: roughly a quarter-billion dollars in net assets against ~$27.5M in annual expenses, a ~9.9x upper-bound ratio in the same range as several of the sample's healthiest cases — though this is inflated by real property across a dozen sites, exactly the caveat Pattern 10 raises for asset-heavy operating collections, and should not be read as a clean liquidity cushion.

The case also sharpens the [[chinati]] comparison the candidate universe flagged it for. Chinati is Judd's own, single-site, artist-controlled permanence foundation, and the sample's live puzzle (Pattern 1's "complication") is that Chinati's `founder-sole` governance produced 32 years of thriving post-Judd continuity, unlike Terra's failure under the same coded value. Dia offers a partial resolution: like Chinati, Dia's core artistic content (permanently installed, largely unrotated single-artist galleries for Judd, Flavin, Serra, de Maria, and others) is designed to remove ongoing discretionary curatorial fights, the same "permanence doctrine" mechanism Pattern 1 credits for Chinati's resilience. But Dia's *governance*, unlike Chinati's, did not stay founder-sole — it was forcibly professionalized in 1985, decades before any founder's death, precisely because the funding side (not the collection side) was in crisis. Read together, Dia suggests the permanence doctrine protects the *art* from founder-sole risk, while a separate, harder question — who controls the *money* — still needs an independent board to survive a real crisis. Chinati has not yet been tested on that second axis the way Dia already has.

Finally, Dia is a useful complication for [[storm-king]]'s family-succession model. Storm King's governance passed cleanly through three generations of one family (Ogden → Stern → Stern) over 66 years with no crisis. Dia's multi-site, multi-founder structure (three co-founders, only one of whom controlled the money) never had a comparable family-succession option once Friedrich was ousted from an institution he had co-founded but did not own outright — a reminder that "foundation" as a legal form can mean either "family trust that quietly reweights control across generations" (Storm King) or "an institution whose founders can be removed from it entirely by its own board" (Dia), and the schema's single `governance_control_at_founding` field cannot yet distinguish which kind of founder-sole a case is.
