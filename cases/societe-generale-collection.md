## Narrative
Société Générale's collection began in 1995 under then-chairman Marc Viénot, timed to the bank's move to its La Défense tower, with the stated aim of bringing "a little soul" and a forward-looking cultural identity to the new headquarters [secondary: [Société Générale Private Banking](https://www.privatebanking.societegenerale.com/en/insights/societe-generale-collection/)]. Structured around three axes (painting, sculpture, photography), the collection mixes major postwar French names (Pierre Soulages, Pierre Alechinsky, Olivier Debré, Zao Wou-Ki) with younger contemporary artists (Jean-Michel Othoniel, Laurent Grasso, Fahamu Pecou), and the bank describes it as "widely shared" through employee and public access, loans, off-site exhibitions, and school/education partnerships [secondary: [collection.societegenerale.com](https://collection.societegenerale.com/en/about); [Musée des Beaux-Arts de Lyon](https://www.mba-lyon.fr/fr/exposition-dossier-mbalyon/collection-de-la-societe-generale)].

Two findings are worth flagging for the ecosystem picture. First, the collection is legally and financially distinct from the bank's separate philanthropic vehicle, the Société Générale Fondation (which funds grants and sponsorships, per its own "project submission" page) — a structural split between "corporate art collection as an owned operating asset" and "corporate philanthropy as a grant-making foundation" that this sample has not previously had to distinguish this cleanly, since [[ubs-art-collection]] and [[progressive-art-collection]] both hold their collections directly with no parallel foundation entity to compare against, and [[fondation-cartier]] and [[fondazione-prada]] are themselves the philanthropic-foundation model, not a split from one. Second, no source located this run connects the bank's own well-documented 2008 crisis (the Jérôme Kerviel unauthorized-trading scandal, a €4.9 billion loss) to any interruption, contraction, or disposal of the art collection — if that absence of evidence holds up under a more thorough search, it would be a sixth data point (after JPMorgan Chase's four mergers and Microsoft's sustained layoffs) for Pattern 9's claim that corporate-collection continuity tracks the parent's *balance-sheet survival*, not merely its exposure to a serious institutional crisis; Kerviel's losses were absorbed without triggering Société Générale's collapse, unlike Lehman or Enron, so this case (if the absence holds) would sit on the "survived a real crisis, because the parent itself survived" side of that pattern rather than testing it against a case where the parent failed outright.
