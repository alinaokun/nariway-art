## Narrative
Victor and Sally Ganz built one of the most focused and art-historically significant private collections of the 20th century from a modest textile-manufacturing income, not old wealth — buying Picasso directly and in depth (multiple works from single series, including his 1955 *Femmes d'Alger* variations and *Le Rêve*) alongside an equally serious run of Johns, Stella, Rauschenberg, and Hesse. It is the closest thing this sample has to a "before" picture for the entire market-intelligence "estate rush" thesis: no foundation was ever created, no museum relationship was cultivated during the collectors' lifetimes to the point of an intact-gift plan, and the collection's eventual fate was decided entirely by the next generation, under the concrete pressure of estate tax, in the same year the surviving spouse died. The result was not a fire-sale discount — Christie's exceeded its estimate by roughly 65% — but it was still the market resolving a governance vacuum by default, exactly the mechanism [[what-we-now-believe]]'s "sell wins by default, not by design" belief describes, twenty-nine years before that belief was written. Worth flagging for the schema: `outcome_category: closed-dispersed` and `durability_signal: failed` correctly describe this as institutional non-survival, but they read oddly applied to a case that was never intended to become an institution and where the sale itself was, by ordinary market standards, a triumph — this is a case where "failure to survive" and "financial success" are simultaneously true and the current vocabulary cannot hold both without a narrative footnote.
