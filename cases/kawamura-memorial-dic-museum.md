## Narrative (sourced)

Katsumi Kawamura, the second president of what became DIC Corporation — one of the world's largest manufacturers of pigments, inks, and resins — began collecting 20th-century art personally and on the company's behalf in the 1970s, and in 1990 the company opened a dedicated museum on its Sakura City campus, in Chiba Prefecture near Tokyo, designed by architect Ichiro Ebihara. The museum became internationally known chiefly for one room: a complete installation of seven of Mark Rothko's Seagram Murals, one of only four such dedicated Rothko rooms anywhere in the world, alongside Monet, Chagall, Stella, and Picasso works spanning the collection's roughly 754-work total description.

The museum operated for 26 years past Kawamura's 1999 death with no documented governance crisis — a straightforward `survived_founder: yes` case, unremarkable on that axis alone. What makes it distinctive is why it closed. Reporting located this run (The Art Newspaper, September 2024; Art & Object; ArtAsiaPacific) attributes the decision specifically to activist-investor pressure on DIC Corporation's board: the museum's DIC-Corp-owned assets, valued at ¥11.2 billion on a book basis, were judged by newer, more assertive shareholders to be an inefficient use of capital — a framing explicitly contrasted, in Art & Object's own reporting, with "previous shareholders [who] were happy to maintain the museum out of a sense of corporate social responsibility." This is not a funding-gap in the sense this dataset's vocabulary usually captures (an institution running out of money) — DIC Corporation was not in financial distress — but a parent company's capital-allocation logic changing under external shareholder pressure, treating a non-core cultural asset as one more line item subject to the same "capital efficiency" test as any other holding. The museum ceased operating as a public venue on April 1, 2025 (a date pushed back twice from an initial late-January target, reportedly due to a late surge of visitor interest), and DIC Corporation designated roughly 25% of its 384 directly owned works for sale.

The collection was not, however, dispersed outright. The remaining works, including the Rothko Room reconstructed as a dedicated space, are slated to relocate to a new west wing of the International House of Japan in Tokyo, designed by the Pritzker-winning firm SANAA, expected to open in 2030 — a five-year gap during which the collection's public life is suspended entirely rather than continued through loans or a rotating program, distinguishing this case from [[deutsche-bank-collection]]'s immediate pivot to partner-venue rotation. This makes Kawamura a genuine sixth data point on the "building vs. collection" comparison set: like Deutsche Bank, a financially healthy corporate parent gave up a dedicated venue while preserving most of the collection intact; unlike Deutsche Bank, the preservation mechanism is a single future venue rather than an immediate distributed one, and the driving mechanism — shareholder activism targeting a specific asset's balance-sheet efficiency, not a real-estate lease decision or a strategic refocusing narrative — is new to this evidence base. It is a candidate addition to this template's `primary_friction` controlled vocabulary: "activist-investor/capital-efficiency pressure" is a distinct mechanism from `funding-gap` as currently defined, closer in spirit to a hostile-takeover-adjacent pressure than to an institution's own revenue shortfall, and worth flagging to Alina for a possible vocabulary amendment rather than silently coding it into an existing value that doesn't quite fit.
