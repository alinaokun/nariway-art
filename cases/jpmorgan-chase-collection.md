## Narrative
The JPMorgan Chase Art Collection dates to 1959, when David Rockefeller — then president of Chase Manhattan Bank, a decade before his own family's Rockefeller collecting is more widely discussed — established what became a template other corporations later copied: art integrated directly into the architecture and daily workplaces of a major bank, rather than segregated into a dedicated gallery. Today the collection numbers more than 30,000 objects across roughly 450 offices worldwide, spanning modern and contemporary painting, sculpture, works on paper, and photography, with an occasional public-facing dimension through touring exhibitions such as *Collected Visions*, which brought roughly 70 signature works to international venues including Istanbul's Pera Museum and New York's Bronx Museum.

What makes this case useful to the report is less what has happened to it than what *hasn't*: unlike Lehman Brothers and Enron, whose collections were liquidated within months of their parent companies' bankruptcies, and unlike Deutsche Bank, which is voluntarily retreating from a dedicated public venue in 2026, the JPMorgan Chase collection has continued through at least four major corporate transitions of its own parent institution — the 1996 Chase–Chemical Bank merger, the 2000 Chase–J.P. Morgan merger that created the present entity, the 2004 Bank One acquisition, and the 2008 crisis-era acquisitions of Bear Stearns and Washington Mutual assets — without, in any source found this session, being dispersed, paused, or restructured as a program. This suggests a mechanism the sample hadn't yet isolated: **M&A alone does not kill a corporate collection the way outright parent bankruptcy does** — Chase's collection absorbed multiple acquiring/acquired transitions and kept growing, while Lehman's and Enron's died with their parent's Chapter 11 filing. Whether this generalizes (Credit Suisse's collection, absorbed into UBS in the 2023 emergency merger, is the sample's live comparison case, not yet coded) is a genuine open question the report should test as more corporate cases are added.

One flagged and important caveat: [[candidate-universe]] §8 describes this collection as "active + deaccessioning," citing that JPMorgan "began selling parts in the 2020s." **This session's research could not corroborate that claim** — no auction record, press report, or JPMorgan statement describing a 2020s deaccessioning was found, despite a dedicated search. One historical secondary source instead states works are occasionally *given* to museums but "rarely sold." This is treated the same way the First Republic Bank Collection candidate-universe claim was treated (report-dataset.md Batch 3 note): **not coded as fact**, flagged here as an unconfirmed premise in the candidate pool that should be corrected or sourced before it appears in any report claim.
**Re-narrowed 2026-08-18 (WebSearch only, WebFetch still blocked):** a second, independent search pass again surfaced no 2020s sale, auction, or deaccession record for this collection, and again turned up the same "rarely sold, works occasionally given to museums" characterization (attributed to a former senior JPMorgan Chase curator, via myartbroker.com's bank-collections survey) — a second source pointing the same direction, not a new source contradicting it. This still does not prove a negative; absence of a discoverable sale record is not the same as confirmation none occurred. The claim remains unconfirmed, not refuted, and stays out of any coded field.
