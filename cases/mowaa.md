# Museum of West African Art (MOWAA)

One-line: A UK-registered charity conceived by Phillip Ihenacho, designed by Sir David Adjaye, and backed by roughly $25M+ from the British Museum, the German government, the Mellon Foundation, and other institutional donors, set out to build a major new museum in Benin City to help house Benin Bronzes repatriated to Nigeria — and, despite that institutional and financial backing, has had its public opening postponed indefinitely since a November 2025 protest disrupted a preview event and Edo State's government revoked the project's land rights, in an unresolved dispute over the traditional monarch's own claimed authority over the same heritage.

**Why in the sample:** closes the research-program's own flagged "abandoned/changed museum plans" gap in Africa, and — more importantly — is a genuinely different kind of case for this dataset. Every prior founder-museum failure ([[terra]], [[di-rosa]]) traces to a governance vacuum or funding exhaustion inside the institution itself. MOWAA has neither: it has professional, multi-institutional, arguably better-than-average governance (a dedicated charity board, named international institutional funders, a marquee architect) and, per public reporting, adequate funding for its construction phase. It has stalled anyway, for a reason no case in this dataset has shown before — contested authority over the underlying cultural patrimony and land, asserted by a party (the Oba of Benin, a traditional monarch with no board seat) entirely outside the project's own governance structure. A direct disconfirmation-relevant test of H6/H8's implicit assumption that professional, independent governance is sufficient for durability (see Part D below and [[what-we-now-believe]]).

## Narrative
Phillip Ihenacho conceived the Museum of West African Art in Benin City as a purpose-built home, designed by Sir David Adjaye, for Benin Bronzes being repatriated to Nigeria after more than a century in Western museum collections, alongside a wider contemporary West African art program. The project was launched around 2020 under a dedicated UK-registered charity, Legacy Restoration Trust, and drew substantial institutional backing rare for a new African museum: the British Museum and the German government helped raise early-stage funds, the Mellon Foundation contributed a $3M grant (2024), and total funds raised reached roughly $25M or more from a mix of international donors and Edo State itself. A pre-opening "creative campus" — research, conservation, and event space — opened to visitors in late 2024, and a full public opening was announced for November 11, 2025. That opening was disrupted when about twenty protesters, some wielding bats, interrupted a preview event; they were asserting the authority of Oba Ewuare II, the Edo people's traditional monarch, over the region's cultural patrimony, reviving a 2023 dispute in which the Oba argued that returned Benin Bronzes should be displayed at the royal palace, not a new independent museum, after Nigeria's federal government had ruled the Oba could decide where repatriated objects are housed. Edo State's government, under a since-changed administration, then revoked the Right of Occupancy previously granted to the project, citing "overriding public interest." As of this run, MOWAA's full public opening remains postponed indefinitely; no resolution to the underlying dispute over who controls the Bronzes and the land has been reported.
