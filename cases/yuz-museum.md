# Yuz Museum Shanghai — succession by a daughter, after an attempted museum-to-museum lock-in failed to close before the founder's death

## Narrative
Budi Tek, an Indonesian-Chinese businessman and one of the most prominent collectors of contemporary Chinese art, founded Yuz Museum Shanghai in 2014 to house a collection he had built over roughly two decades. Diagnosed with pancreatic cancer around 2016, he spent his final years trying to secure the museum's institutional future rather than leaving it to chance: starting in 2018, he pursued a formal joint foundation with the Los Angeles County Museum of Art, explicitly intended to give the Yuz Museum and its collection long-term institutional backing beyond his own lifetime. That structure was never finalized. Press coverage attributes the failure to close it to deteriorating US-China relations during the period and further delays from the COVID-19 pandemic — external forces, not a change of heart or a family disagreement of the kind that sank Terra or complicated Marciano. Tek died in March 2022 at 65, with the LACMA structure still unresolved.

What happened next is, so far, a quiet continuity story rather than a crisis. Tek's daughter, Justine Alexandria Tek, became Director of both Yuz Museum Shanghai and the Yuz Foundation. In 2023 the museum relocated to an entirely new, purpose-built venue in Shanghai's Panlong Tiandi district — itself a significant capital commitment made *after* the founder's death, evidence of continued institutional investment rather than retrenchment. Exhibition programming continued through 2024 and into 2025, including shows organized in partnership with both LACMA and Qatar Museums, suggesting the international relationships Tek cultivated survived him even though the specific joint-foundation structure he wanted did not materialize.

For the report, Yuz Museum earns its place less as a settled data point (survival is not yet testable by the sample's own 5-year rule, and financial durability is entirely unmeasurable given China's disclosure regime) and more as a distinct *type* the sample didn't have yet: a founder who tried, in good faith and years in advance, to build a legal-institutional succession mechanism, was blocked by forces outside his control, and whose museum nonetheless continued via an informal family handoff. It sits between Kreeger's successful architectural pre-commitment and Terra's total absence of planning — a reminder that "did the founder plan for succession" and "did succession actually work" are related but not identical questions, and that international cases can add mechanisms (cross-border joint foundations, geopolitics as a governance risk factor) the US-centric sample doesn't otherwise surface.
