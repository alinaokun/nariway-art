## Narrative
Every prior corporate case in this sample tests one of two clean mechanisms: a parent's bankruptcy forcing full liquidation (Lehman 2008, Enron 2001), or a healthy parent voluntarily narrowing its public footprint while keeping the collection whole (Deutsche Bank), or a parent absorbing repeated ownership-changing M&A with no effect on the collection at all (JPMorgan Chase). Credit Suisse is the cell the report's own pattern-tracking flagged as missing: a parent that was **distressed but not bankrupt** — solvent enough to be sold rather than liquidated in court, but only because Swiss authorities brokered an emergency, forced sale to UBS in March 2023 to prevent a bankruptcy — a genuinely intermediate case between "parent fails outright" and "parent merely changes strategy or ownership voluntarily."

The result reads as neither pure survival nor pure failure. The ~13,500-work Credit Suisse collection was not liquidated at auction and not donated away; it was absorbed wholesale into UBS's own, larger (~40,000-work) corporate collection, and UBS is now working through a multi-year integration process rather than a single disposition event. The friction reported is not financial distress but **collection-incoherence at the point of merger**: Credit Suisse's Swiss-artist-heavy holding (Hodler, Vallotton) sits awkwardly against UBS's globally-curated, blue-chip contemporary collection, the two banks' inventory systems didn't match (different software, largely German-language records with incomplete valuation and location data on the Credit Suisse side), and the absorbed collection carries additional non-fine-art legacy material (decorative posters, model ships) inherited from Credit Suisse's own 1980s acquisition of First Boston — a reminder that corporate collections accumulate in M&A layers over decades, the same way this report's cases show family collections accumulating across marriages and generations.

The disposition mechanism reported — internal sales to employees at discount, proceeds to charity — is itself a governance value with no clean vocabulary in the current schema: neither a museum deaccession, nor an auction-house sale, nor a donor-directed gift, but an internal-market liquidation mechanism specific to large corporate employers. This is a fourth disposition shape for the corporate sub-sample, alongside forced-bankruptcy-auction (Lehman, Enron), voluntary-retreat-loan-model (Deutsche Bank), and unaffected-continuity (JPMorgan Chase) — worth tracking as a named fifth corporate case only, not yet a rate.
