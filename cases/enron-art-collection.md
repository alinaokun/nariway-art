## Narrative
Enron's art collection is the earliest corporate-bankruptcy dispersal identified in the sample so far (2001–2003), predating Lehman Brothers' by seven years, and it is a useful contrast case precisely because the collecting itself was so recent and so clearly discretionary. Rather than a decades-deep corporate holding like UBS's or Deutsche Bank's, the Enron collection was assembled only in the final few years before the company's collapse, under the direction of Lee Fastow — wife of CFO Andrew Fastow, one of the executives later convicted in the accounting fraud — to decorate Enron's downtown Houston headquarters. It included six-figure sculptures by artists such as Donald Judd, Claes Oldenburg, and Martin Puryear.

Enron filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection on December 2, 2001, at the time one of the largest corporate bankruptcies in U.S. history, with the company owing tens of billions of dollars. As with Lehman later, the art collection became a creditor asset to be liquidated alongside far more mundane corporate property (office furniture, computers, construction equipment), sold off across multiple auctions in 2002–2003. The results were reportedly disappointing relative to Enron's own purchase prices: one work that sold for $405,500 at auction (with commission) had cost the company $574,500 just two years before, a loss press coverage treated as emblematic of the broader liquidation's poor timing — selling into a market shaped by the company's own scandal and a soft economy, rather than at a moment of the sellers' choosing.

For the report, Enron sharpens rather than merely duplicates the Lehman pattern: it shows the same "parent-entity balance-sheet failure liquidates an unrelated cultural asset" mechanism operating on a much smaller, newer, more clearly speculative/decorative collection, and it adds a second, distinct data point on outcome — proceeds well below purchase cost — that Lehman's case (which sold above pre-sale estimates at Sotheby's) does not show. Two corporate-bankruptcy cases is still not a rate, but it begins to separate "how much the collection was worth going in" and "market timing at forced sale" as variables independent of the parent's failure itself.
