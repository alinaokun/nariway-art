## Narrative
Reader's Digest supplies the sample's cleanest example yet of a mechanism its own corporate-case pattern-tracking had predicted should exist but had not yet found: **a healthy-enough (not insolvent) parent voluntarily monetizes a legacy art asset for cash, years before any crisis forces the issue.** This is meaningfully different from every prior corporate case. Lehman Brothers and Enron were both liquidated *through* the bankruptcy process itself, the sale mechanism *was* the crisis. Deutsche Bank's 2026 PalaisPopulaire closure keeps its collection fully intact while narrowing only the dedicated public venue. JPMorgan Chase and UBS ([[jpmorgan-chase-collection]], [[ubs-art-collection]]) show unaffected continuity through repeated M&A. Credit Suisse→UBS shows a distressed-parent absorption. Reader's Digest is none of these: the company sold **part** of its collection in 1998 to fund a struggling but still-operating core business, then continued operating for **another eleven years** before its first bankruptcy filing (2009), and a second filing four years after that (2013). The art sale reads, on this timeline, as an early, proactive balance-sheet lever pulled well before the company's eventual collapse, not a symptom of the collapse itself, a fifth, genuinely distinct corporate disposition mechanism: **anticipatory monetization**, alongside forced liquidation, voluntary retreat, unaffected continuity, and distressed absorption.

The case also reopens, in corporate form, a question this sample has mostly treated as a nonprofit-governance issue: **what happens to the part of a collection that isn't the headline sale?** The 1998 Sotheby's sale accounted for at most 39 of a reported ~8,000-piece total collection, well under 1%. No source found this run describes what became of the other ~7,960-plus works, whether they were quietly sold off in later, smaller, unreported transactions as the company's fortunes worsened through the 2000s, distributed in the two subsequent bankruptcies, or are still hanging in whatever remains of Reader's Digest's corporate offices today. This is a substantially larger and less-explained "missing collection" gap than [[fisher-landau]]'s already-flagged ~700-1,000-work gap, and unlike Fisher Landau (a single, traceable nonprofit foundation), Reader's Digest's ownership structure changed hands repeatedly through two bankruptcies and multiple private-equity owners since 1998, making the trail materially harder to reconstruct without a dedicated archival pass.

## Gaps / contradictions
- **The overwhelming majority of the collection (>99% by piece count) has an entirely unknown disposition** — this is the largest unresolved "missing collection" gap in the sample to date, larger than [[fisher-landau]]'s comparable gap, and it is a corporate case, so there is no probate or foundation-filing trail of the kind that might eventually resolve a nonprofit case's version of this problem.
- **Exact lot count contested** (39 consigned vs. 37 sold vs. "37 pieces" cited as the sale total in one source) — treated here as consistent (39 offered, 37 sold, matching a typical buy-in rate), not a true contradiction, but not independently confirmed against Sotheby's own sale catalogue.
- **No governance/decision-maker identified** — unlike Fondazione Prada or even the bank cases (which at least name a Global Head of Art), no individual or committee is named in any source found as the specific decision-maker behind the 1998 sale; it reads as an ordinary corporate-management decision with no individual attached in the public record.
- **Relationship between the 1998 sale and the 2009/2013 bankruptcies is inferred, not sourced** — no source found this run explicitly frames the 1998 sale as a *precursor* to the later bankruptcies; that connection (both are downstream of the same multi-decade circulation/advertising decline) is this case file's own synthesis, not a claim made in any single source, and should be labeled as such wherever it is used.
