# Hillwood Estate, Museum & Gardens

One-line: Marjorie Merriweather Post (1887–1973) first bequeathed her Washington estate and a $10M endowment to the Smithsonian, which returned it as under-funded; her foundation reopened Hillwood as a house museum in 1977 — endowment later boosted by the Mar-a-Lago sale — now among the best-capitalized founder museums in the sample (~$390M net assets).

## Narrative

Marjorie Merriweather Post — heiress to the Postum/General Foods fortune — built her Washington estate Hillwood in the 1950s to display the largest and most comprehensive assemblage of Russian imperial art outside Russia together with important 18th-century French decorative arts [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillwood_Estate,_Museum_%26_Gardens]. In 1962 she arranged to bequeath the estate plus a $10M endowment to the Smithsonian Institution, but the endowment's ~$450K annual yield proved inadequate to run the site, and the Smithsonian returned Hillwood [source: https://siarchives.si.edu/blog/toasting-museum-never-was]. After her death in 1973, the property and most of the collection passed to the Marjorie Merriweather Post Foundation, which reopened Hillwood to the public as a house museum in 1977 [source: https://siarchives.si.edu/collections/siris_sic_939]. The endowment was later augmented by the 1981 sale of Post's Mar-a-Lago estate and has been reported to reach roughly $200M [source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillwood_Estate,_Museum_%26_Gardens]. That capitalization is confirmed at the balance-sheet level: the operating charity reported FY2024 net assets of $389.7M against $16.1M of expenses — a net-assets-to-opex ratio near 24x (an upper bound including land, mansion, and gardens), with revenue of $20.8M exceeding expenses [source: https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/526080752]. Hillwood is a clean instance of survive-the-founder: opened four years after Post's death and durable across successive directorships for nearly half a century. The initial Smithsonian failure is the instructive branch — a museum bequest can be refused when its endowment cannot carry the operating burden, which is why funding-gap, not taste or governance, is the coded primary friction here.
