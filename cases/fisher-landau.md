## The collector and the collection
Emily Fisher Landau began collecting in the late 1960s, reportedly starting with an Alexander Calder mobile and works from a Josef Albers show at Pace Gallery, funded in part by an insurance payout after a jewelry theft from her home; she built a Modernist group (Picasso, Matisse, Mondrian, Rothko, Klein, Klee, Nevelson) before shifting, from the late 1970s, toward the postwar and contemporary American artists (Jasper Johns, Willem de Kooning, Robert Rauschenberg, Ed Ruscha, Cy Twombly, Andy Warhol) that became the collection's core [source: search snippet citing https://www.sothebys.com/en/articles/emily-fisher-landau-a-life-in-art; confidence: secondary]. She opened the Fisher Landau Center for Art in 1991 in a converted 25,000-square-foot former parachute factory in Long Island City, Queens, open to the public by appointment and later on a regular schedule; the collection is variously reported at "over 1,200 works" and "1,500 works" spanning roughly 1960 to the 2000s — the two figures are contested and not resolved here [source: search snippets citing Wikipedia/flcart.org; confidence: secondary].

## The transition(s)
Three distinct, separately dated events make up this case, and treating them as one "transition" would misstate the record:
1. **2010 — lifetime gift to the Whitney.** Fisher Landau, a longtime Whitney trustee, pledged 367 works (some sources say 419) by nearly 100 artists to the Whitney Museum of American Art, announced May 7, 2010 by director Adam Weinberg; the gift was valued at $50–75 million and toured in the exhibition *Legacy: The Emily Fisher Landau Collection* (opened 2011) [source: https://whitney.org/press/emily-fisher-landau (via search snippet); confidence: secondary].
2. **2017 — closure of the Center.** The Fisher Landau Center for Art closed to the public on November 20, 2017, while Fisher Landau was still living (she was roughly 96). No source found states a reason for the closure — age, cost, or a deliberate decision to consolidate the collection are all plausible but unconfirmed; this is coded `unknown`, not inferred.
3. **2023 — posthumous auction.** Fisher Landau died March 27, 2023. Christie's and Sotheby's competed for her estate's consignment; Sotheby's won, and *The Emily Fisher Landau Collection: An Era Defined* sold across an evening sale (31 lots, $406.4M, headlined by Picasso's *Femme à la montre* at $139.4M) and a day sale (80 lots, $18.3M) on November 8–9, 2023, for a combined **$424.7 million** — reported as the most valuable auction ever devoted to a woman collector [source: https://www.sothebys.com/en/articles/historic-auctions-see-emily-fisher-landau-collection-reach-425-million and https://news.artnet.com/market/emily-fisher-landau-sothebys-results-2391881; confidence: secondary].

The arithmetic is worth stating plainly: **~111 auction lots generated roughly 5–8x the dollar value of the ~367–419-work Whitney gift**, even though the gift was larger by piece count. The fate of the remaining ~700–1,000+ works in a collection reported at 1,200–1,500 pieces (i.e., what was neither in the 2010 gift nor in the 2023 sale) is not documented in any source found — a significant, named gap below.

## Why this case matters for the report
Fisher Landau is a useful counter-case to Terra precisely because the *institutional* failure mode (a single-collector museum that does not outlive its 1990s founding generation) looks similar, but the *governance* story is different in kind, not just in tone. Terra's collapse ran through public litigation between a board and the founder's widow after his death. Fisher Landau's closure ran through a quiet, undocumented decision by the founder herself, while she was alive and fully in control, followed six years later by an estate/auction-house process after her death that the record does not show being pre-committed to any institution. If `succession_locked_before_founder_death` and `donor_intent_instrument` resolve toward "no locked plan" once the will is obtained, this case would support the report's thesis that governance — specifically, whether disposition is locked in *before* death — predicts outcome independent of whether the founder controlled the institution alone (which she clearly did, successfully, for 26 years). If a will or bylaws surface a coherent standing plan, the case would need re-coding toward a more orderly picture.
