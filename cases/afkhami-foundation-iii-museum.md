## Narrative (sourced)

Mohammed Afkhami, a Dubai-based real-estate and commodities executive (vice chairman, London Strategic Land; founder, MA Partners DMCC), began collecting Iranian modern and contemporary art in 2004 after a trip to Iran introduced him to modernist painters including Sirak Melkonian and Masoud Arabshahi [secondary: Artsy]. His collection grew to encompass roughly 140 Iranian artists spanning the late 1950s to the present, housed across five combined units on the 50th floor of a Dubai high-rise [secondary: The National]. Roughly a decade into collecting, Afkhami launched the Mohammed Afkhami Foundation to fund scholarship, publications, and exhibitions connected to Iranian and Middle Eastern art, including scholarships for regional students [secondary: Cultured Magazine, 2021]. In spring 2023 the foundation launched its most distinctive public-access mechanism yet: the iii Museum (iii.art), a free virtual museum built with adaptive digital architecture — lighting, layout, and spatial arrangement that respond in real time to the viewer — designed specifically to present Iranian art to a global audience without the constraints (sanctions-complicated international loans, the political sensitivity of physical Iranian-art exhibitions in some markets) that would attend a conventional bricks-and-mortar museum. The project won a Red Dot Design Award, with initial coverage noting phase one made roughly 15% of the collection viewable [secondary: Red Dot; The One Club]. Unlike every other `no-building` case already coded in this dataset — which use touring exhibitions, loans, or a host venue as their access mechanism — the iii Museum is not a supplement to a physical space that exists or once existed; it is, per available sourcing, the collection's primary and currently only public-facing venue. We flag this as a genuinely new pattern for the evidence base to track going forward, not yet common enough to justify its own controlled-vocabulary value, but worth watching as digital exhibition technology matures and diaspora or politically-constrained collections in particular may find it a lower-friction alternative to a physical institution.
