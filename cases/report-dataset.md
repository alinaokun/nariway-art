## Preliminary patterns (44 of 50 cases coded, PROVISIONAL)
Updated 2026-08-15 (fourth run). Coverage is still 44 — this run's work was reconciling 20 already-coded cases into this synthesis for the first time (see Reconciliation batch below), not adding new ones, and that reconciliation changes several patterns below more than any single new case would have. **Not conclusions.** The set is hand-selected for variation, not random, so no rates are published off it; these are hypotheses sharpened enough to test across all 50. Data gaps are named, not hidden.

1. **Governance, not money, decides whether a collection survives its founder — and the mechanism can be architectural or legal, and can lock in decades before the building even exists.** Among the deceased-founder cases, survival is the norm, and the failures locate the cause. Terra had roughly $450M behind it and still failed, its governance was **founder-sole** (the only original directors were the founder and his son), so his death was a control vacuum that became litigation and closure. The survivors consistently had **independent boards and succession set before death**: McNay's self-perpetuating board, Grounds For Sculpture's board that predated the founder's death by 20 years, the de Menils, Phillipses, and Havemeyer Webbs all handing to professional successors. **[[kreeger-museum]] added a variant**: its succession was locked in not through a will or board charter but through the building itself — Philip Johnson designed the 1968 house explicitly to convert into a public museum, a physical pre-commitment made 22 years before the founder's 1990 death. **[[fondation-beyeler]] now extends the pattern further**: its governing foundation (a Swiss Stiftung) was established in 1982 — 28 years before Ernst Beyeler's death, and 15 years *before the museum building itself opened* in 1997. That's the earliest and most complete pre-death lock-in the sample has found, and it comes with a distinct twist: Beyeler had no children, so unlike the family-handoff survivors, the succession ran directly to an independent, non-family board (chaired by industrialist Hansjörg Wyss) with no family-continuity option ever on the table. Emerging read, sharpened: **governance design (legal or physical) locked in before death predicts survival better than endowment size or family continuity — the lock-in can precede the institution's own building, and can bypass family entirely.** Test at 50, and check how many other house-museums in the sample (McNay, Phillips, Weisman) had a comparable physical pre-commitment. **This run's reconciliation adds two more independent-board survivors and one genuine complication.** [[storm-king]] is the cleanest multi-generation version yet: Ralph Ogden founded it in 1960, died in 1974, and it passed to his son-in-law H. Peter Stern, then (2008) to Stern's own son John — three consecutive family successions, 66 years, still thriving, the longest continuous family-succession chain in the sample. [[pyramid-hill]] is the inverse test: Harry Wilks installed **no family successor at all** — a founder-sole decision-owner with nobody groomed to follow — yet after his 2014 death the board simply ran a national search and hired an outside executive director in 2019, and the institution survived anyway. Read together with Terra (founder-sole *and* no independent board *and* failed), Pyramid Hill suggests the operative variable isn't family continuity, and isn't even founder-sole decision-making during life — it's specifically whether an **independent board with hiring authority already existed** at the moment of death, family successor or not. **The complication**: [[chinati]] is coded `governance_control_at_founding: founder-sole` — the same value as Terra — and it has thrived for 32 years since Judd's 1994 death, not failed. Chinati and Terra cannot both be explained by "founder-sole governance fails" alone; the difference more likely sits in *what* founder-sole control was exercised over. Judd's permanence doctrine (works installed as fixed, unrotatable ensembles) removed most of the ongoing discretionary decisions a successor board could fight over, while Terra's board faced live, contestable choices (deaccession, location, program) with no settled precedent. Worth testing at 50: does founder-sole governance only predict failure when paired with *unresolved* future discretion, not when the founder's design pre-decides the hard questions?

2. **Two failure modes, on two clocks — plus a corporate-sector clock that now splits into three sub-mechanisms, one of which isn't failure at all, and a fourth that isn't even disruption.** Governance failure kills fast (Terra, on the founder's death). Funding failure kills slow (Corcoran dissolved after 126 years as its endowment eroded below ~0.5x operating coverage; di Rosa distressed and now selling its campus). [[lehman-brothers-collection]] added a third clock — a corporate parent's balance-sheet failure, liquidating an otherwise well-run art program overnight regardless of the collection's own health — and **[[enron-art-collection]] confirmed that clock with a second, independent case** (2001–02 vs. Lehman's 2008–10), while adding a variable Lehman alone couldn't show: timing and cost basis at forced sale (Enron's flagship lot sold at a documented loss against its own purchase price; Lehman's sold above estimate). **[[deutsche-bank-collection]] added a fourth, distinct shape that the report should not fold into "failure" at all**: a financially healthy corporate parent voluntarily retreating from a dedicated public venue while explicitly keeping the collection intact. **[[jpmorgan-chase-collection]] now adds a fifth shape that isn't disruption at all**: a corporate collection that has continued, apparently uninterrupted, through *four separate M&A events of its own parent* (1996, 2000, 2004, 2008) without dispersal or pause — suggesting the operative variable isn't "did the parent change hands" but specifically "did the parent's balance sheet fail" (Lehman, Enron) versus merely change ownership or strategy (JPMorgan Chase's mergers, Deutsche Bank's voluntary pivot). Institutional mortality (and near-mortality) in the corporate sub-sample is now at least three distinct mechanisms — forced liquidation (bankruptcy), voluntary retreat (healthy parent narrows footprint), and unaffected continuity (healthy or even repeatedly-acquired parent, no impact on the collection) — the report should track corporate-collection cases (§8 of [[candidate-universe]]) as their own category with this three-way split, not fold them into the museum/foundation funding-gap story. (Credit Suisse → UBS, still uncoded, is the natural next test: an M&A absorption of a *distressed*, not bankrupt, parent — a fourth cell the sample doesn't have yet.)

3. **The eponymous museum is losing share to lighter models, especially among living founders.** The set spans multiple pathways, and the newer/living-founder cases cluster on the alternatives: the Broad's lending library, the Cisneros and Daskalopoulos gift-and-co-ownership dispersals, the Fisher 100-year loan, the Neue Galerie merger, the traveling Kinsey. Consistent with the wealth-transfer thesis, the generation now deciding reaches for models that avoid a standalone institution's cost and fragility. Real shift or selection artifact is a 50-case question. **[[vogel-collection]] shows the same "lighter model" instinct can be a *founding* choice rather than a later pivot, and for a reason that has nothing to do with 21st-century institutional fatigue**: the Vogels never had the wealth to build a standalone museum in the first place (funded entirely from a postal clerk's salary), and total intentional dispersal into 51 existing institutions was their only realistic option — a useful reminder that "avoids a standalone institution's cost" isn't only a strategic choice available to the wealthy; for the sample's poorest founders it's closer to the *only* option.

4. **A data-availability finding the report can own — and it now splits into three distinct shapes of "unknown," not one.** The durability metric everyone wants, endowment coverage, is largely **unmeasurable from public data**: the true endowment (IRS Schedule D) is `unknown` for most cases, the headline net-assets ratios are all upper bounds inflated by real property, and they are meaningless for grantmaking/lending vehicles (Broad ~109x, Longleaf ~111x are non-signals). How rarely a collection's durability can be verified from the outside is itself a finding, and a reason collectors cannot benchmark themselves. This run's batch sharpens the finding into three genuinely different categories that were previously collapsed together: **(a) blocked by tooling** — 15 of 44 coded cases (see Primary-verification backlog, top of file) carry no primary-sourced figures at all because this session's WebFetch tool has been blocked by network egress policy across four separate research runs (2026-08-13, 2026-08-14 first run, 2026-08-14 second run, 2026-08-15 third run — this run's test specifically tried a non-aggregator third-party PDF host, foundationsearch.com, hoping a direct-linked 990-PF PDF might route around the block; it did not, confirming this is a blanket session-level restriction, not a domain- or content-type-specific one); a 990 filing or sale record exists and would resolve the figure cleanly, it just couldn't be pulled directly. **(b) unmeasurable in principle** — [[yuz-museum]]'s finances are `unknown` not because a document is unreached but because China has no 990-equivalent public-disclosure regime for private foundations; no re-fetch will ever resolve this one. **(c) not yet created** — [[benini]]'s Form 990 is `unknown` because the Benini Foundation only received 501(c)(3) status in June 2025 and likely has no first filing yet; this `unknown` will resolve with time, not with better tooling. The report should keep these three apart: only (a) is a fixable artifact of this session's network restrictions; (b) and (c) are honest, durable findings about the field itself.

5. **The living-founder cohort is the report's forward-looking core.** A large share (Glenstone, Crystal Bridges, Magazzino, Bechtler, Audain, Thoma) code as survival **not-yet-testable**, these are exactly the significant collections about to enter the transition the report is about. Analyze survival on the deceased-founder set; frame the living-founder set as the coming decisions, tying the historical data straight to the wealth-transfer moment.

6. **The `decision_owner` and governance sub-fields undercount civic and corporate origination stories.** [[michener-art-museum]] (founded by Bucks County government and an existing arts nonprofit, with the namesake as principal donor rather than founding collector) and [[lehman-brothers-collection]] (disposition decided by a bankruptcy estate/creditors' committee, not any person or board) both resist the current vocabulary, which assumes a collector, family, or museum/foundation board sits behind every transition. [[enron-art-collection]] and [[deutsche-bank-collection]] both hit the same wall in the prior run, and **[[jpmorgan-chase-collection]] now makes it five corporate cases with no clean `decision_owner` fit**, still not a rate, but a confirmed and recurring *type* of gap that spans every corporate case coded so far, crisis or not. The report should either name this as an explicit schema limitation or extend the vocabulary (`civic-institutional`, `bankruptcy-estate`, and `corporate-executive` values, at minimum) before the 50-case set is used to characterize "who decides." **[[de-la-cruz-collection]] adds a sharper point on the existing `spouse` value**: the sample's other spouse-decision case, [[weisman]], is a spouse *institutionalizing* a continuation (widow as successor-director inside a standing foundation); De la Cruz is a spouse choosing *immediate, discretionary liquidation* with no institutional intermediary at all. Same coded value, opposite outcome — worth flagging before `decision_owner` distributions get charted, since the field currently can't distinguish "spouse who continues the institution" from "spouse who winds it down."

7. **"Succession locked before death" is not binary — an attempted-but-blocked mechanism is a distinct third case, and the sample's first international case shows why it matters.** [[yuz-museum]] (Shanghai) is the first case where a founder actively pursued a formal succession instrument years in advance — a joint foundation with LACMA, from 2018 — and had it blocked by forces entirely outside his own planning (geopolitics, pandemic timing), yet the institution continued anyway via informal family succession. That sits between Kreeger's *completed* architectural lock-in and Terra's total absence of any attempt; coding it as a flat `unknown` would flatten a real and interesting distinction. Going international at all also surfaces a disclosure-regime gap the US-heavy sample hadn't hit yet: China has no 990-equivalent, so financial durability is unmeasurable there in principle, not just unpulled (see pattern 4c). [[fondation-beyeler]] adds a second, cleaner international disclosure case: Switzerland sits somewhere between the US regime (searchable, if this session's tooling could reach it) and China's (no equivalent at all) — a Swiss foundation's financials exist and are presumably filed with a supervisory authority, but are not searchable/aggregated the way a US 990 is. Three international cases now show three different disclosure regimes; the report's data-availability finding should eventually map degrees of opacity by jurisdiction, not just US-vs-not-US.

8. **NEW: governance can substitute completely for endowment — the sample's zero-endowment case still reads as maximally durable.** Every other case in the sample's durability discussion runs through some size of endowment or net-assets ratio, even when flagged as an upper bound. [[vogel-collection]] has **no endowment at all, ever** — the entire "capital structure" was a postal worker's salary — and yet its disposition mechanism (total, deliberate, pre-death distribution into 51 established institutions, 1992–2009) is arguably the most durable in the whole dataset by construction: no single point of failure, no balance sheet to erode, nothing dependent on any one board or foundation's future decisions. This is the sharpest available test yet of the report's central claim that governance design predicts durability better than endowment size — Vogel is the $0-endowment extreme, and it still reads as durable as, or more durable than, cases with endowments in the hundreds of millions. One case; the mechanism (dispersal into existing, independently-endowed institutions rather than a single new entity) generalizes conceptually to Souls Grown Deep and the Pearlman Foundation's forced-circulation mandate, both already in the sample — worth an explicit cross-case comparison once dispersal-pathway cases reach a testable count.

9. **NEW: for corporate collections specifically, surviving the parent's M&A is not the same test as surviving the parent's bankruptcy.** [[jpmorgan-chase-collection]] is the sample's first corporate case with no crisis at all — 65+ years of continuous operation through at least four ownership-changing events for its parent bank (1996, 2000, 2004, 2008), apparently without the collection itself ever being paused or dispersed. Read against Lehman and Enron (both destroyed by their own parent's bankruptcy) and Deutsche Bank (a healthy parent voluntarily narrowing its public footprint while keeping the collection intact), the emerging read is that **the operative variable for corporate-collection survival is whether the parent's balance sheet actually fails, not whether the parent changes hands or strategy**. This reframes pattern 2's corporate sub-typology as a three-way split (forced liquidation / voluntary retreat / unaffected continuity) rather than the two-way failure-mode framing that works for the founder-death cases. Flagged, one caveat: a claim in [[candidate-universe]] that JPMorgan "began selling parts in the 2020s" could not be corroborated this run and is not treated as fact — see [[jpmorgan-chase-collection]] Narrative.

10. **NEW (reconciliation finding): the net-assets-to-opex ratio can measure the wrong entity entirely, and this bites hardest exactly where a founder deliberately split "who owns the art" from "who runs the public program."** [[kinsey]] is the clearest case: the collection is owned and toured by the family's for-profit-adjacent KBK Enterprises, while the public 990 belongs only to a small educational foundation (FY2024 net assets $649K, opex $354K) — a 1.83x ratio that describes almost nothing about the actual enterprise's durability. [[thoma]] shows the same trap from the opposite direction: two same-family entities exist (a Santa Fe art foundation and a separate Chicago foundation), the ~$1.2B net-assets figure pulled this run sits on one EIN, and until the collection-holding entity is confirmed that figure cannot responsibly be coded as this case's durability signal — it is held `unknown` rather than risk attributing a $1.2B cushion to the wrong balance sheet. Every private-operating-foundation case in the reconciled batch ([[broad]], [[glenstone]], [[magazzino]], [[longleaf]], [[bechtler]], [[thoma]]) shares a milder version of this problem: because a 990-PF operating foundation typically carries its own art collection at book value, its net-assets-to-opex ratio is a *softer* upper bound than a public-charity museum's, not just an equally-soft one — the [[claims-register]] C4 caveat should distinguish 990 (public charity, art often not on the balance sheet at market) from 990-PF (private foundation, art often is). Worth a dedicated methodology note before the 50-case charts ship, since it would otherwise silently overstate durability for exactly the best-capitalized, most family-controlled cases in the sample.

11. **NEW: the university-partnership pathway's first governance-conflict case.** Every university-partnership case coded before this run (Michener) resisted the `decision_owner` vocabulary but was not distressed. [[ogden]] is different: Roger Ogden's 1999 gift to the University of New Orleans produced a thriving regional museum, but state budget cuts to UNO strained the affiliation badly enough that Ogden **sued the UNO Foundation** to move ownership of the art to the museum's own nonprofit — a university partnership turning adversarial over who actually controls the collection, a friction type (`governance-conflict`, specifically donor-vs-host-institution rather than founder-vs-board) the pathway hadn't shown yet. Net assets also *fell* sharply, FY2021's $9.78M to FY2024's $5.68M, an unexplained decline that blocks a firm durability read. One case, but it reframes "hand the collection to an existing institution" as carrying its own distinct failure mode — loss of control to the host — distinct from the funding-gap and governance-vacuum clocks pattern 2 already tracks.

12. **NEW: a taxonomy-boundary case worth naming rather than quietly coding around.** [[onera]] is included specifically because it stresses the schema: David B. Peterson's Onera Foundation is, by its own history, an architectural-preservation advocacy and grantmaking body (the Onera Prize at Columbia predates any public venue) that acquired a landmarked 1836 house and only in October 2025 began showing loan/commission exhibitions in it — there is no owned art collection at all, and `collection_size` is correctly coded `n/a`, not `unknown`. It also could not be located on ProPublica under any name tried, so unlike the 15-case WebFetch-blocked backlog, its financial opacity may be structural (a 990-N filer below the disclosure threshold, or registered under an unrecognized legal name) rather than a tooling failure. As the 50-case set fills in, the report should decide explicitly whether "collection" needs to mean *owned art* to qualify a case for inclusion, or whether stewarding a landmarked building as a public exhibition platform counts — Onera is the test the schema hasn't had to answer yet.

13. **NEW: a fourth national disclosure regime, cleaner than expected.** [[audain]] is the sample's first Canadian case, and it revealed a disclosure gap distinct from the three the sample already tracks (US 990 — searchable; Swiss foundation supervisory filing — exists but not aggregated; Chinese — no equivalent at all). Canada's CRA T3010 is a real, public, per-charity filing (comparable in principle to a US 990) that simply was not pulled this run — a structural cousin of the WebFetch-blocked backlog, but on a different regulator and portal than every other item in it. The museum's own FY2023 figures (expenses ~CAD $8.7M, revenue ~CAD $40.8M) came through a third-party aggregator (charitydata.ca) rather than CRA directly, and the revenue figure is almost certainly a one-time gift inflating it more than 4x above expenses — a reminder that a "primary-adjacent" aggregator figure still needs the disaggregation a raw filing would show plainly.

### First real signal (pilot complete, 9 cases + McNay)
The **survived_founder field is doing exactly the work the report needs**, it separates failure modes that a crude "closed = failed" would flatten:
- **Terra** = did **not** survive the founder (governance vacuum on his death → litigation → closure). Friction: governance.
- **Corcoran** = survived 126 years, then **dissolved on money** (endowment coverage fell below ~0.5x). Friction: funding.

Emerging hypothesis to test at scale: **governance design at founding (independent board + succession plan) predicts survival better than collection value or endowment size** — Terra had ~$450M behind it and still failed on governance; the durable ones had board continuity locked before the founder's death. **New pattern this batch: the corporate-failure clock generalizes, but severity varies with what was bought and when.** Enron is the sample's second corporate-bankruptcy dispersal (after Lehman) and confirms the basic mechanism — a parent's balance-sheet collapse liquidates an unrelated art asset regardless of the collection's own merits — but adds a variable Lehman's case didn't show: **timing and cost basis at forced sale.** Lehman's Sotheby's lot sold *above* pre-sale estimate in 2010; Enron's flagship lot sold for a documented ~30% loss against its own purchase price in 2002, in a market shaped by the seller's own scandal. Two cases is still not a rate, but it now separates "did the parent fail" from "how much value survived the forced sale" as a second, independent axis worth tracking as more corporate cases are coded.

**New pattern this batch: a corporate collection can retreat from public visibility without any financial distress at all — a fourth failure-adjacent mode, but not a failure.** Deutsche Bank's PalaisPopulaire closure is the first corporate case in the sample where the parent is financially healthy and the collection is explicitly *not* dispersed — only the dedicated public venue is. This is mechanistically the same "building stopped paying for itself, mission/collection continues" pattern the report already logs for CAM Raleigh and the Rauschenberg Foundation HQ ([[candidate-universe]] Signals rows), now confirmed at large corporate scale. It sharpens rather than contradicts pattern 2 below (two failure clocks): this is neither clock, a reminder that `outcome_category: pivoted` needs to stay analytically distinct from `distressed`/`dissolved` even when the surface story ("closing a space") sounds similar.

**New pattern this batch: three distinct shapes of "unknown," worth naming explicitly before the 50-case pass locks the schema.** This batch's cases surface unknowns that are not interchangeable: (a) **unmeasurable in principle** — Yuz Museum's finances, where no 990-equivalent disclosure regime exists in China at all; (b) **not yet created** — Benini's Form 990, where the foundation was only granted 501(c)(3) status in June 2025 and a first filing likely doesn't exist yet; (c) **blocked by this session's tooling** — the great majority of the backlog, where a primary document (990, sale results, institution page) exists and would resolve the figure, but WebFetch could not reach it this run. The report's existing data-availability finding (pattern 4, below) currently treats these as one undifferentiated category; splitting them out is more honest and more useful to a reader trying to judge whether a gap is fixable.

**New pattern this batch: an attempted-but-uncompleted succession lock is a distinct governance outcome, not a subtype of "no plan."** Yuz Museum is the first case where a founder actively pursued a formal institutional succession mechanism (the LACMA joint foundation) years before death and had it blocked by forces outside his control (geopolitics, pandemic timing), yet the institution still continued via informal family succession. This sits on a spectrum with Kreeger (mechanism completed, just architectural rather than legal) and Terra (no mechanism ever attempted) — the `succession_locked_before_founder_death` field currently reads as binary yes/no/unknown, but "attempted and blocked" is analytically different from both an unattempted plan and a simple unknown, and the report should consider a fourth coded value once more international cases test whether this is common.

**Art-park pathway gap closed.** Before this batch, every art-park case in the sample (Chinati, Pyramid Hill, Longleaf) was a survival or successful-pivot story. Benini is the first art-park case where the *original site* did not continue — filling a real gap in the outcomes-by-pathway chart — though it is a uniquely low-stakes example of discontinuity: founder-driven, while both founders remain alive, with a successor institution already built and now formally incorporated. It cannot yet answer whether art parks fail differently than built museums; it only proves the pathway can end without death, litigation, or documented funding collapse.
