## Narrative

Arthur Andersen's UK arm began acquiring British art in 1968 to decorate its offices, building over the following decades into one of the earlier and better-known examples of a UK professional-services firm running a genuine corporate art collection — the ICAEW's own historical-resources materials describe it as among the first of its kind in the country. In 2002, the worldwide Arthur Andersen partnership collapsed within months following the Enron accounting scandal and the firm's criminal obstruction-of-justice conviction (later overturned, too late to save the firm). Andersen's national practices split apart and were absorbed piecemeal by rival firms; the European Commission cleared Deloitte & Touche's takeover of Andersen's UK business that August. The art collection passed to Deloitte as part of that absorption, continuing to hang in the merged firm's UK offices with no reported interruption or loss.

The collection's eventual end had nothing to do with the collapse that brought it to Deloitte in the first place. By the mid-2000s Deloitte had moved into new open-plan offices — a workplace-design trend that left no walls suited to displaying traditional framed paintings — and in January 2008 the firm consigned the full 448-work, 40-year-old collection to auction (Lyon & Turnbull, at the Royal Academy), estimated at £750,000 to £1,000,000.

This is a useful addition to this dataset's corporate-collection sub-sample for a reason distinct from every other coded corporate case: it separates two failure modes this dataset has sometimes conflated. A collection can survive a genuinely catastrophic *parent-firm* collapse (Andersen's implosion was far more sudden and complete than any bank merger or bankruptcy already in this sample) via absorption into an acquirer, and still be dispersed years later for a reason with no connection to that collapse at all — an ordinary real-estate and workplace-design decision. Set against [[lehman-brothers-collection]] and [[enron-art-collection]] (where the collection's dispersal *was* the direct, immediate consequence of the firm's own failure), Arthur Andersen shows that "the firm that built the collection ceased to exist" and "the collection was eventually sold" are not the same event, and treating them as one would overstate how much a parent firm's collapse, by itself, explains a corporate collection's fate.
