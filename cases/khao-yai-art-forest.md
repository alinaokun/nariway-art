## Narrative
Khao Yai Art Forest gives this sample its first genuinely Southeast Asian case, and it sharpens the land-art/art-park comparison the candidate universe flagged it for in a specific way: unlike [[chinati]] (Judd's own single-artist permanence doctrine, now under independent-board governance since his 1994 death) and [[storm-king]] (a three-generation family-succession foundation, 66 years old), Khao Yai is barely two years old, privately owned rather than foundation-incorporated in any source found this run, and directed by a single living founder with no board, foundation, or succession mechanism of any kind documented. It is, in effect, this sample's youngest and least institutionalized art-park case — closer in stage, though not in region or wealth scale, to [[muzej-lah]]'s pre-institutionalization phase (a personally-funded, founder-sole project not yet wrapped in any confirmed legal/governance structure) than to Chinati's or Storm King's mature foundation models. That makes it a useful marker for where the `found-art-park` pathway's governance question (does the site outlive the founder's personal ownership, the same test that determined Terra's failure and Chinati's/Storm King's survival) has not yet had the chance to be answered, rather than a case where it has been answered badly.

The disclosure-regime question this case raises is sharper than a simple "unpulled figure" gap, and worth stating precisely because the case-template treats structural unavailability as a first-class finding rather than an exception. Thailand does have a real foundation-registration and annual-financial-reporting framework (Civil and Commercial Code §§110–136, administered by the Ministry of Interior), a regime that in principle resembles Canada's CRA T3010 ([[audain]]) more than China's total absence of any equivalent ([[yuz-museum]]) — foundations that register under it are required to file. But no source found this run establishes that Khao Yai Art Forest is itself registered as a Thai foundation at all; every account (ArtNews, Designboom, Monocle, ArtReview) describes the project as personally owned land, personally funded, with a landscape-design collaborator (Marco Pansera) but no independent legal entity. If that reading is correct, Khao Yai sits in a genuinely new fifth position for this sample's growing disclosure-regime map (after US 990, Swiss foundation-supervisory, Chinese no-equivalent, Canadian CRA T3010, and the corporate-sponsorship-line regime shared by [[fondazione-prada]] and [[fondation-cartier]]): **a real disclosure regime exists in the jurisdiction, but the specific case has apparently not (yet) opted into it by incorporating as a foundation at all** — a sixth distinct shape of "why the number is unknown," distinct from all five the sample has documented so far, and one the report should track explicitly rather than folding into the Thai-regime question generally, since the gap here is about this entity's legal form, not the country's transparency laws.

Khao Yai also extends Pattern 8's zero-endowment durability question from a different angle than [[vogel-collection]]. Vogel's zero-endowment case reads as maximally durable *by construction* (total, deliberate dispersal into 51 independently-endowed institutions, removing any single point of failure). Khao Yai's absence of a formal endowment or foundation reads the opposite way: durability here depends entirely on one wealthy family's continued willingness and ability to fund an open-air site indefinitely, with no independent capital base, no board, and — as far as this run's sources show — no legal entity separate from the founder's personal ownership. This is closer to Fondazione Prada's and Fondation Cartier's "ongoing sponsorship, no independent capital" architecture than to Vogel's, except with an individual family rather than a company as the funding source, and with no institutional layer (foundation, board, or public-charity registration) yet interposed between the founder's personal wealth and the site's survival at all. Whether that changes as the project matures — and whether it ever incorporates formally under Thailand's own foundation-law framework — is the single most important open question for this case's future coding.
