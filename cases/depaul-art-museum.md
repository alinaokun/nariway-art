# DePaul Art Museum

One-line: A university teaching collection begun in 1972 by DePaul University's Women's Board became a public art museum in 1985; 41 years later, facing a $27.4M university-wide budget deficit, DePaul closed the museum's public operations (June 2026) while keeping its ~4,000-object collection on campus, accessible by appointment for research and coursework only.

*(Public projection fields are in the frontmatter above, per [[case-template]] — the single machine source the export reads. The prose below is the internal research record.)*

**Why in the sample:** a genuinely different failure shape from this dataset's usual founder-collector cases — there is no individual founder and no founder death to test survival against. It is a live 2026 instance of an institutional host retrenching from a *public-facing* art museum while explicitly *not* dispersing or selling the underlying collection, closer in mechanism to [[cam-raleigh]]'s and [[rauschenberg-foundation-hq]]'s "building vs. collection" comparison set than to a founder-succession case, but with a university's own general-fund crisis (not the art program's own finances) as the proximate cause. A clean, fresh instance of the H6-adjacent "money kills slowly, even where governance is fine" pattern, this time inside an institutional (not founder-controlled) host.

## Narrative (sourced)

DePaul University's Women's Board launched an "Art for DePaul Drive" in 1972 to mark the university's upcoming 75th anniversary, assembling an initial ~67 etchings, watercolors, drawings, and paintings; the collection grew steadily through gifts (including, later, a WPA-era print collection from Belverd and Marian Needles and 114 works by contemporary Chicago artists from collector Chuck Thurow in 2016) until it was formalized as a dedicated public museum in 1985 [secondary: DePaul Art Museum exhibition history, search-synthesized]. On February 26, 2026, DePaul announced the museum would close, a decision folded into a university-wide plan to cut $27.4M in spending amid a projected 2026 budget deficit driven by falling international graduate enrollment, rising financial-aid need, and higher benefits costs — a plan that had already produced 114 staff layoffs the previous December [secondary: WBEZ]. The museum itself was inexpensive to run (~$745,000/year, of which the university covered ~$420,000), and its closure drew organized community pushback: an open letter from students and faculty gathered more than 3,000 signatures within days [secondary: Hyperallergic]. The closure proceeded on schedule; public operations ended June 2026. Critically, DePaul did not disperse, sell, or transfer the ~4,000-object collection — the university committed publicly to "maintain and steward the art collection as part of our academic and research offerings," keeping it in place at the museum's Lincoln Park building, now accessible by appointment for coursework and research rather than general public visitation [secondary: WBEZ, Chicago Sun-Times, Axios Chicago]. This is a genuinely different mechanism from this dataset's usual founder-museum failures (Terra's governance vacuum, Corcoran's financial exhaustion): the pressure came from the *host institution's* general finances, not the art program's own economics or governance, and the resolution was a downgrade of public access rather than a legal dissolution or an asset sale.
