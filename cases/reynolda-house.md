## Narrative
Katharine Smith Reynolds and her husband R.J. Reynolds, founder of the R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Company, built Reynolda as their family estate in 1917 — a working farm and residence, not a museum [secondary: Wikipedia, "Reynolda House Museum of American Art"]. Decades later, in the mid-1960s, their son-in-law Charles Babcock reimagined the property as "a center for the arts," and appointed his daughter Barbara Babcock Millhouse — the Reynoldses' granddaughter — founding president of a newly formed board, Reynolda House Inc., established in 1965 [secondary: Wake Forest Magazine, "Reynolda at 100"]. Millhouse became, by every account located this run, the driving force behind the actual art collection: she personally selected works she believed would be accepted as iconic examples of American art by future generations, building a holding art historian John Wilmerding called "the finest concentration of American art in a public collection south of Washington, D.C." [secondary]. Reynolda House Museum of American Art formally opened in 1967, two years after the board's founding, and Millhouse continued to steward the institution — restoring the historic house, adding an education and exhibition wing, and eventually negotiating the museum's 2018 affiliation with Wake Forest University — across roughly four decades as board president (1965-2004) and beyond [secondary].

For this dataset, Reynolda is a useful structural variation on the house-museum pathway already represented by McNay, Kreeger, and Hillwood: in each of those cases, the person who lived in the house and assembled the collection is also the museum's founder in a single act, typically formalized through their own will. At Reynolda, the house predates the museum by nearly half a century, and the founder is a third-generation family member who chose to convert an inherited property into a public institution and then build a collection specifically for that purpose — a `decision_owner: children`(-generation) case distinct from every founder-alone or spouse-decision case already coded. It also offers a data point on long single-person board tenure inside an otherwise independent, multi-member board structure: Millhouse held the board presidency for nearly 40 years, longer than any founder-successor tenure in this dataset outside of a founder's own lifetime, without the case collapsing into a founder-sole pattern — the board itself, not her personal control, was Reynolda's founding legal structure throughout.
