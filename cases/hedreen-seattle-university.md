# The Hedreen Collection at Seattle University — a purpose-built university gift, promised but not yet transferred

*Coded to [[case-template]]. WebFetch confirmed `EGRESS_BLOCKED` this run against www.seattleu.edu (one direct attempt made, per the task brief, then abandoned rather than retried); all figures below are WebSearch-snippet synthesis, tagged `secondary` throughout, drawing on Seattle University's own newsroom (via search snippets, not direct fetch), The Seattle Times, The Art Newspaper, ARTnews, Artsy, Artnet News, the Chronicle of Philanthropy, Capitol Hill Seattle News, The Seattle Spectator, Archpaper, Wikipedia, and the Hedreens' obituary record. One-line: Seattle real-estate developer Richard C. Hedreen pledged his and his late wife Betty's 200-plus-work, six-century art collection (publicly valued ~$300M) plus $25M in construction seed money to Seattle University in March 2024, to fund a new purpose-built teaching museum (SUMA); as of this coding (Aug 2026) the museum is not yet built, the art has not yet legally transferred, and transfer is explicitly conditioned on the building's completion — planned for fall 2028.

## Narrative (sourced)

Richard Hedreen built R.C. Hedreen Co. into one of Seattle's major hotel-and-tower development firms — the Seattle Hilton, the Grand Hyatt, the Hyatt Regency Seattle, and other downtown towers among its projects — after founding it in 1963 following a University of Washington civil-engineering degree and early work as a contractor's estimator [secondary: peoplepill/Wikipedia synthesis]. He met Betty Ann Petri while she was an undergraduate at Seattle University; they married in 1957 and, over roughly six decades, assembled a collection running from 15th-century Old Masters (Titian, Pontormo, Lievens, Vigée Le Brun, Gainsborough, Meléndez) through postwar and contemporary American and international art (de Kooning, Rauschenberg, Lichtenstein, Indiana, Picasso, Johns, Warhol, Kiefer, Cecily Brown, Rashid Johnson, Amy Sherald) — more than 200 works publicly valued around $300M and repeatedly described in the press as among the most finely curated private collections in the United States [secondary: Seattle Times; ARTnews; Artsy]. The Hedreens were already major, decades-long donors to Seattle University well before this gift: they funded the Steven Holl-designed Chapel of St. Ignatius (1997) and co-chaired the campaign that built the campus's Lee Center for the Arts [secondary: The Art Newspaper]. Betty died on January 29, 2022, at 85; Richard has said he decided afterward to give the entire collection to her alma mater in her honor, wanting to keep it together and on public view rather than see it dispersed, sold, or held in storage [secondary: Seattle Times; Artsy].

Seattle University announced the pledge on March 13, 2024: the full collection (~$300M) plus $25M in seed funding toward a new purpose-built museum, described by the university as the largest gift of art ever made to a US college and the largest gift of any kind in the school's 133-year history [secondary: multiple, converging]. Crucially — and this is the fact that most distinguishes this case from its two nearest comparators in this sample — **the art has not yet legally transferred**: coverage of the announcement states plainly that the collection "will be transferred to the university once the museum is built and ready to receive it," making this a promised, deferred gift rather than an executed one [secondary: Spokesman-Review/AP wire pickup]. The university selected architect Tom Kundig of Olson Kundig in spring 2025 for a corner-site building at the campus's 12th Avenue entrance, describing it as a "teaching museum" [secondary: Archpaper; Olson Kundig]. Realizing that site required demolishing the Lee Center for the Arts — the very building the Hedreens themselves had helped fund roughly two decades earlier — which produced this case's one clearly documented friction: theater faculty and students, who learned of the decision without being consulted, organized public opposition through 2025 and into 2026 over the loss of a 20-year-old performance venue and the logistics of a relocated theater program, with Capitol Hill neighbors and Cornish College of the Arts community members joining public meetings on the project [secondary: Capitol Hill Seattle News, Feb/Apr/Jul 2025; The Spectator, Apr 2025 & Feb 2026]. Groundbreaking is anticipated August 2026 (this month, at the time of this coding) with the Lee Center's final production staged in May 2026; the museum is planned to open before fall-2028 classes begin, at which point the collection is expected to transfer [secondary, consistent across sources].

Set against this sample's other two university-partnership cases, Hedreen resembles [[anderson-collection-stanford]] far more than [[ogden]] in *shape*: like Anderson, this is a purpose-built, free-standing building on university land, governed (as best this run can tell, absent a public incorporation announcement) as a university institutional unit reporting to the Board of Trustees rather than folded into an existing university museum system the way Ogden was folded into a University of New Orleans affiliation. But Hedreen introduces a genuinely new wrinkle neither prior case carries: **the gift is not yet executed**. Anderson's founders pledged and the museum opened within three years, while both founders were alive; Ogden's 1999 gift transferred art at the time of the founding agreement. Hedreen's structure explicitly conditions legal transfer of the art on the building's completion — a founder-controlled deferral mechanism (H8) that keeps leverage with the 90-plus-year-old donor for as long as construction takes, and that this sample has not yet coded elsewhere in the university-partnership family. Whether that deferred-transfer structure is itself the seed of a third sub-form — "pledge now, deliver on completion" — or simply a timing artifact of this being caught mid-construction, is the open question this case adds to H6.
