# The Milton Resnick and Pat Passlof Foundation

One-line: two married Abstract Expressionist painters who shared a former Lower East Side synagogue as home and studio for over 40 years left their estate to a foundation that restored the building and reopened it, free, as a public exhibition space in 2018 — a joint-couple artist-estate foundation, distinct from the single-artist-estate shape of [[nancy-graves-foundation]] or [[mark-rothko-foundation]].

*(Public projection fields are in the frontmatter above, per [[case-template]]. The prose below is the internal research record.)*

**Why coded now:** flagged alongside [[nancy-graves-foundation]] by Alina's 2026-09 artist-legacy field scan ([[candidate-universe]] "Artist-endowed foundation candidates") — the building hosts CPAL's own conference, so this is a textbook artist-endowed-foundation transition Alina will encounter directly. It adds a genuinely new variant this dataset had not yet coded: a **jointly-held, two-artist estate** (both spouses were working painters, not a collector-and-artist pairing) converting into one foundation, with an 11-14 year gap between the first spouse's death (2004) and the foundation's actual creation (2015), then a further 3 years to public opening (2018) — a slow, deliberate timeline distinct from the faster artist-foundation cases already in the sample (Chinati, Beyeler).

## Narrative (sourced)

Milton Resnick (1917-2004) and Pat Passlof (1928-2011), both Abstract Expressionist painters, moved into 87 Eldridge Street — a building that had served as a synagogue before 1963 — and Resnick converted its "grand but dilapidated two-story sanctuary" into his painting studio; the couple lived and worked there together until their deaths [secondary: [Archpaper](https://www.archpaper.com/2018/09/milton-resnick-retrospective/)]. Unlike most artist-foundation cases in this dataset, neither spouse created the foundation personally during their lifetime: it was the couple's estate that established The Milton Resnick and Pat Passlof Foundation in 2015, eleven years after Resnick's death and four after Passlof's — a materially slower and more deliberate timeline than founder-driven cases like Chinati (created by Judd himself, seven years pre-death) or Fondation Beyeler (created by the Beyelers themselves). The foundation then spent roughly three more years stabilizing the historic building and converting it into public-facing gallery space, reopening in 2018 to coincide with the centennial of Resnick's birth, with rotating exhibitions plus lectures and readings continuing since [secondary: [resnickpasslof.org](https://www.resnickpasslof.org/)]. Governance sits with a small named board (Kernan, Dorfman, Cherry) and a professional Executive Director (Reynolds), an independent-board shape with no located family control — closer to Nancy Graves's artist-designed-but-independent board than to a family-dominated foundation. No financial detail (assets, expenses, an EIN) was locatable via WebSearch this run, a real gap distinct from Nancy Graves's case, where aggregator sites at least reported approximate figures; this foundation appears small enough, or recent enough in its current form, that it has not yet accumulated the same aggregator footprint.
