# Artizon Museum (formerly Bridgestone Museum of Art, Ishibashi Foundation)

One-line: A tire-company founder placed his personal art collection into an independent private foundation two decades before his death, and that foundation - not the company - has run the museum continuously for 70+ years, surviving his death, the foundation's own multi-generational family succession, and a full 2020 rebuild and rebranding, all while remaining legally and financially separate from Bridgestone Corporation itself.

*(Public projection fields are in the frontmatter above, per [[case-template]]. The prose below is the internal research record.)*

**Why in the sample:** the corporate-adjacent origin (H9's scope note explicitly includes collections built from corporate-founder wealth as a tagged sub-population) plus a governance shape distinct from every other corporate case in this dataset — [[wesfarmers-art-collection]] and [[td-bank-art-collection]] are collections the company itself still owns and governs; here the founder deliberately *separated* the collection from the company decades before his death, into a family-controlled but institutionally independent foundation. That separation is arguably why this collection was never at risk from Bridgestone Corporation's own ordinary corporate risks (leadership change, a merger, a strategy pivot) the way [[unicredit-art-collection]] and [[credit-suisse-ubs-collection]] were.

## Narrative (sourced)

Ishibashi Shojiro (1889-1976), founder of the Bridgestone tire company, opened a gallery on the second floor of the Bridgestone Building in Kyobashi, Tokyo, to show his personal art collection to the public; the Bridgestone Museum of Art opened January 8, 1952 [source: artizon.museum, History]. In 1956, Ishibashi established the Ishibashi Foundation "with the goal of making a lasting contribution to art and culture," giving the foundation the bulk of his collection and entrusting it with the museum's management [source: ishibashi-foundation.or.jp] — a deliberate act of separating the collection's future from the company's, two decades before his 1976 death. The foundation ran the museum without interruption through his death and for decades afterward; after roughly ten years of planning, the museum closed, was entirely rebuilt, and reopened in January 2020 under a new name, the Artizon Museum, continuing "the DNA of the Bridgestone Museum of Art" under the same foundation [source: artizon.museum]. Current foundation leadership is a family member (Hiroshi Ishibashi), and the Ishibashi family remains a major Bridgestone Corporation shareholder without holding an executive role at the company; Bridgestone itself operates as an ordinary public company with no single controlling shareholder [source: Forbes profile; corporate-governance summaries]. The result is a collection whose durability has never depended on the tire company's own business fortunes, because the founder structurally decoupled the two well before any succession question arose.
