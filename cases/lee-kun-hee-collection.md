# Lee Kun-hee Collection → National Museum of Korea / MMCA

One-line: The 23,181-work personal collection of Samsung chairman Lee Kun-hee — built across two generations of the Lee family, spanning Korean antiquities to Monet, Picasso, Chagall, and Dalí — was donated in full to South Korea's national museums in 2021 by his heirs, alongside (not in exchange for) a separately paid $10.8 billion cash inheritance tax bill, the largest in the country's history; the donation is credited with driving a subsequent 2023 change to Korean tax law that now lets art be used to pay inheritance tax directly.

It also feeds this run's Part D disconfirmation hunt directly (see [[what-we-now-believe]]): the tax reform this case's own precedent helped trigger is a genuine test of H7A's "no one owns the pre-decision" claim.

## Narrative (sourced)

Lee Kun-hee, Samsung's chairman from 1987 until his death, inherited both the company and a family collecting habit reportedly begun by his father, Samsung founder Lee Byung-chul, and expanded it over decades into one of the largest private art collections in the world: 23,181 works spanning Korean antiquities, calligraphy, and ceramics through Western Impressionist and modern masterworks by Monet, Picasso, Chagall, and Dalí, plus more than 100 Picasso ceramic pieces [secondary: CNN, ARTnews]. When he died in October 2020, his estate — reported at roughly 26 trillion won ($17.7 billion) — triggered an estimated 12 trillion won (~$10.8 billion) inheritance tax bill under South Korea's steep, up-to-60% inheritance tax regime, believed to be the largest such tax bill in the country's history [secondary: Reuters via multiple outlets, UPI]. In April 2021 the family announced it would pay the full cash tax bill over five years — a payment schedule confirmed completed by 2026 [secondary: UPI] — while separately donating the entire art collection to South Korea's national museums: roughly 1,500 works, primarily Korean modernism plus a smaller group of Western masterworks, to the National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art (MMCA), and more than 20,000 items, mostly antiquities and books, to the National Museum of Korea (NMK) [secondary: Artnet News]. The family separately pledged 1 trillion won (~$900M) in cash toward a new infectious-disease hospital, framed explicitly alongside the art gift as an image-repair gesture after years of Samsung succession controversy, including Lee Jae-yong's own bribery conviction [secondary: KED Global, ARTnews]. Importantly, the donation was **not** a direct in-kind tax payment under Korean law as it stood in 2021 — it functioned as a separate charitable gift that reduced the taxable estate, while the tax itself was paid in cash [secondary, synthesized]. That distinction matters because the Lee Kun-hee case, alongside the Kansong Art Museum's 2020 forced auction of two treasures to cover its own inheritance tax bill, is widely credited with the political momentum behind a subsequent Korean tax law reform: since 2022/2023, Korean law has allowed beneficiaries to pay inheritance tax directly with donated cultural artifacts or art (provided the tax due exceeds 20 million won and exceeds the inherited financial assets), adjudicated by a seven-expert appraisal panel under the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism — the first such in-kind payment approved in January 2024 [secondary: Korea Times]. A dedicated single-site museum to house the full Lee Kun-hee Collection is under construction in Seoul's Songhyeon-dong district, near Gyeongbok Palace, targeted to open by 2028 [secondary: Korea Times]; MMCA opened a dedicated wing for its share of the collection in 2024 [secondary].

- Whether any additional works were distributed to regional Korean museums beyond MMCA/NMK (some early reporting suggested this) is not confirmed this run.
- No US-style Form 990 or comparable financial disclosure exists for this transfer — a structural, not tooling, gap, consistent with several other non-US cases in this dataset.
