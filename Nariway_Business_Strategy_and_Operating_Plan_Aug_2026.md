## How to use this document

Treat this document as the authoritative strategy for Nariway as a company.

Do not try to reconcile this model with prior versions of Nariway centered on a research institute, future-of-work research, exhibitions, books, general collection legacy advisory, museum creation, or a software-as-a-service product. Some research assets and ideas from earlier work remain useful, but the company described here is a different and much more specific professional-services business.

The current model is:

> **Nariway is a specialist service used by estate attorneys and fiduciaries to control and document the resolution of significant art collections.**

Nariway is not being built as SaaS. Technology is an internal operating advantage that allows a small founder-led firm to deliver work that feels as organized, rigorous, responsive, and institutional as work from a much larger firm.

The company is built around a simple premise:

> **Nariway creates a controlled, evidence-based process for fiduciaries making decisions inside an opaque art market.**

Operationally, Nariway makes information:

> **Complete. Comparable. Controlled. Documented. Reconcilable.**

And its independence is explicit:

> **Nariway is paid only by the estate or fiduciary for its professional services. Nariway does not receive auction commissions, dealer commissions, referral fees, or a percentage of sale proceeds.**

# 1. Executive summary

Nariway is a founder-led specialist professional-services firm focused on a narrow but growing problem created by the transfer of privately held art.

When a significant private collection enters an estate, the executor, trustee, or other fiduciary becomes responsible for resolving assets that do not behave like ordinary financial property. Individual works may be physically dispersed. Ownership records may be incomplete. Several types of value may exist for the same work. Qualified appraisers, auction houses, dealers, art advisors, conservators, attorneys, tax professionals, beneficiaries, shippers, storage facilities, museums, and nonprofit recipients may all contribute information or participate in the process.

Each specialist may do excellent work. The problem is that no single specialist necessarily owns the fiduciary-side control record across the full disposition process.

Nariway fills that gap.

It does **not** determine whether art is important, attractive, authentic, historically significant, or marketable. It does **not** provide legal, tax, appraisal, authentication, provenance, conservation, or curatorial opinions. It does **not** replace estate counsel or art specialists. It does **not** take custody of artworks.

Instead, Nariway creates the structured decision environment around the specialists.

For each significant work or group of works, Nariway helps the fiduciary and estate counsel understand:

- what is in scope;
- who owns it;
- where it is;
- what supporting documentation exists;
- which values have been supplied and for what purpose;
- which experts supplied those values or opinions;
- what disposition alternatives have been received;
- how competing proposals compare on a common financial basis;
- what beneficiary considerations or unresolved issues exist;
- what decision the fiduciary made;
- what evidence supports the decision;
- whether execution occurred;
- whether settlement and cash or transfer records reconcile; and
- whether the art workstream is actually closed.

Nariway's value is therefore not art judgment. Its value is **control, comparability, documentation, exception management, and reconciliation**.

The firm will initially sell three levels of service:

1. **Collection Disposition Review** — a $5,000 fixed-fee, tightly scoped entry engagement.
2. **Managed Collection Resolution** — a larger project engagement, initially expected to fall roughly in the $30,000-$60,000 range depending on complexity.
3. **Complex Collection Resolution Office** — an ongoing retained engagement for large or complicated collections, potentially $75,000-$150,000+ over the life of a matter.

These are pricing hypotheses to test, not permanent published market rates. The $5,000 service is intentionally more productized and may be appropriate to publish on the website.

Nariway's primary referral and client channel will be trusts-and-estates attorneys who handle estate administration, followed by professional fiduciaries, bank trust departments, trust companies, family offices, and other professional advisors. The Estate Planning Council and broader NAEPC network are especially attractive because they already bring together attorneys, CPAs, trust officers, financial professionals, and other estate-planning specialists.

The firm should remain small, founder-led, and supported by independent specialists and contractors rather than a large employee base. The internal technology environment should automate document ingestion, extraction, normalization, exception detection, calculations, reporting, and source linking while keeping human review and professional boundaries intact.

# 2. The strategic shift

## What Nariway was becoming

Earlier versions of Nariway explored several adjacent ideas:

- private collection to public legacy advisory;
- helping collectors decide whether to create museums, foundations, long-term loans, gifts, or other public structures;
- research into the life cycle of private collections;
- collection transition case studies;
- a donation marketplace or routing platform;
- a software product for attorneys and fiduciaries;
- a broader collection-resolution platform;
- cultural research and publishing.

Those explorations were useful because they surfaced the larger structural problem: a major transfer of privately held art is coming, and much of the work involved in transition is fragmented across professional specialties.

They also exposed an important founder constraint.

Nariway should **not** depend on the founder being better than art historians, appraisers, curators, dealers, auction specialists, or lifelong art advisors at judging the quality, importance, authenticity, or market prospects of art.

That would place the founder in a permanent credibility deficit.

## What Nariway is now

The current model deliberately moves the company into a lane where the founder's finance, audit, strategy, and operating background is central rather than incidental.

The founder's job is not to know which painting is better.

The founder's job is to make sure the process around the painting is complete, comparable, supportable, and resolved.

This is closer to a controller, transaction-management, or financial-control role applied to a specialized asset class.

Internally, the audit analogy is powerful:

| Audit or control question | Art-estate equivalent |
|---|---|
| Existence | Does the artwork exist and where is it? |
| Completeness | Have all in-scope works been captured? |
| Rights and obligations | Does the estate or trust own it and have authority over it? |
| Valuation | What value has been supplied, by whom, for what purpose, and as of what date? |
| Classification | Is the number an estate FMV, insurance value, auction estimate, dealer offer, or something else? |
| Authorization | Who approved the disposition? |
| Occurrence | Did the sale, donation, or beneficiary distribution actually happen? |
| Accuracy | Do gross proceeds, transaction costs, net settlement, and cash received reconcile? |
| Cutoff and status | Is the work still an estate asset or has title/custody transferred? |
| Evidence | Where is the source document that supports the field or decision? |

Nariway should not market itself as an audit firm unless legal and professional advice confirms that terminology is appropriate. The analogy is an internal design principle.

# 3. The structural market thesis

## The Great Art Wealth Transfer

The strongest macro tailwind is not art-market appreciation. It is ownership transition.

The 2025 Deloitte Private and ArtTactic Art & Finance Report estimates that approximately **$992 billion in art and collectibles** will transfer over the next decade, equivalent to roughly $100 billion per year.

This figure must be described precisely.

It is:

- global;
- forward-looking over ten years;
- **art and collectibles**, not art alone;
- an ownership-transfer estimate, not a sales forecast;
- derived from an estimated $19.84 trillion UHNW wealth transfer and an assumption that 5% relates to art and collectibles.

The number is useful as evidence of scale, but Nariway should never present it as a guaranteed $992 billion art-sales market.

The important business insight is the underlying event:

> A large body of privately held art and collectibles will change hands because owners age, die, transfer assets, restructure ownership, make gifts, or otherwise transition collections.

That event does not depend on a rising art market.

## Why this is attractive relative to most art businesses

Traditional art businesses are often exposed to transaction volume and price cycles.

Auction houses, dealers, art advisors paid on transactions, and art-investment models generally benefit when works trade and values are strong.

Nariway's demand is different.

A fiduciary still has to administer the collection when:

- art prices are rising;
- art prices are falling;
- auction demand is weak;
- the heirs want the art;
- the heirs do not want the art;
- the estate needs liquidity;
- the estate can afford to wait;
- a museum is interested;
- no museum is interested.

A difficult art market can increase the need for structured decision-making because timing, guarantees, private sales, holding costs, liquidity needs, and net proceeds become harder to compare.

Nariway is therefore built around **administrative inevitability**, not discretionary art consumption.

## The relevant market is not every inherited artwork

Nariway is not intended for every estate containing paintings.

A household with eight decorative works worth $12,000 does not need a specialist control service.

The initial market is the subset of matters where art is financially meaningful, operationally complicated, legally sensitive, or dispute-prone enough that the attorney or fiduciary wants a dedicated process.

Possible qualification indicators include:

- roughly 20-25+ meaningful works;
- aggregate collection value around $1 million-$2 million or more;
- several locations or storage sites;
- multiple ownership entities;
- incomplete or inconsistent records;
- several beneficiaries with competing preferences;
- significant estate-tax or liquidity pressure;
- multiple appraisers or art specialists;
- several auction houses, dealers, or private-sale proposals;
- a mix of sale, beneficiary distribution, donation, and retention;
- material shipping, storage, conservation, or insurance costs;
- large numbers of lower-value works creating a difficult tail;
- a professional executor or trustee who wants a documented process.

These are **initial qualification heuristics**, not rigid eligibility rules.

A $15 million collection containing three obvious blue-chip works may be simpler than a $3 million collection containing 120 heterogeneous works and four beneficiaries.

Complexity, not headline value alone, should determine fit.

# 4. Customer, user, payer, and referral architecture

## Primary customer

The primary customer is:

> **Trusts-and-estates attorneys handling administration of HNW and UHNW estates or trusts containing significant art.**

The strongest prospects are attorneys who actually administer estates, not only attorneys whose work is primarily drafting planning documents.

Ideal attorney profiles include:

- estate-administration partners;
- private-client partners;
- probate and Surrogate's Court practitioners;
- attorneys advising professional executors and trustees;
- art-law attorneys involved in estates;
- lawyers in firms with HNW private-client practices.

## Secondary clients and referral partners

Nariway can also be engaged or referred by:

- professional executors;
- professional trustees;
- private trust companies;
- bank trust departments;
- multi-family offices;
- single-family offices;
- fiduciary accountants;
- CPAs involved in estate administration;
- private banks;
- art attorneys;
- collection managers;
- independent art advisors;
- qualified appraisers.

Some of these professionals can be both referral sources and collaborators.

## Economic payer

The engagement should ordinarily be structured so that Nariway is paid by the estate, trust, or fiduciary client rather than by an auction house, dealer, or other disposition channel.

Whether a particular fee is properly chargeable to the estate, trust, or tax return must always be determined by the responsible attorney or tax professional.

There is strong precedent for matter-specific professional and technology costs within estate administration.

For example:

- the IRS recognizes categories of estate administration expenses such as fiduciary, attorney, accounting, appraisal, management, conservation, maintenance, and certain property-sale expenses;
- Estateably offers per-file pricing and explicitly notes that per-file invoices make pass-through to clients straightforward;
- EstateExec charges per estate and markets that structure to lawyers;
- Heirloom markets automated asset discovery to trusts-and-estates firms and specifically promotes charging its service through to the estate as an administration expense, with the law firm continuing to bill its professional review time.

This validates the **commercial mechanism**, not the tax treatment of Nariway specifically.

## Purchase trigger

The best initial purchase trigger may be:

> **The appraisal is complete. Now the estate has to decide what to do with the art.**

At that point:

- the collection definitely exists;
- the attorney and fiduciary are already engaged;
- the estate knows the art is significant enough to warrant professional attention;
- beneficiary preferences may be clearer;
- market proposals may be arriving;
- disposition decisions are unavoidable;
- transaction costs and conflicts become visible;
- the need for documentation becomes immediate.

The $5,000 Collection Disposition Review should be designed precisely around this moment.

# 5. The problem from the attorney's perspective

The attorney does not need a general lecture about art-market opacity.

The attorney's practical concerns are more immediate:

- "Do we have a reliable record of what is in this collection?"
- "Are we relying on the right appraisal for the right purpose?"
- "Why are there five different values for the same work?"
- "How do these auction proposals actually compare?"
- "What will the estate net after all fees and costs?"
- "Does this expert have an economic interest in the transaction?"
- "Did a beneficiary request this work before we sell it?"
- "Why did the executor choose this sale route?"
- "Where is the document supporting that decision?"
- "Has the work actually shipped?"
- "Did the settlement statement reconcile to the cash received?"
- "Which works remain unresolved?"
- "If a beneficiary challenges this two years from now, what is in our file?"

## The four major pressure points

Research to date suggests four recurring pressure points.

### 1. Establishing control over the collection

The estate must determine:

- what exists;
- where it is;
- what belongs to the estate or trust;
- what records support ownership;
- whether anything is on loan, consignment, or held elsewhere;
- whether everything on the insurance schedule has been physically located;
- whether works have moved after death.

This is time-consuming but partially served by collection-management systems and general estate software.

### 2. Managing valuation inputs

Art creates several legitimate but different forms of value:

- acquisition cost;
- insurance value;
- estate-tax fair market value;
- charitable appraisal value;
- auction estimate;
- dealer offer;
- private-sale target;
- actual sale result.

The problem is not simply "what is the value?" It is making sure each number is classified, sourced, dated, and understood in context.

The IRS specifically requires appraisal information for qualifying art reported on Form 706, reinforcing the need for specialist valuation and supporting documentation.

### 3. Managing beneficiaries

Tangible personal property can create conflict disproportionate to its economic value because emotional and financial interests overlap.

For art, possible issues include:

- competing beneficiary requests;
- unequal values;
- specific bequests;
- proposed equalization payments;
- heirs who want cash rather than art;
- heirs who believe the decedent informally promised them a work;
- objections to sales;
- disputes about timing or venue.

Nariway should help structure the status and evidence without mediating family conflict or providing legal advice.

### 4. Disposition and defensibility

This appears to be the strongest initial product wedge.

When the estate has to sell, distribute, donate, or otherwise resolve art, information becomes fragmented across:

- appraisals;
- auction-house proposals;
- dealer offers;
- private-sale opportunities;
- art-advisor recommendations;
- beneficiary requests;
- shipping quotes;
- storage and conservation costs;
- tax deadlines;
- consignment contracts;
- settlement statements.

The fiduciary needs to act prudently and the attorney needs a file that supports the process.

This is where Nariway can create the greatest value.

That does not mean Nariway guarantees a fiduciary decision is legally defensible. Only counsel can advise on legal sufficiency.

It means Nariway creates a factual, organized, evidence-based record that helps counsel and the fiduciary understand and document the process.

# 8. Service architecture

Nariway should launch with three service levels.

The names are working names and can be refined, but the scope logic should remain.

## Service 1 — Collection Disposition Review

**Working price:** $5,000 flat fee  
**Role:** Productized entry engagement  
**Primary purpose:** Help an attorney and fiduciary make sense of one defined disposition decision after appraisal.

### Ideal matter

The collection has already been inventoried and appraised.

The estate is considering what to do with a specific group of works and has already received, or is about to receive, proposals from auction houses, dealers, private buyers, or other channels.

Examples:

- 18 works the beneficiaries do not want;
- three auction-house proposals for a group of 12 works;
- a mix of auction, private-sale, and donation alternatives;
- an executor who wants a neutral summary before approving a consignment.

### Proposed scope

The $5,000 engagement can include:

- one 60-minute intake meeting;
- review of the existing inventory and qualified appraisal relevant to the defined tranche;
- review of up to three material disposition proposals or offers;
- one local working meeting or site visit when useful and geographically practical, with travel outside the agreed local region billed separately;
- a Value and Expert Record for the defined tranche;
- normalization of proposal terms;
- estimated net-proceeds comparison using documented inputs;
- a conflicts/interests visibility check based on disclosed roles and compensation;
- an exception and missing-information list;
- one fiduciary/attorney decision meeting;
- a concise written Disposition Review suitable for the estate file.

### Possible scope limit

Do **not** define the scope solely by number of works.

Better wording:

> One defined disposition decision or one material tranche of a collection, with up to three principal market proposals.

The engagement letter can also include an assumed work-volume range to prevent abuse.

### Target turnaround

Approximately 10 business days after complete materials are received.

### Explicit exclusions

The $5,000 review does not include:

- physical inventory;
- full collection cataloguing;
- sourcing art appraisers;
- sourcing multiple auction proposals unless separately added;
- art-market recommendation;
- beneficiary mediation;
- legal or tax advice;
- ongoing transaction monitoring;
- shipping coordination;
- settlement reconciliation after sale;
- final collection closeout.

### Why this service matters

This is not a teaser.

It must solve a useful problem completely.

It gives attorneys a low-friction way to try Nariway on a real matter.

It also creates a natural path to a larger engagement if the review reveals that the estate needs ongoing control and execution support.

## Service 2 — Managed Collection Resolution

**Working price range:** $30,000-$60,000  
**Role:** Core service  
**Primary purpose:** Control and document the resolution process from appraisal through final disposition and reconciliation.

### Ideal matter

- 25-150+ meaningful works;
- several beneficiaries;
- multiple disposition channels;
- several experts;
- several auction houses or dealers;
- six to twelve months of active work;
- significant documentation or reconciliation burden.

### Includes

- intake and scope review;
- initial onsite control assessment;
- controlled collection ledger;
- document and evidence organization;
- Value and Expert Record;
- ongoing exception reporting;
- beneficiary/disposition-status tracking;
- proposal normalization;
- financial comparison of documented alternatives;
- fiduciary decision records;
- execution tracking;
- periodic meetings and onsite visits;
- sale, donation, and beneficiary-distribution tracking;
- settlement and transaction-cost reconciliation;
- final Collection Resolution Report.

### Pricing principle

Price by complexity, not by collection value.

Possible drivers:

- number of works;
- number of locations;
- number of owners/entities;
- number of beneficiaries;
- number of experts;
- number of disposition channels;
- number of proposal rounds;
- duration;
- volume and quality of source documents;
- international complexity;
- expected onsite requirement.

## Service 3 — Complex Collection Resolution Office

**Working structure:** Initial control assessment of roughly $15,000-$25,000, then approximately $7,500-$15,000 per active month, or a custom project fee  
**Potential total engagement:** $75,000-$150,000+  
**Role:** External control office for the art workstream

### Ideal matter

- 100-500+ works;
- multiple residences and storage locations;
- multiple trusts, LLCs, or owners;
- several beneficiaries;
- multiple auction houses;
- private sales;
- donation and institutional discussions;
- complex timeline;
- high documentation burden;
- large transaction volume;
- international or multi-jurisdiction issues;
- a professional fiduciary who wants institutional-quality oversight.

### Includes

Everything in Managed Collection Resolution, plus:

- more frequent progress meetings;
- continuous exception reporting;
- recurring onsite presence;
- coordination across multiple tranches;
- management reporting to counsel and fiduciary;
- contractor/analyst support when appropriate;
- expanded transaction reconciliation;
- custom reporting;
- higher-touch closeout.

### What it is not

It is not a percentage-of-assets service.

A $50 million collection does not automatically generate a larger Nariway fee than a $10 million collection if the work is simpler.

# 9. The end-to-end Nariway engagement

The service should feel like a controlled professional engagement rather than informal consulting.

## Phase 1 — Matter intake

The estate attorney or fiduciary contacts Nariway.

Nariway asks operational questions:

- How many works are in scope?
- Where are they?
- Is there an existing inventory?
- Has legal ownership been established?
- Has a date-of-death or other required appraisal been completed?
- Are there specific bequests?
- Have beneficiaries expressed preferences?
- Have auction houses or dealers been contacted?
- Are there tax or liquidity deadlines?
- Are works in storage, on loan, or on consignment?
- Who currently controls physical access?
- What software or records already exist?
- What is the attorney most worried about?

### Founder role

The founder handles the initial call personally.

This is a relationship and judgment point, not an automation point.

### Technology role

Once engaged, the internal system creates:

- matter record;
- collection record;
- document repository;
- artwork or tranche ledger;
- source index;
- exception structure;
- timeline.

## Phase 2 — Initial control review and onsite assessment

Nariway meets the attorney, fiduciary, and appropriate specialists.

The founder may visit:

- a residence;
- storage facility;
- law firm;
- collection office;
- other site.

The purpose is not to authenticate or appraise art.

The purpose is to understand the control environment.

Questions include:

- What is the starting population?
- Is there a reliable inventory?
- Do physical IDs match record IDs?
- Are there works at other locations?
- Is ownership documented?
- What work is already underway?
- What is missing?
- Who has custody?
- Who is authorized to move property?
- Which experts are already engaged?

If a formal physical inventory or cataloguing is needed, a registrar, appraiser, or collection professional should do it.

### Deliverable

**Initial Collection Control Assessment**

This can include:

- scope;
- record-quality assessment;
- ownership/document gaps;
- valuation status;
- known beneficiary issues;
- existing disposition activity;
- time-sensitive matters;
- specialist requirements;
- major exceptions;
- proposed resolution plan.

## Phase 3 — Build the controlled record

Inputs may include:

- inventory spreadsheets;
- appraisals;
- insurance schedules;
- invoices;
- photographs;
- condition reports;
- loan agreements;
- consignment documents;
- estate documents supplied by counsel;
- beneficiary records;
- shipping documents;
- emails and proposals.

The internal technology extracts structured fields and links each field back to source evidence.

### Key rule

**AI is never the evidence.**

Every extracted field that matters should link to a source document, page, email, or other verified record.

Human review is required for material fields.

## Phase 4 — Build the Value and Expert Record

For each material value or opinion, capture:

- provider;
- provider role;
- credential or specialty when supplied;
- purpose;
- effective date;
- value or opinion;
- source document;
- engagement context;
- known economic interest in a proposed transaction.

Example value classifications:

- acquisition cost;
- insurance value;
- estate-tax FMV;
- charitable FMV;
- auction low estimate;
- auction high estimate;
- dealer offer;
- private-sale offer;
- guarantee;
- actual hammer/gross result;
- net settlement.

Nariway does not decide which appraisal is correct.

It makes the distinctions visible.

## Phase 5 — Develop and capture disposition alternatives

Art-market specialists may determine:

- which auction houses should review the work;
- whether private sale is appropriate;
- which works should be grouped;
- whether donation is plausible;
- whether market timing matters.

Nariway captures the resulting alternatives.

Possible channels:

- beneficiary distribution;
- auction;
- private sale;
- dealer sale;
- museum donation;
- university donation;
- hospital/nonprofit donation;
- long-term loan;
- temporary retention;
- other disposition.

Museum Exchange may eventually be a useful downstream partner for qualifying donation matters.

## Phase 6 — Prepare the fiduciary decision package

Nariway organizes the information into a common structure.

For material sale alternatives, comparison fields may include:

- low estimate;
- high estimate;
- guarantee;
- reserve;
- seller commission;
- other seller charges;
- marketing commitment;
- conservation contribution;
- shipping responsibility;
- insurance responsibility;
- sale venue;
- sale date;
- private-sale rights;
- withdrawal terms;
- payment timing;
- expected gross proceeds;
- expected transaction costs;
- estimated net estate proceeds;
- proposal expiration;
- other conditions.

For each field, source evidence should be retrievable.

### Decision rule

Nariway does **not** make the fiduciary's decision.

Preferred record language:

> Nariway compiled the alternatives and their documented terms. The fiduciary, in consultation with counsel and appropriate specialists, selected the disposition.

## Phase 7 — Execution control

After approval, Nariway tracks:

- agreement execution;
- shipment;
- custody changes;
- insurance changes;
- condition documentation;
- auction date;
- sale result;
- unsold lots;
- private-sale status;
- donation acceptance;
- beneficiary delivery;
- receipts and releases;
- outstanding documents;
- settlement status.

Periodic onsite visits can occur when useful.

## Phase 8 — Reconciliation and close

For each resolved asset, Nariway links:

> beginning record → decision → execution → settlement or transfer → supporting evidence

Sale example:

- estate FMV;
- gross result;
- commission;
- shipping;
- insurance;
- conservation;
- other charges;
- net settlement;
- cash receipt;
- closing evidence.

Noncash transfer example:

- estate FMV;
- recipient;
- transfer date;
- receipt/release/deed;
- final location;
- supporting evidence.

Anything that does not reconcile becomes an exception.

The matter closes with a Collection Resolution Report.

This is not a beautiful collection catalogue.

It is a control document.

## 3. Value and Expert Record

Purpose:

Make different values and professional opinions legible.

Possible fields:

- work ID;
- expert/provider;
- role;
- qualification/specialty;
- purpose;
- date;
- value or opinion;
- source;
- transaction interest;
- notes.

## 4. Disposition Comparison

Purpose:

Normalize competing transaction alternatives.

The most powerful feature is not the table itself. It is that each field can be traced to source evidence.

Example:

> Seller commission — 0%  
> Source — House A proposal, page 7, dated May 12

## 5. Exception Report

This may become the signature Nariway work product.

Instead of forcing an attorney to review 150 records, Nariway surfaces what is unresolved.

Examples:

- 11 works lack current estate FMV support;
- 4 works appear on the insurance schedule but not the verified inventory;
- 7 works have beneficiary interest but remain marked for sale;
- 3 proposals expire within 14 days;
- 2 material works have only one market proposal;
- 1 appraiser is affiliated with a proposed sales agent;
- 6 sold works lack settlement statements;
- 2 settlement statements do not reconcile to cash receipts;
- 3 distributed works lack signed receipts;
- 1 work remains physically unverified.

Nariway should distinguish between:

- informational exceptions;
- action required;
- specialist/legal review required;
- critical unresolved.

The exact labels should be reviewed with practicing attorneys before finalizing.

## 6. Fiduciary Decision Record

Purpose:

Create a factual record of a material disposition decision.

Possible content:

- asset or tranche;
- alternatives considered;
- expert inputs received;
- documented economics;
- beneficiary considerations;
- relevant deadlines;
- known interests or incentives;
- missing information;
- fiduciary decision;
- decision date;
- approval evidence;
- counsel notes supplied for inclusion if appropriate;
- source index.

Nariway does not provide the legal conclusion.

## 7. Collection Resolution Report

Purpose:

Close the art workstream.

Possible executive summary:

- original number of works;
- aggregate estate FMV where available;
- works distributed;
- works sold;
- works donated;
- works retained;
- other resolutions;
- unresolved works;
- gross sale proceeds;
- transaction expenses;
- net sale proceeds;
- noncash transfers;
- remaining exceptions.

Appendices can include:

- disposition schedule;
- expert record;
- decision record;
- transaction reconciliation;
- document index;
- unresolved exceptions.

The goal is for the attorney to be able to put this report in the permanent estate file.

# 16. Economics

## Comparable technology economics

Estateably validates the willingness of estate firms to use matter-specific technology and offers current per-file pricing.

EstateExec similarly uses per-estate pricing.

Heirloom validates a stronger proposition: specialized infrastructure can be added to estate administration as a matter-specific service without adding headcount.

These products are cheaper than Nariway because Nariway is not software. It is senior professional service supported by internal technology.

## Attorney time economics

Clio's 2026 published New York data, based on 2025 billing data, reports:

- average New York lawyer rate approximately $426/hour;
- average New York nonlawyer legal-professional rate approximately $227/hour;
- New York wills-and-estates lawyers approximately $448/hour;
- New York trusts lawyers approximately $425/hour.

This supports the proposition that even 20-40 hours of professional administrative burden can create meaningful economic cost.

Nariway should **not** pitch itself primarily as "eliminating billable hours."

A better pitch is:

- keep lawyers focused on legal judgment;
- reduce high-friction document and spreadsheet work;
- make complicated art matters easier to take on;
- improve capacity without adding permanent headcount;
- improve the quality and consistency of the estate file;
- reduce late-stage reconstruction;
- create better visibility for the fiduciary.

# 23. Key risks

## Risk 1 — The market is narrower than expected

We do not yet have a reliable count of annual U.S. estates with collections complex enough to justify Nariway.

Mitigation:

- do not overinvest before customer interviews;
- use the $5,000 service to test real willingness to pay;
- expand referral targets from attorneys to trust companies and family offices if needed;
- maintain a low fixed-cost structure.

## Risk 2 — Art advisors already perform the work

Some do.

Mitigation:

- position clearly around financial control and evidence;
- collaborate with art advisors rather than displace them;
- stay out of connoisseurship;
- make the output meaningfully more structured than an ordinary advisory engagement.

## Risk 3 — Category confusion

Prospects may assume Nariway is an appraiser or art advisor.

Mitigation:

- repeat the boundary everywhere;
- show the role-allocation table;
- make deliverables concrete;
- avoid art-market language that implies expertise Nariway does not claim.

## Risk 4 — Liability

The work touches fiduciary decisions and valuable property.

Mitigation:

- strong engagement terms;
- appropriate professional-liability insurance;
- cyber insurance;
- no custody;
- no valuation opinions;
- no legal advice;
- source-linked records;
- careful documentation of client-supplied inputs;
- legal review of service scope before launch.

## Risk 5 — Data security

Estate records are sensitive.

Mitigation:

- secure architecture;
- restricted access;
- vetted vendors;
- no public AI systems using client data for training;
- retention policy;
- encryption;
- incident procedures.

## Risk 6 — Founder overload

Complex matters can become coordination-heavy.

Mitigation:

- strict scope;
- project-based support;
- internal automation;
- cap simultaneous complex matters;
- charge for complexity;
- distinguish control work from general project management.

## Risk 7 — Becoming an art-market intermediary

If Nariway starts recommending venues, negotiating art-market strategy, or earning transaction compensation, the distinction weakens.

Mitigation:

- maintain compensation policy;
- use art specialists for market judgment;
- focus on factual proposal normalization and control.

## Legal and tax context

**IRS Form 706 Instructions and Schedule F**

Key point:

- when qualifying art/collectibles meet relevant thresholds, appraisal documentation by an expert and appraiser qualifications are required.

**IRS estate administration expense guidance**

Key point:

- estate administration expense categories can include fiduciary, attorney, accounting, property-management/conservation, and qualifying sale-related costs; counsel must determine treatment for a specific matter.

**New York Public Administrator Guidelines**

Key points useful as a control benchmark:

- property disposition should be controlled by the fiduciary;
- auction items should be reconciled to inventory;
- sale results should be retained in the estate file;
- multiple bids are required in certain public-administrator dispositions;
- vendor selection, approvals, and documentation matter.

These rules do not automatically apply to every private executor. They are useful evidence of how fiduciary prudence becomes operational controls.

# 29. Final strategic statement

Nariway should be built around this idea:

> **A significant art collection entering an estate does not need another general advisor. It needs a controlled process that lets the fiduciary use many specialists without losing the record of what was known, what was compared, what was decided, what happened, and whether everything reconciled.**

Nariway's role is therefore deliberately narrow and valuable.

> **Art experts provide art judgment. Attorneys provide legal judgment. Appraisers provide valuation. Auction houses and dealers provide market proposals. The fiduciary makes the decision. Nariway makes the information complete, comparable, controlled, documented, and reconcilable.**

That is the company to build.
