The Landscape

The Great Wealth Transfer and Art

A great deal of art is changing hands as wealth passes between generations, and much of it stays within families. What passes with a collection is not only the objects and their value. It is the responsibility for deciding what the collection becomes.

Over the coming decade a large share of the world's private wealth will move to a new generation, and a great deal of privately held art will move with it.

$992 billion

An estimated $992 billion in art and collectibles could change ownership over the coming decade, part of a broader transfer of wealth between generations.

The size of that broader transfer is not a settled number. Three respected estimates circulate, and they measure different things.

$31 trillion

Deloitte and ArtTactic. Global, over about a decade. The basis for the art figure above.

$124 trillion

Cerulli Associates. United States, through 2048.

$83 trillion

UBS. Global, over 20 to 25 years.

They cannot be added together or read as a single figure. Nariway uses the art-specific number from Deloitte and keeps the totals apart.

Art is a material part of what is moving. Holdings of art and collectibles among the wealthiest families were about $2.56 trillion in 2024 and are projected to reach $3.47 trillion by 2030. For many collectors art is a fifth or more of their wealth, and it is the part least like cash. It is concentrated, hard to value, slow to sell, and personal.

A transfer is not the same as a sale. When a collection passes between generations, most inherited art is kept. About 72% of inheritors hold on to at least some of what they receive, and roughly 80% of collectors intend to pass their collections to family. Storm King has stayed with one family across three generations and sixty-six years. When a collection does reach the market soon after a death, it is usually because no one arranged anything else, as happened when the de la Cruz collection was auctioned within months of its founder's death.

Keeping a collection is not the same as deciding its future. Just over one in ten collectors have formalized a plan with an advisor, and only about a third of those who intend to leave art to their families have discussed what they will leave or provided for its care. More often, holding the collection postpones the decision rather than settling it.

Ownership can transfer more easily than the things that make a collection what it is.

The objects move, and their financial value moves with them. The knowledge of why each work was acquired, the relationships with artists and institutions, the resources to store and insure and care for the work, and the intent behind the whole, do not move on their own. The first person to inherit is often a surviving spouse. The next generation may collect in ways the founder never did. A collection also asks for money and attention simply to be held, and someone has to be willing and able to give it.

Where that knowledge and intent carry across a generation, a collection can hold together, as Storm King's has. Where they do not, its future is settled by default, or by a plan the founder would not have recognized. Albert Barnes wrote his intentions into an ironclad deed, and a court still rewrote them so the Barnes Foundation could survive.

What a collection needs, and what a family can bring to it, decide which futures are actually open. Giving it away is not a reliable default. An estimated 90% to 95% of art offered to museums is declined, and the door is hardest for collectors with no prior tie to the institution. When Marjorie Merriweather Post left her estate to the Smithsonian, it was handed back because the museum could not afford to run it. Founding an institution is costly to sustain, and whether one lasts turns more on its governance than on its endowment. The Fisher family's plan for their own museum proved unworkable, and their collection went to the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art on a hundred-year loan instead.

In the end a collection can be kept, divided, sold, given, lent, partnered with an institution, or made public through a new one, and many move through several of these over time. Some become museums, as the Barnes and Chinati did. Some are placed with an existing institution, as the Fisher and Anderson collections were. Some are given across many museums, as the Vogels' was. Some are dispersed deliberately, as Souls Grown Deep has done. Each is a different answer, and none of them is automatic.

The Great Wealth Transfer moves more than objects and financial value. It transfers the responsibility for deciding what happens next.
