**1. The collection that gave itself away**
> William Arnett spent thirty years assembling the largest collection of Black vernacular art of the American South, work by self-taught artists the art world had spent a century declining to see. A collection of that weight usually ends in a museum with the collector's name over the door. He did the reverse. He gave it away, one work at a time, into more than twenty institutions. The instinct is to keep a collection whole, since its meaning seems to live in the assembly. Arnett understood the opposite. The way to make these artists impossible to ignore was not to gather them where the art world could hold them at a distance, it was to place them permanently inside the rooms that had left them out.

**2. The art America cannot quite see**
> There is a collection of American art most Americans will never see. Hundreds of works, placed by a foundation called FAPE, hang inside United States embassies around the world, in rooms a citizen generally enters only as a guest, or not at all. In a restored 1836 building in New Canaan, Connecticut, a small foundation has begun bringing some of it home for a season, into a town where anyone can walk in. What holds my attention is the quiet radicalism of the gesture. It is not about owning remarkable art. It is about letting people stand in front of what has been kept out of reach.

**3. She planned a museum she would never see**
> In the 1920s Marion Koogler McNay designed a Spanish Colonial house in San Antonio, down to the stencils she painted onto the ceilings by hand. In her will she ordered most of it taken apart. When she died in 1950 she left the house, the collection, and two thirds of an oil fortune to become the first museum of modern art in Texas, along with exact instructions for how the rooms she had made were to be undone. It opened four years later with a Picasso exhibition she never saw. She had planned every part of a future that did not include her. Seventy-five years on it is still there, and what stays with me is the arithmetic of it, how much a person surrenders for a collection to outlast them.

**4. Then what**
> Collectors tend to assume that a lifetime of collecting ends with a grateful museum. More and more, the museum says no. Space is finite, conservation is expensive, and a private collection rarely matches an institution's own direction. The gift is declined, politely, and the question that has been deferred for decades arrives all at once. What happens to the work now. Almost no one stands beside the collector when that question finally comes.

**5. A postal clerk and a librarian**
> On a postal clerk's salary and a librarian's, in a one bedroom apartment in New York, Dorothy and Herbert Vogel built one of the significant collections of minimal and conceptual art of their century. They bought what they could carry home on the subway. When the time came to let it go, they did not sell. They gave fifty works to a public museum in each of the fifty states. The collection was never a function of how much money they had. It was a function of how closely they looked.

**6. The question the art world keeps not asking**
> The question the art world returns to is what a collection is worth. The more interesting question is what becomes of it. A museum, a foundation, a gift to a university, a traveling exhibition, a quiet distribution across many institutions, an auction, and now and then a disappearance. Every collection of consequence eventually reaches that turning, and it is a surprisingly solitary place to stand, since the people who could help think it through are usually assembled only after the decision has already been made.

**7. Founding a museum is the easy part**
> Founding a museum is the part that makes the news. Keeping one alive for fifty years is the part that does not. A collection I have been studying became a sculpture park on more than two hundred acres in Napa, and now sells work and rents its grounds for events to stay open. The pattern repeats across the cases. The building is the beginning of the problem, not the resolution of it.
