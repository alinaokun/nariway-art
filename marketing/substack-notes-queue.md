**1 — The collection that gave itself away**
> I keep running into a story that overturns an assumption I didn't know I had: that preserving a collection means keeping it together. William Arnett spent decades building the largest collection of Black vernacular art of the American South — and then gave it away, deliberately scattered across more than twenty museums. He seems to have understood something counterintuitive: the way to make these artists un-ignorable wasn't to build a monument, it was to place them *inside* the institutions that had left them out.

**2 — The art America can't quite see**
> Some of the most interesting American art in public service is also among the hardest for the public to actually see — hundreds of works placed by FAPE inside U.S. embassies around the world, encountered mostly by diplomats. A small foundation in New Canaan, Connecticut is quietly letting people in. I can't stop thinking about *access* as its own kind of radical act: not owning great art, but letting people see what's otherwise out of reach.

**3 — She planned a museum she'd never see**
> Marion McNay designed a Spanish-Colonial house in San Antonio down to the stencils she painted by hand — and then, in her will, ordered most of it torn apart to become Texas's first modern art museum. She planned an institution she would never live to enter. Seventy-five years later it's thriving. I keep thinking about how much a person has to *give up* for a collection to outlive them.

**4 — "Then what?"**
> Here's something that surprised me: museums increasingly say *no* to gifts. Collectors tend to assume their local museum will want the art — and often it won't (no space, no conservation budget, no fit). Which quietly raises the real question for anyone who's spent a life collecting: *then what?* Sell it? Scatter it? Build something? Almost no one is there to help think it through.

**5 — A postal clerk and a librarian**
> On modest civil-service salaries, in a one-bedroom New York apartment, Dorothy and Herbert Vogel assembled one of the great collections of minimal and conceptual art. Then they gave it away — fifty works to a museum in each of the fifty states. A reminder that a great collection was never about how much money you had. It's about how you see.

**6 — The most interesting question in the art world**
> I've become convinced the most interesting question in the art world isn't what a collection is *worth*. It's what *becomes* of it. A museum, a foundation, a university gift, a traveling show, a quiet dispersal, an auction — or, sometimes, simply vanishing. Every serious collection eventually reaches that fork, and it's a surprisingly lonely place to stand.

**7 — Starting a museum is the easy part**
> Starting a museum is the easy part. Keeping one alive for fifty years is the hard part. I've been studying collections that became institutions and then struggled — including a beautiful sculpture park in Napa that has been selling works and renting out its grounds to stay afloat. The lesson keeps repeating: the building is a beginning, not an ending.
