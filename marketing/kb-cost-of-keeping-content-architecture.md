> The publication-grade **content architecture** for the second Wave 1 knowledge-base entry (per [[knowledge-base-v1-content-plan]]). Not an essay, not a page. ## Role of this page
The most **distinctive and unowned** entry in the set. No one else assembles the full annual cost of holding a significant collection into one honest figure, and that quiet, compounding cost is the pressure behind most transition decisions. Where the [[kb-gwt-content-architecture|Great Wealth Transfer]] page says *why now*, this page says *why a collection becomes a decision at all*.

- **Reader question:** What does it actually cost to hold a significant collection each year?
- **Wave:** 1. **Depth:** Strong. **Distinct research form demonstrated:** an assembled cost model, scattered benchmarks synthesized into one annual carrying-cost figure (a form nothing else in the set uses).

## Charts and tables (ruthless — one core model, one restrained visual, two callouts)
1. **Core research element — the annual carrying-cost model (a table).** Line items: *Insurance · Storage · Conservation · Collection management · Handling/transit*, each with a sourced range and a one-line driver. The assembled-cost-model centerpiece; it is the thing no one else builds. Ends with the honest note that a real total depends on size, location, and home-vs-vault.
2. **One restrained visual — the home-vs-vault insurance swing.** ~1–2% at home → ~0.1–0.5% vaulted (DP1). A single quiet comparison, no dashboard treatment. (Alternative: the SFMOMA 95%-in-storage stat; pick one at design.)
3. **Two quiet callouts — DP4 (the foregone-return frame, correctly stated) and DP8 (most collectors under-insure).**

**Woven into prose, not visualized:** DP5 (SFMOMA), DP6 (the freeport paradox), DP7 (transit is the main loss). The Collection-page discipline again, most facts belong in the text.

## Explanatory modules (short, factual)
- **M1 · What "cost of keeping" includes.** The five recurring cash line items, assembled once.
- **M2 · Home versus vault.** Storage and security are a lever on insurance, better conditions lower the premium (DP1). The single most useful practical point.
- **M3 · The hidden cost is a foregone return.** The emotional-yield frame, stated correctly (DP4): the return a purely financial owner gives up, not a cash bill; ~2.28% for art.
- **M4 · The largest risk is moving art, not storing it.** Transit/handling dominate claims (DP7); agreed-value cover and the inadequacy of homeowners' limits.
- **M5 · Most collectors under-insure.** The Chubb gap (DP8), price is rarely the reason.
- **M6 · Why the cost drives the decision (the bridge).** Retention is the norm ([[kb-gwt-content-architecture]] DP7), so most owners keep paying this cost for years, which is exactly what turns "what happens to the collection" into a live question.

## Methodology notes (published briefly)
- Ranges are order-of-magnitude and US-centric; a real figure depends on size, location, and storage choice.
- **Market-size figures are stated as unreliable, not cited:** fine-art insurance (C27), appraisal (C33), and logistics (C43) all lack a methodologically transparent source, so the page states the data-quality gap rather than a number.
- SFMOMA (DP5) and the Freeport (DP6) are single cited cases/press estimates, not sector rates.
- The emotional yield (DP4) is a foregone return, not a cash outlay.

## Caveats (explicit, on-page, no internal labels)
- The do-not-say set: DP4 is not a cash cost (and is ~2.28% for art, not 2.6%); DP5 is not a sector rate; no insurance/appraisal/logistics market-size figure is cited as fact.
