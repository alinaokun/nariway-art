## 0. Profile (the front door)
The line under Alina's name shows next to every Note, so it is her most-seen copy. It is the personal profile bio (Settings, Profile), distinct from the publication's short description, but both **truncate** in feeds and cards, so the rule is the same: **keep it to about one short line (4 to 12 words, under ~90 characters), front-load the subject, and lead with the beat, not the credential.** A full-sentence bio is the actual mistake, not because it reads badly on the profile page but because truncation turns it into a broken fragment (a real cautionary case cuts off mid-word at "presen…"). That is why the earlier long drafts read wrong. **Decision (2026-08): drop "Author of The Brief Experiment."** Peers doing it well include Hall Rockefeller's Less Than Half ("A newsletter about women, art, the market, and how we can change the status quo"). Governed by [[positioning]] and [[voice]].

**Six options** (a different register each, all short enough to survive truncation):
1. **Significant private art collections and what becomes of them.** — bare-beat, the exact subject, impossible to truncate badly.
2. **Writing about great private art collections and their legacy.** — the most common Substack convention, verb-first and scannable.
3. **Artobiography | the future and legacy of great private art collections** — LinkedIn-headline register with a pipe, name plus beat in one scan.
4. **Founder of Nariway. Writing Artobiography on collections and their afterlives.** — credential plus beat, as two short clauses not a paragraph.
5. **Great collections outlive their collectors. I write about what happens next.** — voice and hook, a little intrigue.
6. **For collectors thinking about the future of what they have built.** — reader-facing, speaks straight to the collector, good for converting serious ones.

Lead recommendation: **1** (safest short beat) or **3** (name recognition plus beat in one headline).

### Notes format, what performs (2026, data-backed)
The goal is a **restack**, not a like. A restack is worth roughly 12x a like for turning a stranger into a subscriber, so a Note must be a complete, self-contained thing someone will re-broadcast.
- **Length.** 31 to 60 words is the sweet spot. The 61 to 120 band is the dead zone. A full 300 to 600 word micro-story is the other winning format. The middle loses.
- **Hook, line one.** A specific, verifiable fact, a provenance detail, a concrete scene, or a flat claim. No throat-clearing. Specificity stops the scroll, not elegance.
- **One idea.** Cut anything serving a second purpose.
- **Visual ladder.** Two or three beats with a blank line between them, and white space around them. A wall of text is the most common death. The ladder comes from **beat breaks, not from fragmenting sentences.**
- **Payoff last, but concrete.** The final beat is a flat, specific fact or image, built to be restacked. **Not a manufactured moral** ("it was never about X, it was about Y" is the tell to avoid) and **not a question** (a closing question cut conversion by about a third). Let the last fact carry the meaning.
- **No links in the body** (they suppress reach).
- **Voice reconciliation ([[voice]]).** A Note is the same voice made simple and readable, not a different grammar. Write **plain, complete sentences, one idea each, the kind a person says out loud.** Avoid both edges, no fragment stacks (three clipped stubs, the machine tell, and a staccato-ban violation) and no dense clause-heavy sentences (which read stiff). Break the beats with blank lines so the white space comes from the breaks, not from chopping sentences. A voice-true Note usually runs a little above the 60-word optimum, an accepted trade. Openers are plain and direct, which is not the same as casual.
- **Growth is behavior.** Replying to comments and restacking others with an added line of insight moves the list more than any single Note's wording.

## 4. The Artobiography issue format (the digest, working spec 2026-08)
The concrete embodiment of *Artobiography = the synthesis layer* ([[editorial-direction]] "The publishing architecture"). Nariway owns the primary material (the Conversations live on the site, [[conversations-series]]); **the newsletter is where Alina says "here's what I'm noticing."** It is **not** a link roundup. Each issue is organized by an **idea** she noticed across several conversations, not by a list of names. The differentiator vs. the digests she studied (The Art Newspaper, Schwab): they *aggregate*; Artobiography *connects ideas across original conversations only Nariway has conducted.*

**The five components (a fixed, recognizable grammar):**
1. **Alina's note** (200 to 350 words). The signature of the publication, and the part neither reference newsletter has. Not "here are this week's links," but "I've been speaking with people on different sides of the same decision, and here is what keeps coming up." Her thinking lives here.
2. **The lead conversation.** One person given prominence: large portrait, strong headline, a 50 to 100 word setup in her voice, then "Read the full conversation" to Nariway. No generic "learn more."
3. **Two more conversations.** Smaller treatment, image + headline + 40 to 70 words each. **Three conversations per issue total** is the target, not five, three lets each person matter; five reads as a roundup. **Curated, not exhaustive**, if seven were published since the last issue, still pick three.
4. **One thing I'm thinking about** (75 to 150 words). A recurring element, a glimpse of the field research developing behind Nariway (e.g. "public ownership isn't public access"). **This is the bridge to [[what-becomes-of-great-art-collections|the flagship report]] and [[field-definition]]**, a report finding surfaced early and publicly, without turning the newsletter into the report.
5. **One discovery request.** Sits quietly at the bottom: a one-line reply invitation ("know a collection I should look into?"). Every issue is another inbound sensor, the same loop as the Welcome P.S. and Qwoted.

**Subject lines carry the idea, never the issue number.** Not "Artobiography #47." Use the idea ("The museum is only one answer"; "Who decides what happens to a collection?"; "What collectors don't see when they offer a gift"). The **preview text carries the names** ("New conversations with Mark Hilbert, Leslie Anderson, and Colleen Boyle"). The idea gets the open; the names add credibility.

**Cadence: biweekly as the default rhythm, but it follows the research, not a timer.** Weekly creates pressure to manufacture a theme every seven days and invites filler, the exact thing the Nariway-archive / Substack-synthesis split exists to avoid. Publish an issue when three conversations genuinely belong together and there is something to say across them (early on that may be every three to four weeks, which is fine). **Do not start until there is enough quality material to be selective**: lay the first ~five finished conversations on the table, ask *what do these people unexpectedly have in common?*, and let that question choose the best three for Issue 1. Increase frequency later only if the material clearly warrants it.

**Repurposing is a feature.** A published Conversation is a permanent asset. Mark Hilbert can lead "The museum is only one answer" now and support "What makes a museum last?" six months later. The same primary material serves many editorial questions over time, which is the payoff of putting the archive on Nariway.

**Keep the tagline.** Readers care about the subject, not the publishing architecture, so the description stays *"The stories behind remarkable art collections, and what becomes of them"* ([[positioning]]), not "a digest of Nariway Conversations."
