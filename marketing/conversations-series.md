# Conversations — Artobiography's written-interview series

*An editorial format under [[substack]] (owned by CMO + Research), not a new function. Revives the written interviews Alina ran in the original Artobiography, now with a rule for who belongs and a mechanism to convert inbound interest. It is the operational form of the "Artobiography as the relationship engine" technique in [[problem-discovery]].*

## Why this, why now
Alina already ran written interviews and liked the process. [[Hall W. Rockefeller]] once approached the old Artobiography wanting it as a platform, which proves inbound pull existed even without a mechanism behind it. Today an interview can become **article → relationship → introduction → research insight → specialist and collector network → possible case → possible future Nariway relationship.** The interview is not the asset. The **relationship graph accumulating behind the interviews is.** Three years of these is a written history of private collecting and stewardship during the Great Wealth Transfer, and one of Nariway's most valuable intellectual assets.

## The two editorial forms (keep them separate)
- **Artobiography** — the heavy format. Deeply researched collection stories (McNay, Souls Grown Deep, Onera, Fisher). The format Alina just built. Do not turn every edition into an interview.
- **Conversations** — the light format. Shorter written interviews with people who shape what happens to art and collections. A heavy format and a light format that **feed each other**: publish an Artobiography on Onera, interview its founder, they suggest another foundation, that becomes the next Artobiography, someone in it becomes the next Conversation. The publication starts creating its own network.

## The rule for who belongs
One editorial question sits under the whole series:
> **Who can teach us something important about how art is collected, cared for, shared, inherited, or brought into public life?**

That is a large but coherent universe: collectors; founders and directors of private foundations; people who inherited collections; museum directors, curators, collection managers; estate attorneys, art advisors, appraisers, conservators, insurers; museum planners; philanthropists; researchers; founders of unusual exhibition spaces and alternative models such as traveling collections. Much more interesting than "people in the art world."

## Written, not Zoom (a deliberate choice, not a lesser one)
Five thoughtful written questions are a very low-friction ask. The interviewee answers when convenient, a PR person can facilitate, the answers arrive as quotable language in their own words, there is no transcription, and prominent people who would never give an hour will often spend fifteen minutes on five good questions. So Alina can interview far more people. And now the answers double as **research data**: ask thirty advisors some version of "when does a collector begin thinking seriously about what happens to the collection," and you hold thirty informed perspectives on a question central to Nariway. The public sees interesting interviews. Nariway sees patterns.

## The credibility test (the boundary that protects the publication)
Before any interview runs, apply one test:
> **Would I still publish this if Nariway had no advisory business?**

If yes, it belongs in Conversations. If no, it is a private research conversation, not an editorial interview. Readers and interviewees must receive something worthwhile whether or not Nariway ever sells anything. This is what keeps it from becoming disguised customer research.

## Question architecture (three fixed + two person-specific)
*SUPERSEDED for outreach (2026-08) by "The research-integrated conversation program" below (the five-lens spine). Kept for reference. The editorial framing, the five movements, still lives in [[editorial-direction]] ("The Artobiography conversation format"); the lenses are its research-integrated evolution. Under the conversation-first decision (2026-08-17), this is Artobiography's primary format for year one.*
Not identical questions for everyone, that goes dull. Three fixed questions that quietly probe Nariway territory, plus two unique to the person's collection or work.

**For a collector**
1. What changed between the first work you bought and the collection you have today?
2. Do you think of owning art primarily as ownership, or does it come with some responsibility for what eventually happens to it?
3. Have you thought about what you would ultimately like the collection to become?
Then two specific to their collection.

**For an institution or foundation founder**
1. Was creating a public space always the plan?
2. What alternatives did you consider?
3. What turned out to be much harder than you expected?

**For an advisor / attorney / family office**
1. Think about the last collector you worked with who was weighing the future of a significant collection. What started that conversation?
2. Who became involved?
3. What part of the decision was hardest to resolve?

These map directly onto the four discovery questions in [[problem-discovery]], so every Conversation with a practitioner is also a logged data point there.

## The research-integrated conversation program (evolved 2026-08, the First-10)
The conversations are **not a separate content project**. They are the **human research layer of the same knowledge base producing** [[what-becomes-of-great-art-collections]]. Each conversation tests or deepens a report proposition and builds an ecosystem relationship. Working principle (from the First-10 outreach plan, Alina's working doc): *the interviews are the primary-source layer of the report.*

### The common research spine (the five lenses)
Do not ask everyone the same words. Ask everyone through the same **five lenses**, so answers compare later, with custom questions per person:
1. **Trigger** — when did the collection's future become a real decision, not a distant idea?
2. **Choice** — what options were considered, and why did one path win?
3. **Friction** — what was harder, costlier, or more constrained than outsiders expect?
4. **Structure** — how did money, governance, tax, family, institutions, access, and physical care shape the outcome?
5. **Learning** — what does this person know now that could change how Nariway understands the field?

Every conversation ends with the two **network questions**: who else should I speak with? what collection, institution, or model do most people overlook?

**Claude's one recommendation (accept or drop):** keep the five lenses, and also ask ONE **identical signature question** of everyone, *"What do you think a collector owes to the collection they've built, if anything?"* The lenses give consistency of *theme*; an identical question gives consistency of *wording*, which is the only thing that lets you truly compare a collector's answer against an heir's, a museum director's, and an attorney's, the cross-position divergence that is the most citable thing the series can produce. One identical question does not make the set formulaic.

### Evidence discipline (matches the report's McKinsey/BCG/WEF bar)
**An interview is primary *qualitative* evidence, not statistical proof.** An expert can explain a mechanism, reveal a practice, suggest a hypothesis, challenge a finding, or point to a source. A claim like "80% of collectors do X" does **not** enter [[what-becomes-of-great-art-collections]] because an expert said it, it is verified independently first, or marked unknown. This separation keeps the conversations credible as report input.

### After every conversation, capture
One thing that changed or sharpened the view · one report claim/hypothesis this supports, challenges, or complicates · one fact or number needing independent verification · one new pathway/institution/collection/role to investigate · one introduction to request · one quote or idea possibly worth publishing · whether this belongs on Nariway as a public Conversation. **Each capture updates the [[what-we-now-believe|Evidence Base]]**: move the relevant hypothesis's confidence and add the new evidence (for OR against) and the next test, this is the point of the Conversations as hypothesis tests. Practitioner process-answers also route to [[problem-discovery]].

### Outreach message essentials (outbound, from the Hilbert draft, 2026-08)
Every outreach message and interview Doc should:
- **Personalize from real homework** (dates, names, specifics). The Hilbert questions do this well, and it is the whole edge over PR-generated Q&As.
- **Make the Doc self-explanatory.** It may reach the subject without the Qwoted/intro note, so put a short framing at the top of the *Doc itself*: what Nariway is, that this is a written Nariway Conversation, that it publishes on Nariway first and is later featured in Artobiography.
- **Offer a review-before-publish step.** Telling the subject they can review the final piece before it goes live is the single biggest willingness-to-participate lever, and standard courtesy for written interviews.
- **Set async, no-length-pressure expectations**, and note one or two possible follow-ups.
- **Verify every fact asserted to the subject** (collection size, gallery count, anniversaries) and any "you have said" attribution. Getting the subject's own facts wrong undercuts the homework effect. Source it or soften it.
- **Acknowledge a respondent's prior words ONCE, in the cover note, then let the questions stand clean.** Do not open question after question with "You mentioned… / You noted… / You also made the point…". Handing someone their own words back *caps* the answer, they re-explain what they already told you, and it reads formulaic. The cover note does the "I read your response" work; the questions should name **what you want to know**, not what they said, so each one opens new ground. (Mark Winter set, 2026-08-20.)
- Route through the [[voice]] rules; use *they* for anyone whose pronouns you do not know.
- **Cold outreach needs a credibility anchor** (unlike the warm, PR-mediated inbounds). A cold recipient must be able to verify you are real in seconds, so include a signature with name + "Founder, Nariway" + **nariway.com**, and point to the live series ("recent conversations at nariway.com") now that published Conversations exist as proof (Hall Rockefeller, Adam Szymanski). Keep the ask **two-step** for cold, invite first, send the questions only if they say yes (don't dump five questions on a stranger).
- **Qwoted often auto-adds the expert's PR rep to your message** (seen 2026-08-20). This is a positive, it effectively warms a cold outreach, because a publicist wants their client featured and will facilitate. Treat any PR reply as a good sign and respond warmly and promptly, same as a warm inbound.
- **For an ADJACENT or borderline respondent, qualify before you invest.** Rather than accept-or-decline on the pitch alone, send a short **qualifying question** that tests for the specific on-thesis experience (e.g. to an appraiser: "has your work involved collections going through estate settlement, division among heirs, museum gifts, or retain-vs-sell decisions?"). It tests the assumption instead of acting on it, and it puts the burden on them to reveal genuine transition experience. Note: appraisers who work **estates** are often at the trigger moment ("who got called? the appraiser"), so an authentication-forward pitch can still hide real on-thesis experience. Take them only if the qualifying answer clears the bar, then scope the questions to the *decision*, not the technique.

### The selection test — if we have to convince ourselves someone belongs, they don't (Mark Winter, 2026-08-20)
The clearest signal a guest is right is **effortless fit**: their experience immediately makes us think *"there are five things I really want to ask this person."* The clearest signal they are wrong is having to **work to make their expertise produce a Conversation we're excited about** — the effort of manufacturing the questions is itself the verdict. Leslie (too many good questions, had to cut) and Matthew (his own writing opened them) fit effortlessly; Mark we kept having to pull toward the mission. Two corollaries: (1) **a guest's ability to express ideas clearly is part of the selection criterion** for a *written* series — if you already expect a heavy rewrite, the finished piece will sound more like Alina than the guest, which defeats the point; (2) **a 360-degree view does not require publishing every perspective** — you can learn from someone (and file their insight) without giving them a slot. The roster now has enough momentum to **wait for the effortless yes** rather than fill slots.

## Qwoted — run it as a sensor, not a publishing calendar (decided 2026-08-16)
The point of Qwoted is not to fill an interview slate. It is to **discover people, stories, models, and problems we don't yet know exist** — the things desk research and our own taxonomy can't surface. So run it as a sensor: **ask → listen → notice the anomalies → ask a better next question → repeat.** Do not pre-program a fixed sequence of queries and dutifully execute it; let the *responses* choose the next query. The Drucker move applied to outreach.

**The internal rule for the whole program:** the response we most want is the one that makes us say **"I didn't know anyone was doing that."** A query that only returns ten museum consultants explaining standard museum formation has failed, even if all ten are real. Frame every query broad enough to surface the unconventional answer, and treat *"this doesn't quite fit what you're asking, but one of my clients…"* as the jackpot, not the noise.

**Discipline:** post ONE, watch what comes back, and let the nature of the responses be the intelligence. Twenty pitches with three fascinating ones is a success. Specificity still matters (a PR person must know which client qualifies), but specificity about the *territory*, not a pre-decided outcome.
