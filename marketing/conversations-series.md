# Conversations — Artobiography's written-interview series

*An editorial format under [[substack]] (owned by CMO + Research), not a new function. Revives the written interviews Alina ran in the original Artobiography, now with a rule for who belongs and a mechanism to convert inbound interest. It is the operational form of the "Artobiography as the relationship engine" technique in [[problem-discovery]].*

## Why this, why now
Alina already ran written interviews and liked the process. [[Hall W. Rockefeller]] once approached the old Artobiography wanting it as a platform, which proves inbound pull existed even without a mechanism behind it. Today an interview can become **article → relationship → introduction → research insight → specialist and collector network → possible case → possible future Nariway relationship.** The interview is not the asset. The **relationship graph accumulating behind the interviews is.** Three years of these is a written history of private collecting and stewardship during the Great Wealth Transfer, and one of Nariway's most valuable intellectual assets.

## The two editorial forms (keep them separate)
- **Artobiography** — the heavy format. Deeply researched collection stories (McNay, Souls Grown Deep, Onera, Fisher). The format Alina just built. Do not turn every edition into an interview.
- **Conversations** — the light format. Shorter written interviews with people who shape what happens to art and collections. A heavy format and a light format that **feed each other**: publish an Artobiography on Onera, interview its founder, they suggest another foundation, that becomes the next Artobiography, someone in it becomes the next Conversation. The publication starts creating its own network.

## The rule for who belongs
One editorial question sits under the whole series:
> **Who can teach us something important about how art is collected, cared for, shared, inherited, or brought into public life?**

That is a large but coherent universe: collectors; founders and directors of private foundations; people who inherited collections; museum directors, curators, collection managers; estate attorneys, art advisors, appraisers, conservators, insurers; museum planners; philanthropists; researchers; founders of unusual exhibition spaces and alternative models such as traveling collections. Much more interesting than "people in the art world."

## Written, not Zoom (a deliberate choice, not a lesser one)
Five thoughtful written questions are a very low-friction ask. The interviewee answers when convenient, a PR person can facilitate, the answers arrive as quotable language in their own words, there is no transcription, and prominent people who would never give an hour will often spend fifteen minutes on five good questions. So Alina can interview far more people. And now the answers double as **research data**: ask thirty advisors some version of "when does a collector begin thinking seriously about what happens to the collection," and you hold thirty informed perspectives on a question central to Nariway. The public sees interesting interviews. Nariway sees patterns.

## The credibility test (the boundary that protects the publication)
Before any interview runs, apply one test:
> **Would I still publish this if Nariway had no advisory business?**

If yes, it belongs in Conversations. If no, it is a private research conversation, not an editorial interview. Readers and interviewees must receive something worthwhile whether or not Nariway ever sells anything. This is what keeps it from becoming disguised customer research.

## Question architecture (three fixed + two person-specific)
Not identical questions for everyone, that goes dull. Three fixed questions that quietly probe Nariway territory, plus two unique to the person's collection or work.

**For a collector**
1. What changed between the first work you bought and the collection you have today?
2. Do you think of owning art primarily as ownership, or does it come with some responsibility for what eventually happens to it?
3. Have you thought about what you would ultimately like the collection to become?
Then two specific to their collection.

**For an institution or foundation founder**
1. Was creating a public space always the plan?
2. What alternatives did you consider?
3. What turned out to be much harder than you expected?

**For an advisor / attorney / family office**
1. Think about the last collector you worked with who was weighing the future of a significant collection. What started that conversation?
2. Who became involved?
3. What part of the decision was hardest to resolve?

These map directly onto the four discovery questions in [[problem-discovery]], so every Conversation with a practitioner is also a logged data point there.

## Qwoted — run it as a sensor, not a publishing calendar (decided 2026-08-16)
The point of Qwoted is not to fill an interview slate. It is to **discover people, stories, models, and problems we don't yet know exist** — the things desk research and our own taxonomy can't surface. So run it as a sensor: **ask → listen → notice the anomalies → ask a better next question → repeat.** Do not pre-program a fixed sequence of queries and dutifully execute it; let the *responses* choose the next query. The Drucker move applied to outreach.

**The internal rule for the whole program:** the response we most want is the one that makes us say **"I didn't know anyone was doing that."** A query that only returns ten museum consultants explaining standard museum formation has failed, even if all ten are real. Frame every query broad enough to surface the unconventional answer, and treat *"this doesn't quite fit what you're asking, but one of my clients…"* as the jackpot, not the noise.

**Discipline:** post ONE, watch what comes back, and let the nature of the responses be the intelligence. Twenty pitches with three fascinating ones is a success. Specificity still matters (a PR person must know which client qualifies), but specificity about the *territory*, not a pre-decided outcome.

The locked design intent (the standard the as-posted version met): framed around the real subject, **people who found a larger public life for a private collection**, deliberately *not* framed around "founders of museums." Museum or foundation creation must not read as the preferred outcome; the query is equally interested in unconventional models, and most interested in the ones that don't fit a category.

> **Seeking people who found a larger public life for a private art collection**
>
> For an Artobiography interview series, I'm looking to speak with people who found a way to bring a privately held art collection into wider public life — and I'm interested in every version of that, not just the traditional museum.
>
> That might mean creating a foundation or a small exhibition space, giving a collection to a university, setting up a long-term lending program, turning a house into a public space, building a traveling collection, distributing works across many institutions, or something that doesn't fit any of these categories. I'm especially interested in **the models that don't fit neatly** — the approaches I wouldn't know to look for.
>
> I'd like to understand the thinking: why make the collection public at all, why this approach rather than another, what turned out to be harder than expected, and how the person thinks about the collection's future.
>
> Artobiography is an independent publication about remarkable art collections and the people behind them. **Interviews are conducted entirely in writing and consist of approximately five thoughtful questions — there is no Zoom or phone interview required.** Selected interviews are published on Artobiography; I read every response but retain editorial discretion over whom I feature.
>
> Please send a brief introduction to the collector, founder, or collection, a sentence on what makes the approach distinctive, and a link where I can learn more.

Why this first: it is the warmest, most flattering door (*you built something interesting, I want to understand why*), it is journalism not lead-generation, and it is aligned with what Artobiography is about to publish (Vogel → Storm King → Fisher are all "a larger life without a standard museum"). The Vogels are the proof of concept for the framing itself — their answer was to distribute 2,500 works across the country rather than build an institution, so **"successful legacy" must not be defined as keeping a collection intact.** The query has to be able to hear the distributed, the unconventional, the not-yet-named.

### Query B — confirmed second post (after A's responses are in)
The behind-the-scenes professionals net — the best *intelligence* query, because one post can introduce ten specialist categories at once.
> **Seeking people who have helped private art collections enter public life.** For an Artobiography interview series, I'm looking to speak with professionals who worked behind the scenes as private collections became museums, foundations, long-term loans, university collections, traveling exhibitions, or other forms of public access — museum planners, attorneys, collection managers, registrars, curators, conservators, insurers, architects, art advisors, family-office professionals, and others. I'm interested in the decisions collectors face, the problems that are underestimated, and what those who have been through it wish collectors had understood earlier. Interviews are in writing, approximately five thoughtful questions, no Zoom or phone required. Please include your relevant experience and one or two example projects where disclosure is appropriate.

### After A and B — do NOT pre-program. Follow the signal.
This is the whole discipline. If Query A returns 25 responses and seven point at succession as the real issue, the third query follows *that*. If Query B surfaces a registrar problem we hadn't considered, follow that. If several attorneys mention collectors arriving *after* an irreversible structural decision, investigate that. The outside world teaching Nariway what it needs to become, in practice.

### Held bank (candidates for later — chosen by the signal, not the calendar)
- **Unusual-models query** — a sharper, narrower cousin of Query A aimed only at art parks, seasonal spaces, appointment-based spaces, traveling/distributed collections. Useful if A works but under-surfaces the non-obvious.
- **Generational-transition experts** — estate attorneys, family-office pros, advisors, appraisers on what collectors postpone and what breaks when heirs differ. Feeds [[problem-discovery]] most directly; overlaps Query B, so run one, not both.
- **Families navigating a collection's future** — closest to Nariway's central question, but asks for real trust; works as a warm-introduction ask, likely never as a cold broadcast.
- **Heir / surviving-spouse voice — deliberately NOT posted.** The most important missing perspective (the person who *received* a collection and had to decide, C8), and precisely the conversation to **earn, not solicit.** A cold headline effectively asking "did you inherit an important collection and have to decide what to do with it?" is intrusive. The right path is a referral, months from now: *"you should really talk to X — her husband built an extraordinary collection and she had to figure out what to do with it."* Wait for that.

## Response templates (stay selective without burning PR goodwill)
Query A will draw PR pitches. Be selective — but the same PR people are contacts worth keeping, so every no stays warm. Never promise publication; the credibility test still governs (only feature what you'd run if Nariway had no advisory business). Adjust the voice per the [[voice]] rules; use *they* for any subject whose pronouns you don't know.

**Invite (a promising fit):**
> Thank you for thinking of the series — this sounds like exactly the kind of story it exists for. I'd like to send [Name] five written questions; there's no call required and they can answer whenever it's convenient. Could you confirm the best email and share any background or a link on the collection? I'll follow up with the questions.

**Triage (intriguing, need more before committing):**
> This is intriguing, thank you. Before I follow up, could you tell me a little more about [the model / who made the decision / how the public encounters the collection]? I want to understand what makes it distinctive.

**Gracious hold (not a fit now, keep the door open):**
> Thank you so much for this — I appreciate you thinking of the series. It isn't quite the right fit for what I'm featuring right now, but I'd genuinely like to stay in touch; the focus evolves and I may circle back. Please keep me in mind for collectors or projects involving unusual ways of bringing privately held art into public life.

## The compounding effect
Every interview ends with the network-branching move from [[problem-discovery]]: "who sees a completely different part of this that I should understand, and would you introduce me?" After enough Conversations the same names recur, which identifies who sits at the center of the ecosystem. And being interviewed by Artobiography eventually becomes desirable, not for reach but because Alina becomes known for interviewing unusually interesting people involved with collections. That is how you eventually reach people you cannot find through a search.
