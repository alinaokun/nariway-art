## Role of this page
The cross-cutting **macro anchor** of the knowledge base. It does two jobs: establishes *why the future of collections matters now*, and demonstrates in its own construction that **Nariway handles contested data responsibly** (the integrity module below is the proof). It is a living reference, updated as each annual report lands — the exact material [[nariway-signals]] already tracks.

- **Reader question:** Why does what happens next to significant collections matter now?
- **Wave:** 1. **Depth:** Strong. **Distinct research form demonstrated:** responsible macro-data synthesis + a data-integrity finding.

## Editorial decisions (locked 2026-08-24)
- **DP5 and DP6 publish** as authoritative institutional figures. Knight Frank/UBS (wealth population) and Art Basel/UBS (collector behavior) are exactly the right kind of source for these claims; the numbers are corroborated and their limits documented. Cite the institution normally (see below); **never expose internal source-tier labels (DOC-secondary, PENDING-PRIMARY) to the reader** — that is research-management language, not public copy.
- **The publicly-stated methodology line:** "Nariway prioritizes primary sources and also uses authoritative institutional surveys and reports where appropriate." A figure is withheld only for substantive uncertainty about the number itself, which does not apply here.
- **C1 public wording (softer, approved):** *"An estimated $992 billion in art and collectibles could change ownership over the coming decade, much of it through inheritance rather than sale."* Then the derivation immediately nearby. The number is large enough; no sensationalizing.
- **Three wording fixes applied below:** "at-risk stock of art" → neutral holdings language; "where the transfer will land hardest" → neutral concentration language; "A shift, not a handoff" → "The next generation may collect differently" (state what the survey shows, not a conclusion the data doesn't prove).
- **Ruthless visual hierarchy (not every fact is a chart):** one hero ($992B), one core research table (three transfer estimates), one restrained holdings-growth chart ($2.56T→$3.47T), two quiet callouts ($54T/>95% women; ~72%/~80% retention). DP5 and DP6 are **woven into prose**, not additional visual modules — the Collection-page lesson (elegance came from *not* making every fact visually important).

## Charts and tables (ruthless — four visual elements, no more)
1. **Hero statistic — $992B (DP1).** The primary visual anchor. A single disciplined stat with the softer C1 line and a two-line "what this measures / what it does not." Not a chart.
2. **Core research table — "Three different transfer numbers" (DP2).** Columns: *Estimate · Source · Geography · Scope · Timeline.* Three rows (Deloitte / Cerulli / UBS). The integrity centerpiece; it visibly refuses the single-number temptation. This is the most important editorial element on the page.
3. **One restrained holdings-growth chart — UHNW holdings 2024→2030 (DP3).** $2.56T → $3.47T. Two points, quiet, no decorative treatment.
4. **Two quiet stat callouts — DP4 ($54T spousal / >95% to women) and DP7 (retention ~72% / intent ~80%).** Together they say: the inheritor is often a spouse, and she usually keeps it.

**Woven into prose, not visualized:** DP5 (US wealth concentration) and DP6 (the next generation may collect differently). Making every fact a module turns the page into an "AI Index" dashboard; the Collection-page discipline is the opposite.

## Explanatory modules (short, factual — not essay prose)
- **M1 · What the number actually measures.** Rotation by inheritance, not art coming to market (DP1). Corrects the most common misreading.
- **M2 · Why there is no single transfer figure.** The integrity module (DP2/C36). Nariway's distinctive move; states plainly that three reputable estimates measure different things.
- **M3 · Who inherits first.** Spouses, and overwhelmingly women (DP4). Reframes "the heirs" from children to a surviving spouse.
- **M4 · The next generation may collect differently.** State only what the survey shows (DP6): younger HNW collectors are prominent in the data and active across adjacent categories. Do NOT assert that inherited fine-art collections will not transfer to them, or that the transfer is something other than a handoff — the data does not prove that.
- **M5 · Why this matters for a collection's future.** Retention is the norm (DP7); therefore the decision is *what happens when control changes*, not *whether it sells*. This is the module that connects the macro page to the rest of the knowledge base and to advisory.

## Methodology notes (published on the page, briefly)
- **How DP1 is derived:** 5% × $19.84T, the UHNW share of a projected ~$31T transfer (Deloitte's construction, not Nariway's).
- **Populations are not additive:** DP2 and DP5 both involve figures that measure different universes (global vs US; $30M+ vs $1M+; 10-year vs 20–25-year windows). The page states this rather than harmonizing them.
- **Source tier disclosed:** each figure carries its confidence. DOC-secondary figures whose primary report was not directly reachable are marked and, per the publication gate, do not go live until elevated or editorially cleared as secondary.

## Caveats (explicit, on-page)
- The consolidated do-not-say list from C1, C34, C35, C36 (above, in the wording-limitation column) is honored in the copy, not just in the notes.
- DP5 and DP6 are **PENDING-PRIMARY**; if the refresh cannot reach the primary reports, the page either cites them explicitly as survey-secondary or holds them.
- No collector-household count is stated anywhere (the register's sizing note: there is no published count of collectors whose collections are large enough that continuity is material; do not invent one).
