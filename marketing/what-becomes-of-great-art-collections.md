# What Becomes of Great Art Collections

**The State of the Private Collection.**  Inaugural edition, 2026. Working draft.

*A Nariway report on the futures of significant private art collections.*

---

## Preface

Every great collection is a lifetime of decisions. Then, often within days, it becomes an estate, and those decisions are re-made quickly by people who did not make the original ones.

The art market is well documented. What happens to a collection *after* it leaves its owner is not. This report maps that territory: the pathways a significant private collection can take into public life, what those pathways cost, and what makes some endure while others fail. It is built on a coded dataset of real collections and on primary research into the world that surrounds them.

*Alina Okun, Founder, Nariway*

---

## Key findings

> **1. The transfer is a sorting problem, not a sale.** Around US$992 billion of art and collectibles could change hands over the coming decade, most of it by inheritance rather than sale (modeled). The decision is almost always unmanaged, not planned.
>
> **2. The first decision-maker is usually a surviving spouse, most often a woman.** About US$54 trillion of US wealth passes first between spouses, over 95% of it to women.
>
> **3. Governance predicts survival better than money.** Across the coded cases, a collection with an independent board and a settled succession tends to outlast one with far more money but sole founder control.
>
> **4. Public ownership is not public access.** A collection can pass into public hands and still not be seen. "Public life" is multidimensional, not a single yes or no.
>
> **5. Holding art costs its owner every year, and no market report prices it.** Storage, insurance, conservation and handling accrue silently and land hardest at transfer.
>
> **6. The receiving end is closing.** Museums increasingly decline gifts, because they must carry the same forever-costs.

## 1. The transfer, and why now

*This chapter sets the scale of the coming ownership change, and why it makes the future of the collection an urgent question rather than an academic one.*

A collection is a biography until its owner dies. Then it becomes an estate. The wealth data shows how large that moment has become.

**Table 1. The transfer in numbers.**  *All figures link to the [[claims-register]]; modeled figures are labeled.*

| Figure | What it measures | Ref |
|---|---|---|
| ~US$992B | Art and collectibles changing hands over ~a decade, mostly by inheritance (modeled, Deloitte) | C1 |
| ~US$2.56T → US$3.47T | UHNW art and collectible holdings, 2024 to 2030 | C9 |
| ~US$54T | US wealth passing first between spouses, over 95% to women | C8 |
| ~72% | Inheritors who keep at least some inherited art | C3 |
| ~80% | Collectors who intend to pass their collection to heirs | C12 |
| ~51% | Wealth managers offering art services in 2025 — up long-run from ~25% (2011), but down from 63% (2023) | C10 |

**What the numbers mean.** This is not a wave of liquidation. It is a vast, largely private process of sorting and governance, happening years before any sale. Retention is the norm, and most collectors intend to keep the collection in the family, so the defining question is not "which museum?" but "what happens when control of this thing changes hands?" The surrounding financial industry has noticed, which is why art services inside wealth management have roughly doubled since 2011.

## 2. What collections become: the pathways

*The unit of analysis is the collection and its pathway, not the individual artwork. This chapter lays out the routes; the next tests which endure.*

A significant collection can take many routes into, or out of, public life:

- Standalone museum
- Private foundation
- Gift to a university
- Long-term loan to an existing institution
- Intentional dispersal across many institutions
- Gift, intact or scattered
- Sale, in whole or in part

## 3. What the evidence shows

*These findings come from Nariway's coded dataset of collections (currently 65 and growing toward ~75, hand-selected for variation; no rates are published off a non-random sample). They are stated as findings to test, not as laws.*

**Governance beats endowment, and a deliberate attempt to break that claim sharpened it instead of disproving it.** A collection with an independent board and a settled succession tends to outlast one with more money but sole founder control. The two poles are the **Barnes Foundation**, whose iron-clad donor control had to be rewritten by a court for the institution to survive, and the **Museum of Modern Art**, whose founding gifts were absorbed into an independent-board institution that long outlived its founders. Nariway now runs a standing check each cycle: actively hunt for the case that would break this finding, not just the next case that confirms it. The first such hunt found one worth taking seriously: the **Corcoran Gallery of Art** ran under a genuine independent board for 126 years, no founder-sole control at all, and still dissolved in 2014 once its endowment was exhausted. Good governance, on the evidence so far, reliably prevents the fast, death-triggered collapse this report's failure cases show for founder-sole institutions (see below); it does not prevent the slow, decades-long failure money alone can cause. The **Dia Art Foundation** supplies a matching case from the other direction: founder-sole from 1974, it produced exactly the kind of crisis this pattern predicts, in 1985, while its controlling founder was still living rather than at his death, and survived only because the crisis forced a real board handoff before its assets ran out.

**A corporate foundation can run for four decades on sponsorship alone, with no endowment of its own.** The **Fondation Cartier**, founded in Paris in 1984, turns out to be the true, decade-older origin case for a funding model this report had only seen once before (the **Fondazione Prada**): an independently run public museum, funded entirely by an ongoing corporate sponsorship rather than a fixed capital endowment. Forty-two years on, Cartier is expanding into a third purpose-built Paris venue, not retreating. Neither case has yet faced a real downturn in its parent company's willingness to keep paying, which is the honest limit of what four healthy decades can show.

**Two failure modes.** Collections fail in two distinct ways. One is *governance collapse*, where the collection does not survive its founder (for example, Terra). The other is *money collapse*, where an institution survives for decades and then dissolves on economics (for example, the Corcoran).

**Distribution is not access.** The Vogels gave 2,500 works to one museum in each of the 50 states, yet a follow-up investigation found durable public *custody* but not demonstrable sustained public *view*. Public life turns out to be multidimensional, ownership, custody, visibility, access, scholarship and reach, not a single yes or no. See [[field-definition]].

**A finding hidden in the gaps.** True endowment and staffing figures are systematically unavailable from standard web sources for most US institutions; they require the underlying tax filings. Reporting that gap honestly is itself a credibility signal. The gap itself has more than one shape: a filing simply not yet pulled, a jurisdiction with no disclosure regime at all, and now, from a Thai land-art site funded entirely by one family, a jurisdiction with a real disclosure regime the institution may never have opted into by incorporating at all.

**Corporate collections fail, or don't, through five distinct mechanisms, and even banks misremember their own history.** Corporate-owned collections (bank, insurer, and finance-house holdings, tracked as a separate sub-population in [[report-dataset]]) turn out to disperse or endure through at least five distinct mechanisms: forced liquidation in a parent's bankruptcy, voluntary retreat from a dedicated public venue while the collection stays intact, unaffected continuity through repeated mergers, absorption of a distressed-but-not-bankrupt parent, and anticipatory monetization, selling art for cash years before any crisis actually arrives. A second, unrelated finding recurs across more than one bank: corporate collections narrate their own founding history about as loosely as families do, compressing decades of merger-driven accumulation into one clean, and often inaccurate, founding date.

> **Box 1a. Same merger, two collections.** One bank's collection was absorbed into another's in a 2023 emergency takeover. Read from the absorbed side and the acquiring side both, the case shows the same M&A layering, and the same tidied-up origin story, that this report's family-collection cases already show across generations. A third, independent bank case shows the identical compression of a genuinely layered, multi-merger history into one clean founding date, now a confirmed cross-sector finding rather than a one-off.

**A collection's durability can rest on a mechanism this report's standard ratio cannot see.** One museum, structured deliberately outside the charitable form by its living founder, covers a large and growing cumulative operating loss indefinitely through dividend income from its founder's unrelated business rather than through any endowment or fund attached to the institution itself. It is unambiguously thriving by every visible measure, attendance, expansion, cultural reputation, while being financially unmeasurable in this report's own vocabulary: a new, "unmeasurable by design" shape for the data-availability finding below, distinct from a filing that simply has not been pulled yet.

**A founder can announce the succession plan instead of leaving it to be inferred.** Most of this report's founder-sole cases only reveal their governance trajectory in hindsight, decades after the fact. One newly opened international museum instead states its intended shift from founder control toward a mixed board, publicly, at the moment of opening. Whether a stated intent is honored more reliably than a private one is untestable yet, but it gives the report's founder-constraint hypothesis (H8) a genuinely new kind of data point to track forward.

**Founder constraints do not uniformly predict failure, once the sample's oldest case is added.** The **Barnes Foundation**'s broad indenture, locking governance, location, and display alike, required a court to override before the institution could survive. The **Museo Poldi Pezzoli**, a 19th-century Milanese bequest now 145 years old, the longest survival window in the dataset, carried a binding constraint too, its founder's will fixed his display method, but handed everything else to a standing public-private board immediately, at founding. The constraint that was honored, not broken, was the narrow one. Founder control appears to predict failure less than the *scope* of what a founder locks in.

**A "foundation" is sometimes a foundation in name only.** A Berlin institution branding itself a "Foundation" turns out, on inspection of its own commercial registration, to be a standard limited-liability company with none of a true foundation's independent oversight. It is the same instinct this report already finds in corporate collections that narrate a tidier founding date than their own merger history supports, applied here to legal form rather than history: a name chosen to sound more durable than the underlying structure is.

**Not every institution in this territory owns a collection at all.** Two coded cases, an architecture-advocacy foundation in Connecticut and a converted Soviet cinema in Kazakhstan, run public exhibition programs on a stated policy of never assembling one. They test the report's own definition of its subject as much as they extend it.

## 4. The surrounding ecosystem

*A collection's future is shaped as much by the world around it, the money, the buildings, the tax code, the museums, as by the art itself. This part maps that world. It is where Nariway aims to be the reference source, because no existing report covers it whole.*

### 4.1 The macro picture

*The generational wealth wave, the women and surviving-spouse shift, UHNW demographics and geography, and the collector-age curve — the demand side of everything this report tracks.*

**The money at the top is concentrating further in the US.** Knight Frank's *Wealth Report 2026* counts 713,626 UHNWIs ($30M+ net worth) worldwide, up 162,191 since 2021, with the US capturing 41% of all new UHNWIs added over that span and rising from 33% to 35% of the global total. UBS's own *Global Wealth Report 2026* corroborates the direction through a broader lens: roughly 57.5 million dollar-millionaires globally, more than 40% of them American, and a fast-growing $5M-100M "HNW" tier of about 7 million people (C34). Two different measures, the same message: the wealth this report tracks is landing in the US more, not less, over time.

**The women's-inheritance shift (Table 1) now has a matching collecting-behavior finding.** The Art Basel/UBS Survey of Global Collecting 2025 found HNW women outspent men on art and antiques by roughly 46% in 2024, and hold noticeably more work by women artists, 49% of holdings against 40% for male collectors (C35). Read against C8's finding that over 95% of first spousal transfers go to women, this suggests the surviving spouse who inherits a collection is often also, independently, an active collector with her own distinct profile, not only a passive recipient of someone else's.

**The collector-age curve points somewhere the headlines don't.** 74% of the same survey's ~3,100 HNW respondents were Millennials or Gen Z, and Gen Z allocates roughly a quarter of personal wealth to collecting (C35), but that collecting skews toward design, sneakers, handbags, and sports memorabilia, not fine art, where Boomers still lead spending. A younger, wealthier collecting cohort is arriving; it is not, yet, a fine-art succession audience.

**One number this report will not repeat as settled.** 2026 press increasingly cites a UBS-sourced "~$83 trillion" wealth-transfer figure alongside the ~$31T (Deloitte, Table 1) and $124T (Cerulli, Table 1) figures already used here. All three measure different things, geography, scope, and timeline all differ (C36), and none supersedes the $992B art-and-collectibles figure this report actually operates on; they should never be added together or cited as if they converge.

### 4.2 The cost of keeping art

Holding a serious collection is neither free nor cheap, and no standard market report prices the burden well. That gap is the opportunity.

**The cleanest single anchor comes from finance, not the art trade.** Collectible ownership carries an "emotional yield" of about **2.6 percentage points per year**, the return a purely financial buyer gives up to own the object, measured across 13 collectible classes over as much as 110 years (C16). It is a foregone-return figure, not a cash outlay, and its authors call it conservative. The point it establishes: art quietly costs its owner something material every year to hold.

**Table 2. The out-of-pocket carrying costs.**

| Cost | Typical range | Ref |
|---|---|---|
| Insurance | ~1 to 2% of value/year at home; ~0.1 to 0.5% professionally vaulted | C13 |
| Storage | ~US$5.50/sq ft/month specialist; up to ~US$12.50 in NYC | C14 |
| Conservation | painting cleaning ~US$100 to 250/hr; small restoration ~US$800 to 1,000 | [[market-intelligence]] #5 |
| Handling & shipping | crates US$200 to 2,000+; international shipping US$1,500 to 10,000+ | [[market-intelligence]] #5 |

A modeled all-in figure for a *specific* collection would be a model, and is left unknown here rather than invented; a specialist's audited rate card or the Deloitte Art & Finance risk chapter would resolve it.

**Why it drives the decision.** The carrying cost recurs every year against an asset that pays no dividend. It accrues silently and lands hardest at transfer, when heirs inherit the storage, insurance and conservation bills without the founder's attachment. That is a concrete, unsentimental reason "sell" or "give it away" wins by default, and part of why museums decline gifts, they would carry the cost forever too (see 4.5).

> **Box 2. The freeport paradox.** The wealthy increasingly hold major art in tax-suspended vaults where no one can see it. Geneva's freeport alone is estimated to hold around 1.2 million works and roughly US$100 billion of art, largely invisible (C15). It is the precise negative of Nariway's mission, and the current default: art stored in the dark.

### 4.3 Art finance

Art-secured lending lets an owner extract liquidity from a collection without deciding its future. That is the product's appeal, and its structural limit: the loan does not resolve the collection's fate, it adds a creditor to it.

**The market has quadrupled in a decade.** Outstanding art-secured loans stood at roughly **US$34 to 40 billion in 2025** (C7), up about **4x since 2015**, with the US market growing 15 to 20% a year over the last five years and projected to reach US$42.0 to 50.1 billion by 2027. Private banks alone hold an estimated US$13 to 15 billion of that book.

**Table 3. How an art-secured loan is actually priced.**

| Term | Typical figure | Ref |
|---|---|---|
| Loan-to-value | 50 to 60%; Citi's standard is 50% LTV with a US$10M minimum loan | C21 |
| Minimum collection size | US$20M+ at Bank of America, US$10M minimum loan | C21 |
| Interest rate | ~0.71 to 3.25%, priced as a spread over SOFR | C21 |

**Auction houses are lenders now too.** Sotheby's Financial Services' own loan book doubled from US$800M to US$1.6B since 2021; Christie's Art Finance is estimated (not disclosed) at roughly US$500M (C22). Both houses can advance against a work's low estimate and, on default, sell the same work, a structural echo of the independence problem the report's advisory thesis is built around.

**Why it matters.** A loan **postpones** the keep/sell/donate decision rather than resolving it. If the collateral's value falls and breaches the agreed loan-to-value, the borrower faces a margin call, additional collateral, paydown, or forced sale, typically at auction, in what is often the worst possible market moment to sell, with continuing liability for any shortfall (C22). For a family already delaying the harder governance question, a loan can quietly convert "undecided" into "decided by a margin call."

> **Box 3. Fractional ownership is a different product, not a lighter version of a loan.** Platforms such as Masterworks sell shares in a single artwork to investors; it solves small-ticket market exposure, not liquidity for an existing collection, and carries its own concentrated platform risk. Not treated as an art-finance instrument in the sense above.

**Insurance is the quiet precondition behind every pathway above.** A lender will not advance against uncollateralized, uninsured art, and a museum accepting a gift inherits an insurance obligation as part of the forever-cost this report already prices (§4.5). One structural finding stands out: fine-art insurance claims are dominated by **transit and handling damage, not theft or fire**, up to roughly half of all claims (C26). Every loan, gift, and sale physically moves a work, which makes movement, not storage, the largest source of insured loss behind every "lighter model" pathway (§2) this report tracks gaining share.

*A note on numbers this report will not repeat:* published estimates of the fine-art insurance market's total size disagree by roughly a hundredfold across vendors, with no disclosed methodology behind any of them (C27). Rather than pick one, this report names the disagreement as the finding. What is reliably known is the market's structure, concentrated among a handful of named specialist carriers and, beneath them, the Lloyd's of London Fine Art and Specie syndicates that have long been the trade's global center.

**Who already stands between a collection and its future.** Four kinds of professional currently touch the keep/sell/donate decision, and none of them is paid specifically, and only, to own it independently. Independent art advisors typically bill a retainer, an hourly rate, or a **commission of roughly 10%** of a transacted work's value, the same figure that recurs as an undisclosed "introductory commission" some dealers pay an advisor for a client referral. The field's own credentialing body, the Association of Professional Art Advisors, bars members from accepting sale-linked payments that could conflict with a client's interest, direct, profession-internal evidence that this conflict is common enough to require a rule against it (C28). Bank art-advisory desks sit inside a lending and asset-gathering relationship (§4.3 above), not an independent one. Estate-planning attorneys are usually the actual coordinators on the legal side, convening appraisers, dealers, and accountants, but only once death or incapacity has triggered the engagement, not years earlier when the pathway is still genuinely open. And collection managers and registrars, who cost a private collection roughly **US$60,000 to $150,000 a year** to employ (C29), execute care and logistics rather than the strategic decision itself. Wealth managers' own delivery of art-related services has, if anything, recently retreated, from 63% of firms in 2023 to 51% in 2025, even as 79% of the field agrees art belongs in wealth management (C10), a live gap between conviction and execution.

Art funds remain *research underway*.

### 4.4 Risk, value, and structure

*The tax code, valuation practice, and property law form the invisible scaffolding under every pathway in [[report-dataset]].*

**2026 pulls the donation decision in two directions at once.** A single piece of federal legislation now shapes both ends of the calculation a family makes about giving art away. The federal estate and gift tax exemption rose to **US$15 million per person (US$30 million per couple)** starting this year, permanently replacing a smaller, temporary exemption, so fewer, larger collections face the estate-tax pressure that has historically pushed families toward a museum gift. In the same law, itemized charitable deductions, including art gifts, are now reduced by a new **0.5% of AGI floor**, making the deduction for that same gift modestly smaller when a collector chooses to give anyway. Both changes point the same way, toward keeping the collection rather than giving it away, reinforcing this report's finding that retention is already the empirical norm (§1, C3), now with a live policy tailwind behind it.

**Table 4. The tax scaffolding around a gift of art.**

| Rule | What it means for a collection | Ref |
|---|---|---|
| Estate/gift tax exemption | $15M single / $30M couple from 2026, indexed thereafter | C23 |
| Charitable deduction floor | Itemized gifts (incl. art) deductible only above 0.5% of AGI, from 2026 | C24 |
| Operating vs. non-operating private foundation | Gift to an *operating* foundation can reach full fair-market-value, up to 50% of AGI; a *non-operating* foundation caps the deduction near cost basis, 20% of AGI | [[market-intelligence]] #8 |
| Fractional-gift completion window | Remaining interest must transfer within 10 years of the first gift, or at death; miss it and every prior deduction is recaptured plus a 10% penalty | C25 |

**Why some founders choose the harder structure.** Several cases in [[report-dataset]], among them Glenstone and Chinati, are organized as *private operating foundations* rather than simple grantmaking ones. The case narratives explain this as a control choice; the tax rule above supplies the other half: an operating foundation earns the founder a materially better deduction on lifetime gifts of the collection itself, not just more control over how it is run.

**Valuation is contested because the government checks it, and finds reason to disagree.** The IRS refers any appraised artwork worth $50,000 or more, on an audited return, to its own **Art Advisory Panel**, a standing, unpaid panel of up to 25 museum directors, curators and dealers. In FY2023, the most recent year a public tally could be found, the Panel reviewed 251 items across 67 taxpayer cases, an aggregate claimed valuation of $360.9 million, and recommended adjusting **63%** of the items it reviewed, a net adjustment of –$64.6 million (C31). Valuation disputes are not the exception in this territory; they are closer to the norm.

**The dispute exists because "value" means three different things.** Fair market value (the IRS standard for a donation deduction or an estate) is not insurance replacement value (typically higher, priced for retail replacement urgency), which is not an auction estimate (a non-binding marketing forecast, not a valuation opinion at all). Each is authoritative within its own purpose; none is more "correct" than the others (C32). A collector, an insurer, and the IRS can each hold a different, individually defensible number for the same work on the same day.

**Appraisal itself runs on a rule almost identical to the one this report already found for art advisors.** A "qualified appraiser," in the IRS's own definition, cannot charge a fee based on a percentage of the appraised value (C30), the same structural anti-conflict logic behind the APAA's no-commission rule for advisors (§4.3, C28). Two adjacent professions, two independently adopted rules against being paid more for saying a number is bigger.

*As with fine-art insurance (§4.3), the appraisal-services market's own size is not reliably known: only one uncorroborated aggregator estimate could be found, no named research house sizes the segment specifically, so this report states the gap rather than a number (C33).*

**Two flexible pathways share a hidden clawback.** Art-secured lending (§4.3) lets a family postpone the keep/sell/donate decision, at the cost of a margin call if the collateral's value falls. Fractional gifts offer a comparable postponement in the other direction, donating a work gradually, at the cost of the same kind of clawback if the ten-year completion window lapses. Read together, they are the report's clearest illustration that a pathway which avoids one hard decision usually just moves the hard decision onto a clock.

> **Box 4. Provenance risk got a new enforcement chapter in April 2026.** The Holocaust Expropriated Art Recovery Act of 2025 was signed into law April 13, 2026, re-arming US courts to hear decades-old Nazi-era restitution claims; a Manhattan court ordered the return of a Modigliani to an original owner's heir the same month, after an eleven-year case. It is a current, live reason museum due-diligence and title-warranty requirements before accepting a major gift (§4.5) keep tightening.

**Valuation stays contested even after both sides have agreed a number in writing.** A billionaire collector sued his insurance syndicate for $410 million in 2025, arguing a fire had robbed five paintings of their "spark," a loss with no visible trace. A New York court rejected the claim in September 2025. The case is not typical of how insurance claims resolve, but it is a vivid, current pairing with the IRS's own Art Advisory Panel finding above: sophisticated parties, a binding agreed-value policy, and still no shared answer to what a work of art is worth once something has happened to it.

### 4.5 Institutional economics: why museums say no

*A gift is not free to the museum that receives it either. This section prices the receiving side of the transaction that 4.2 priced for the owner.*

**Museums decline most of what they are offered.** An estimated **90 to 95%** of art and collectible material offered to museums is declined, per Bank of America's own private-bank art advisory practice (C17). Museums increasingly treat a gift as a liability rather than a windfall: it inherits the same storage, insurance, and conservation clock described in 4.2, permanently, with no revenue attached, which is why some now ask a donor to accompany a gift with cash toward its future upkeep.

**Storage is the concrete reason.** A cited analysis of one major museum's own deaccessioning rationale found **95.2%** of its collection was in storage at any given time, at an estimated **~$4.1M a year** to maintain the stored portion alone (C18), a case study, not a sector rate, but illustrative of the arithmetic behind every museum's "no."

**The sector's finances are getting worse, not better.** The American Alliance of Museums' own 2025 survey of the field found only **52%** of US museums report stronger financial performance than before the pandemic, down from 57% the year before; **55%** report attendance still below 2019 levels; and **34%** have had a government grant or contract cancelled, with only 8% of those museums saying the funding has been fully replaced (C19). A marquee example is playing out in real time: the Philadelphia Museum of Art reported a **~$10M deficit** on a $75.6M FY2025-26 budget, nearly double the year before, projected to reach as much as $15.9M the following year (C20), the same endowment-and-revenue-erosion mechanism the coded [[report-dataset]] Corcoran case shows over 126 years, now visible at a much larger institution on a much shorter clock.

**Deaccessioning rules are narrowly bounded, by the profession's own choice.** The Association of Art Museum Directors permits sale proceeds to fund only a specifically defined "direct care" of the remaining collection, conservation, storage materials, climate control, explicitly excluding staff salaries and exhibition costs. Even the industry's own escape valve for a struggling collection is built to keep the art, not to fund the institution around it.

## Appendix. Methodology and sources

**Source hierarchy** (authoritative first): (1) primary and official, IRS Form 990s, audited financials, deeds, probate, IMLS; (2) reputable institutional research, Deloitte/ArtTactic, Art Basel/UBS, TEFAF, Cerulli, AAM/AAMD; (3) reputable press; (4) scholarship. Museum self-history is color only.

**Discipline.** Every number is sourced with a confidence tag, or marked unknown with the source that would resolve it. Modeled figures are labeled as models. "Unknown" is a counted value; every chart states n and how many cases were unknown for that field. A citation audit gates publication. The recurring numbers live in [[claims-register]]; the coded evidence in [[report-dataset]]; the ecosystem research in [[market-intelligence]].

**Interviews.** The Nariway conversations ([[conversations-series]]) are **primary qualitative evidence, not statistical proof.** An interview can explain a mechanism, reveal a practice, challenge a finding, or point to a source. A quantitative claim from an interview ("80% of collectors do X") does not enter this report because an expert said it; it is verified independently first, or marked unknown.
