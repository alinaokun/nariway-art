## 0. What this report is
The art market is well covered (Art Basel/UBS, Deloitte, TEFAF report on sales and prices). **This report is about what happens to significant collections *beyond* the market** — the fate of great private collections and the pathways into public life (museum, foundation, university, long-term loan, intentional dispersal, gift, sale, retention, and models that do not yet have a name), and what makes them last or fall apart. No one owns that lane. The spine is the **Great Art Transfer**. The edge is a proprietary, coded dataset of how collections actually entered public life and how they fared, plus original cross-case analysis the field only tells anecdotally.

## 1. Executive summary (drafts as findings firm up)
*Placeholder to be written last, from the sections below. Current one-line thesis:* a collection is a biography until its owner dies, then it becomes an estate, and a lifetime of choices is re-decided fast by people who did not make them, usually with no independent party standing beside the collector. The transfer is enormous and already underway; the decision is almost always unmanaged, not planned.

## 2. The transfer, and the money (the demand-side spine)
Sourced to [[claims-register]]; do not restate numbers, cite the claim.
- **~$992B** of art and collectibles could change ownership over ~a decade, mostly by inheritance, not sale (C1, a Deloitte *model*, label it as such).
- UHNW art holdings **~$2.56T rising to ~$3.47T by 2030** (C9).
- **~$54T** of US wealth passes first to surviving spouses, **>95% to women** (C8) — the first decision-maker the market overlooks.
- Retention is the norm (72% keep at least some inherited art, C3), and **~80% intend to pass to heirs** (C12) — so the dominant question is not "which museum?" but "what happens when control changes."
- The surrounding financial industry increasingly treats the collection as a live problem: **~51%** of wealth managers now offer art services, up from ~25% in 2011 (C10); art is ~20-28% of collector wealth (C11).
- **Reading:** this is not a liquidation event, it is a vast, private **sorting and governance process** happening years before any sale. *(Chart candidates: the transfer decomposed; the spousal-first-decider flow; retention vs. donation-intention gap.)*

## 3. The pathways (the models, explained through cases)
The unit is the **collection and its pathway**. The set of pathways is wide: standalone museum, private foundation, university gift, long-term loan, intentional dispersal across institutions, gift (intact or scattered), sale, retention, art park, appointment-based space, traveling collection, and abandoned/changed plans that produced a better outcome than the original. Each is illustrated by coded cases in [[report-dataset]]. *(Chart candidate: pathway distribution across the 50 cases, with n and unknowns stated.)*

## 4. What the dataset shows (the proprietary findings — the moat)
From [[report-dataset]] (50 of 50 coded; hand-selected for variation, no rates published off a non-random sample). Stated as findings-to-test, not laws.
- **Governance beats endowment for survival.** A collection with an independent board and a locked succession outlasts one with far more money but sole founder control (Pattern 14, the first fully-counted governance-survival table). The [[barnes]] (iron-clad donor control, judicially rewritten to survive) and [[moma]] (founding gifts absorbed into an independent-board institution, the "anti-Barnes") are the two poles.
- **Two distinct failure modes.** Governance collapse (a collection that does not survive its founder, e.g. [[terra]]) versus money collapse (survives decades then dissolves on economics, e.g. [[corcoran-gallery]]). The `survived_founder` field cleanly separates them.
- **A shift toward lighter models.** Long-term loan, foundation, and intentional dispersal are rising relative to the standalone founder's museum. *(Test the rate across the universe; do not over-claim.)*
- **Distribution is not the same as access (new, 2026-08-17).** The Vogels distributed 2,500 works to one museum in each of 50 states, yet the afterlife investigation ([[vogel-fifty-states-afterlife]]) found durable public *custody* but not demonstrable sustained public *view*. This forces a real distinction the whole report can hang on: **public life is multidimensional** (ownership, custody, visibility, access, scholarship, education, geographic reach), not binary. See [[field-definition]].
- **The data-availability finding (a finding in itself).** True endowment (990 Schedule D) and FTE are systematically unavailable from standard web views across most US cases, they need the 990 PDF/XML. Honest gap-reporting becomes a credibility signal, not a weakness.

## 6. The macro picture (demand, wealth, demographics)
Deepen §2 beyond the transfer headline: the generational wealth wave, the women/spousal shift, UHNW demographics and geography, the collector-age curve, and how "collecting stops being about acquiring and becomes about deciding" as a demographic phenomenon. *(Research underway.)*

## 7. The cost of keeping art (the ownership burden almost no one prices)
The physical and operational reality that drives many transition decisions: **storage** (climate-controlled facilities, freeports), **facilities and installation**, **conservation and maintenance**, **art handling and logistics/shipping**, framing, security. What does it actually cost per year to hold a serious collection, and how does that cost pressure push toward loan, gift, or sale? *(Research underway — this is a named gap Alina wants covered; likely a distinctive chapter no market report does well.)*

## 8. Art finance (liquidity without a decision)
Art-secured lending (~$33.9-40B in 2025, C7) and how it **postpones** a disposition rather than resolving it; art funds; fractional/shared ownership. Where finance meets the legacy question. *(Research underway; deepen C7.)*

## 9. Risk, value, and structure (insurance, appraisal, tax, legal)
- **Insurance** — the fine-art insurance market and how risk shapes what a collector can do.
- **Valuation and appraisal** — how art is valued for gift, estate, and sale, and why the number is contested.
- **Tax and structures** — estate and gift tax, private (operating) foundations as a museum vehicle, fractional gifts, deaccession law, cultural-property/provenance risk that shadows any collection entering an institution.
*(Research underway. Matthew Erskine's private-operating-foundation framing and the Center for Art Law brief are seeds.)*

## 10. Institutional economics (the receiving end — why museums say no)
Museum finances and endowment models; why museums increasingly decline gifts (space, budget, fit, storage already overcrowded); deaccessioning trends and law; the economics of museum construction and closure. The supply side of "what becomes of it" is only half the story; this is the other half. *(Research underway.)*
