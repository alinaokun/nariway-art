### 2. The most important finding — why demand rises while supply falls

Your Deloitte observation is real and important.

The 2025 Deloitte Private/ArtTactic report says 51% of wealth managers now offer art-related services, versus 63% in 2023, even as client demand has risen. It explicitly describes three strategies now appearing: full integration, complete opt-out where the economics or strategic fit are poor, and a **hybrid model relying on outside art professionals**, particularly among family offices.

Reported summaries of the underlying survey put client requests at **65% in 2025 versus 44% in 2023**, while provision fell to 51%, including approximately 50% for private banks and 52% for family offices.

#### Why the apparent contradiction exists

It is not because collectors stopped needing the services.

It is because art is a terrible capability for many wealth-management organizations to build internally.

Art requires specialists by category. Valuation involves liability and professional standards. Transactions create AML, sanctions and provenance risk. Insurance is specialized. Tax consequences differ according to the purpose of the valuation and transfer. Art does not produce conventional recurring AUM fees. And acquisition advice can create conflicts because the party recommending the object may also benefit from its transaction. Deloitte explicitly characterizes one of the emerging strategies as opting out because art does not align well enough with the business or lacks a compelling business case.

Yet ignoring the asset is increasingly difficult. In the 2025 Art Basel/UBS collector survey, HNW collectors reported an average **20% of wealth allocated to art**, rising to 28% among those with more than $50 million in assets.

So the family CFO faces a basic problem:

**“Twenty percent of this family's wealth sits outside everything our normal systems were designed to manage.”**

That is different from wanting an art historian on staff.

#### What the hybrid model actually looks like

Operationally, the family office or private bank remains the lead wealth relationship. It understands the family's trusts, balance sheet, liquidity requirements, tax situation and succession plan.

It then brings in specialists when needed:

**family office → independent coordinator/art specialist → qualified appraiser + insurer + attorney + lender + conservator + logistics company + auction/private-sale specialist + museum/philanthropy specialist.**

Bank of America describes essentially this arrangement: the family office coordinates financial and estate considerations but may bring in outside art expertise for market evaluation and due diligence.

ARTE Generali operates another version: it owns the insurance relationship while giving collectors access to outside restorers, shippers, storage specialists, appraisers, auction houses, galleries and other specialists.

That distinction matters enormously for your idea.

**The gap is not necessarily another specialist. It may be the person who makes the specialists work together.**

### 3. Where I think the actual gap exists

There is a recurring missing function across this ecosystem.

Consider a collector with $15 million of art.

The estate attorney understands trusts and taxation but does not maintain the object-level collection database.

The CPA understands tax but probably doesn't know whether the 2017 appraisal is still relevant for the required purpose.

The wealth manager understands liquidity but doesn't know which works are liquid or desirable.

The appraiser produces values but isn't necessarily managing the estate strategy.

The insurer knows the insured schedule but doesn't know the collector's succession wishes.

The art advisor understands the artists and market but may be commercially involved in buying or selling.

The collection-management software knows where the works are but cannot decide whether the family should donate, sell, borrow against or transfer them.

**Nobody automatically owns the whole problem.**

That is the strongest evidence I found for your thesis. It is mostly visible through how incumbents describe themselves rather than through one convenient survey question saying “we need a collection CFO.”

The industry's direction reinforces it. WAG's 2025 combination of a traditional appraisal/advisory business with Artory's data infrastructure is effectively a bet that collection ownership increasingly requires **data + valuation + stewardship**, not merely connoisseurship.

Art Basel recently devoted an article specifically to the “hidden work” of cataloguing and preserving collections, noting that 84% of the HNW collectors surveyed in 2025 had inherited art.

So I would distinguish the opportunities this way:

| Opportunity | Attractiveness for you | Why |
|---|---:|---|
| Acquisition advisor | Low | Your disadvantage in primary-market access and dealer relationships matters enormously. |
| Standalone appraiser | Low–medium | Possible, but requires substantial art-specific expertise/credentialing and is increasingly technology-exposed. |
| Collection manager/cataloguer | Low | Real need but CMS software commoditizes much of the administrative work. |
| Art-lending broker | Medium | Finance fit and attractive economics, but narrow and transaction-dependent. |
| Art estate consultant | High | Financial competence matters; episodic but high-value problem. |
| Collection CFO / stewardship advisor | **Highest** | Uses finance, project coordination, decision analysis and art knowledge without requiring you to pretend to be the world's expert on Richter versus Rothko. |
| Independent collection wind-down advisor | High | Major pain, real financial stakes, referral-friendly and closely tied to estates. |
| Philanthropic disposition specialist | Medium–high | Attractive adjacent wedge but requires tax/legal partnerships and institutional relationships. |

The **most valuable deliverable isn't an appraisal**.

It is a decision framework saying:

> Here are the 147 objects. Here is what we know and don't know. Here are current values and insurance gaps. Here is where ownership sits. Here is what each heir wants. Here is what could plausibly be sold, donated or retained. Here are the tax, liquidity and philanthropic implications requiring counsel. Here are the specialists needed. Here is the sequence in which these decisions should be made.

That is CFO work applied to a collection.

### 4. Economics — can this actually produce meaningful income?

This is where the evidence becomes thin.

There is **no reliable public database of independent art-advisory revenue or margins**. Private firms rarely publish prices, and firms mixing acquisition commissions, lending, valuations and collection services make comparison especially difficult.

Traditional acquisition advisors commonly receive transaction commissions, sometimes around 10–20%, while independent appraisal work commonly falls around $150–$500+ per hour. One current market guide puts normal USPAP-compliant appraisal work at $150–$500/hour; another reports straightforward single-object formal appraisals around $1,000–$2,000.

There is anecdotal evidence that collection-management engagements have historically been priced as annual retainers, hourly/project work or occasionally related to collection value, although percentage-of-value pricing is much less defensible for appraisal assignments because it conflicts with valuation independence.

It satisfies your preference for relatively few clients.

### 5. Do not confuse valuation analysis with formal appraisal

This is one of the most important strategic distinctions.

You can analyze collection values, maintain valuation histories, model scenarios and coordinate appraisers.

But issuing qualified valuations for tax and certain legal purposes is a separate profession.

For charitable contributions over $5,000, IRS rules generally require a qualified appraisal by a qualified appraiser. Donations of art for which a deduction of $20,000 or more is claimed generally require the appraisal to be attached to the return. The IRS Art Advisory Panel reviews significant valuations in income, estate and gift-tax cases.

That is why I would initially position yourself as the **client's financial coordinator**, not the person signing the appraisal.

An appraiser cannot simply be a financial analyst who knows valuation theory. A Warhol, 17th-century Dutch painting, Chinese porcelain and Cartier bracelet require entirely different specialist knowledge.

This is another reason not to make appraisal your wedge.

You would spend years turning your strongest advantage — senior financial judgment — into an apprenticeship in somebody else's profession.

### 7. Regulation

#### United States

As of August 2026, the United States still does **not** have the UK-style comprehensive AML regime covering ordinary art-market participants generally. Treasury's 2022 art-market study identified art advisors, intermediaries and art-backed lenders as possible money-laundering vulnerabilities and recommended possible AML/CFT measures, but the US regulatory framework has remained much lighter than the UK/EU regime.

The AML risk is nevertheless real. Treasury specifically cited anonymity, intermediaries, shell companies, high values and art-collateralized lending as vulnerabilities.

#### United Kingdom

The UK is much stricter. A business trading in or acting as an intermediary in the purchase or sale of art in qualifying transactions must register as an Art Market Participant under the Money Laundering Regulations. The threshold is €10,000 equivalent for a transaction or series of linked transactions. Obligations include customer due diligence, records and suspicious-activity controls.

This matters if an advisor actually intermediates transactions. A pure planning/coordination practice has a cleaner regulatory profile than one negotiating art sales.

#### EU

EU AML legislation similarly brought art-market traders and intermediaries into the regulated perimeter for relevant transactions around the €10,000 threshold, with implementation occurring through individual member states.

#### Other licenses

Giving advice specifically about **art**, which is tangible property rather than a security, does not ordinarily make someone an SEC investment adviser. The SEC definition revolves around compensated advice concerning **securities**.

But things change if you begin recommending interests in art funds, fractionalized/tokenized investment products or other securities.

Likewise, merely helping a client organize information for an insurance review is different from actually selling, soliciting or negotiating insurance. State insurance producer licensing applies to people undertaking those activities.

Your cleanest structure therefore is:

**advise and coordinate; do not practice law, issue tax advice, issue formal qualified appraisals, sell insurance or securities, or hold client money.**

### 8. The soft barrier is much more serious than the formal one

This is the strongest case against easy entry.

The art market runs on trust.

Citi proudly says its art team comes from major museums, galleries and auction houses. Gurr Johns' senior personnel biographies similarly show long auction-house, museum and specialist careers.

That is not cosmetic.

A collector deciding whether a Picasso is properly attributed, whether a Richter is sellable privately, whether a gallery relationship matters or whether an auction estimate is realistic wants someone who has lived inside that market.

You cannot credibly compete there immediately.

There **are** finance-to-art precedents. Ralph DeLuca, for example, moved from Wall Street into collecting and eventually art advisory, although his practice is transaction/connoisseurship-oriented rather than the administrative model under discussion. More revealing is Winston Artory Group's choice of Peter Loukas, whose background includes finance and fintech, as president alongside art specialists Elizabeth von Habsburg and Nanne Dekking.

The lesson is not that finance credentials replace art expertise.

It is that finance expertise becomes valuable **when it is paired with rather than substituted for art expertise**.

That would be your operating model.

### 9. Technology is a serious threat to the bottom half of this idea

Software already handles:

cataloguing, locations, provenance fields, appraisal histories, images, documents, insurance schedules, reports, exhibition histories and basic collection records.

Artlogic, Collector Systems, Artwork Archive and others sell these capabilities directly. Entry pricing can be extraordinarily cheap relative to UHNW advisory fees — roughly $29/month at Artwork Archive, approximately $85 at Collector Systems and roughly $115–$135 at Artlogic in one published comparison.

AI is moving into valuation as well. The FT profiled Appraisal Bureau, which uses technology to ingest many more market signals and produce valuations faster than traditional appraisal workflows. Its founder described conventional appraisal engagements taking months and costing thousands for relatively limited analysis. Gurr Johns' Harry Smith nevertheless argued that specialist human judgment remains necessary for quality and context.

I would expect AI to heavily reduce the value of:

**data gathering → auction-comparable searches → object categorization → catalogue creation → insurance-list maintenance → routine valuation updates → first-pass provenance research → document extraction.**

I would expect it to have far less effect on:

**family decision-making → negotiating disagreement among heirs → deciding disposition strategy → judgment about which specialists to trust → coordinating attorney/appraiser/insurer/lender → evaluating conflicting sale proposals → museum negotiations → unusual tax/estate situations → managing a deceased collector's chaotic records.**

That gives you a simple ten-year rule:

**Do not charge UHNW rates for work software will soon perform nearly automatically. Charge for judgment and orchestration.**

### 10. The wealth transfer is real — and more interesting than the headline number

Deloitte estimates **$992 billion of art and collectibles will change hands over the next decade**.

The UBS collector evidence provides substance behind it.

In 2025, **84% of surveyed HNW collectors had inherited artworks**. Inherited works represented about **31% of collection value** and 29% of works held. Eighty percent said they intended eventually to pass their collections to children or spouses.

But inherited art does not simply stay in families.

The 2024 survey found that estate taxes were a motivation for **47% of collectors who sold or donated inherited works**. Space was another major reason heirs disposed of art.

So inheritance triggers a cluster of simultaneous decisions:

**What is it? What is it worth? Who owns it? Who wants it? Where will it go? Can the estate afford to keep it? Should anything be donated? What is the tax basis? Which works should be sold? Who should sell them? Are the records adequate? Is insurance still correct?**

Traditional estate planning deals exceptionally well with the legal ownership of assets.

It does not automatically solve these object-level operational questions.

This is why I think **the life-event wedge is stronger than the collector-category wedge.**

Do not specialize initially in “contemporary art collectors.”

Specialize in:

**collections approaching a transition.**

### 11. Geography

The US is by far the logical primary market.

The US accounted for roughly **44% of global art sales by value in 2025**, with $26 billion of sales. The UK and China followed, while France remained fourth.

Within the United States, New York is overwhelmingly the most useful base because it concentrates the auction houses, banks, insurers, specialist law firms, appraisers, museums and advisors you would need as partners.

But I would **not** think of your prospective clients as New York collectors.

I would think of the business as:

**New York expertise serving nationally distributed wealthy families.**

That naturally supports the travel pattern you described.

The underexploited geography may actually be outside Manhattan: wealthy families in Florida, New Jersey, Connecticut, Boston, Washington, Philadelphia, Texas and California whose estate attorney or family office encounters a substantial collection without having a sophisticated local art-advisory infrastructure.

### 13. Adjacent opportunities

#### Corporate collections

Possible, but I would deprioritize it. Corporate work brings budgets, procurement processes, committees and recurring sales effort. It violates too many of your stated preferences.

#### Artist estates and foundations

Interesting but deceptively specialized. Artist estates involve catalogues raisonnés, intellectual property, authentication, archival practices, licensing, exhibitions and artist-market development. That is almost a separate profession.

#### Museum-adjacent advisory

Useful as an ecosystem and credibility builder rather than your core revenue engine. Museum budgets are poor compared with private wealth.

#### Art-secured lending origination

More interesting. Sotheby's lends from $1 million to $250 million, while Athena, Citi and others compete for borrowers.

A trusted advisor who discovers liquidity needs could introduce business to lenders. But I would treat this as ancillary. Compensation arrangements could introduce conflicts and regulatory questions, and you do not want clients wondering whether every recommendation is designed to create a financing fee.

#### Insurance-side advisory

Very attractive as a referral partnership. Much less attractive as your business itself unless you want producer licensing and insurance economics.

#### Private operating foundations

Interesting only later. The structuring, governance and tax work belongs principally to attorneys and tax professionals.
