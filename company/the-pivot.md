# The Pivot — why Alina left future-of-work research (private note)

*Personal, 2026-08-18. Whether any of this becomes public is TBD, Alina's call. Saved because it is the real reason Nariway exists, and it is more honest and more interesting than a market-opportunity story. NOT public copy, not for the website or Substack without Alina's explicit decision. If it ever goes public it fits Artobiography's personal, reflective register, never marketing.*

## The note (working draft)
> For six years, I researched the future of work. I wrote about it, built projects around it, and spent thousands of hours trying to understand what was happening to work and what might come next.
>
> Eventually, I realized I needed to ask myself a much simpler question. Borrowing from Marie Kondo, does this work still bring me joy?
>
> The answer was no.
>
> In fact, it had become almost the opposite. The more time I spent studying the problems surrounding work, the more discouraged I felt. The subject that had once made me curious was now making me unhappy.
>
> That was difficult to admit because I had invested six years in it. There was research, writing, websites, projects, relationships, and an enormous amount of work. Walking away felt like abandoning something I had spent years building.
>
> But the time already invested wasn't a reason to keep investing more.
>
> So I stopped.
>
> I started paying attention instead to the subjects I found myself returning to without obligation. Art was one of them. I wanted to visit museums. I wanted to learn about collections. I wanted to understand the people behind them. I found myself asking questions simply because I wanted to know the answers.
>
> That difference mattered.
>
> I didn't leave the future of work because I had answered all my questions. I left because I no longer wanted to spend my life asking them.

## Why this matters, and where it might go
- **It is the authentic answer to "why art collections, after six years studying work?"** More credible than a discovered-a-market story, and the honest answer is the better one. If anyone ever asks, this is it.
- **The distinction to preserve:** she did not conclude the future-of-work research was wrong, unimportant, or unsuccessful. She concluded it was no longer how she wanted to spend her time. Keep that precise.
- **The deeper idea, worth a possible future essay: intellectual sunk cost, the identity trap.** We apply sunk-cost thinking to business but rarely to intellectual identity. The better known you become for something, the harder it is to give yourself permission to stop caring about it. This rhymes with her earlier work, which questioned why people stay in careers because of prior investment. Eventually she had to make that same decision about her own body of work.

## The connection to Nariway (the reason to keep this close)
The pivot is the **personal version of the exact decision Nariway advises on.** A collector who clings to "I must keep it intact, I spent 40 years building it" is caught in the same sunk-cost and identity trap Alina escaped ("I must keep researching work, I spent six years"). Her own pivot gives her genuine, earned empathy for the collector's hardest emotional barrier: letting go of what you built well enough to decide *freely* what its future should be. That is not a coincidence, and it may be the deepest reason she is suited to this work. The subject of Nariway and the story of its founder are the same story.
