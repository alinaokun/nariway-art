# Artobiography — Editorial Candidate Packet (v0.1)

*Raw material for an editorial decision. NOT drafts, NOT a format recommendation. 20 story candidates mined from the existing Nariway vault, each judged editorially, with uncertainty preserved. Prepared 2026-08-16.*

---

## Method & coverage note

I mined the coded case corpus (`cases/*.md` — 50 case files carry `type: case`, of which **47 are folded into the master synthesis** in `cases/report-dataset.md`), the ~70–80-entry lead pool in `cases/candidate-universe.md` (including §2 distressed/closed/dispersed, §6 abandoned/changed-plans, and §8 corporate), the pattern synthesis and per-case coded rows in `cases/report-dataset.md`, `research/learnings-log.md`, `research/market-intelligence.md`, and the CRM frameworks in `crm/partners/` and `crm/prospects/`. Everything below is grounded in what those files actually say; where the research is silent I write "not in the research" or flag an inference explicitly.

**Three coverage limits shaped candidate selection, and you should read the packet knowing them.** (1) **WebFetch has been blocked for six consecutive research runs**, so ~18 of the 47 coded cases (including de la Cruz, Fisher Landau, Marciano, Vogel, Yuz, Credit Suisse→UBS, Rubin, Pearlman) rest entirely on WebSearch-snippet synthesis tagged `secondary` — the underlying primary documents (990s, wills, sale results, court dockets) exist but have not been read directly. (2) **Several of the richest-story cases are one-line stub files** (`terra.md`, `corcoran-gallery.md`, `di-rosa.md`, `souls-grown-deep.md`, `neue-galerie.md`) whose real coded detail lives in `report-dataset.md`, not the case file — so those candidates draw on the dataset rows, and original reporting would start closer to scratch. (3) **Two strong story leads exist only as candidate-universe rows with no case file at all** — the **Ganz** Picasso auction and the **Ty Warner** naming-rights litigation — so they are included as leads, not researched cases, and flagged as such. Financial-durability figures are `unknown` for most cases (the true endowment sits in IRS Schedule D, which is not pullable at scale), so I have leaned on human/governance material, which is far richer, rather than money.

A note on **access** and **visual** fields: the vault rarely states outright that a person is interviewable or that a specific image exists. Where I say a founder/director/descendant is a possible interview, I am inferring from a "Living" marker in `candidate-universe.md` or a named role in a case file; where I say imagery likely exists, I am inferring from a named architect, a named auction house (whose catalogues carry lot photography), or a described building/site. **Every such inference is flagged.** I invented no people, images, or facts.

## The 20 candidates

### 1. The Barnes Foundation — the deed that a court rewrote
1. **Collection / person / institution:** Albert C. Barnes and The Barnes Foundation (Merion → Philadelphia, PA).
2. **The story:** Barnes made an antiseptic fortune, assembled one of the world's great Impressionist/Post-Impressionist concentrations (181 Renoirs, 69 Cézannes), and in 1922 chartered it as an *educational foundation* with an Indenture of Trust that forbade ever moving, selling, or loaning a work, froze the wall arrangements, and limited public access. Decades after his 1951 death, the trustees argued the foundation was financially non-viable in Merion and petitioned to move it; in 2004 a court applied *cy pres* and approved relocation to a new Philadelphia building (opened 2012), expanding the board from 5 to 15 and diluting the control Barnes had vested in a historically Black university.
3. **The human center:** Barnes himself (a combative, ideological collector) writing constraints meant to bind forever; the trustees and major funders (Pew, Lenfest, Annenberg) who undid them; Judge Stanley Ott, who signed the order.
4. **The tension:** Can a dead man's iron-clad instructions survive contact with the institution's own survival? Whose intent wins?
5. **The turn:** The foundation thrives today (~$245M net assets FY2025) — but only *because* the court broke nearly every access and location term the founder wrote. Durability was purchased by overriding donor intent.
6. **The ending / current state:** Thriving as a public museum on the Parkway; in 2023 the court went further and permitted temporary loans, another deviation. Coded `pivoted`, not simply "thriving."
7. **Why it could be great:** The canonical demonstration that a documented, aggressive donor-intent instrument is not self-enforcing. High drama, real villains-and-heroes ambiguity, a court record, and a still-live question every collector faces.
8. **What Nariway could learn:** Constraints written to bind forever can be judicially rewritten in the name of viability — "lock it down" is not a durability strategy on its own. Governance design and financial viability, not deed language, decide the real outcome.
9. **Evidence strength:** Strong. Case file is spot-verified with primary FY2025 990 figures (net assets $245,027,793; expenses $30,073,540). What's uncertain: the verbatim indenture text (press paraphrase only), the founding-endowment dollar figure at his 1951 death, the exact Dec-2004 ruling date. Named primary sources to strengthen: the full Indenture of Trust, the Montgomery County Orphans' Court opinions (*In re Barnes Foundation*), Barnes's probate record. (Source: `cases/barnes.md`, `cases/report-dataset.md` #26.)
10. **Access opportunity:** No living founder. The litigation record is public; "Friends of the Barnes" appellants and the *Art of the Steal* filmmakers are named in the vault as parties (inference: potentially interviewable). No current trustees named in the vault.
11. **Visual potential (inferred):** Both purpose-built buildings named (Paul Cret 1925; Tod Williams Billie Tsien 2012); the salon-style ensembles were reproduced wall-for-wall as a court condition — strong architectural/installation imagery almost certainly exists (inference from vault descriptions, not confirmed).
12. **Research readiness:** Strong on the arc; one court-docket pull away from being deeply reported. The narrative spine (litigation + cy pres) is already assembled in the case file.

### 2. Terra Museum of American Art — the widow, the board, and the vanishing museum
1. **Collection / person / institution:** Daniel J. Terra and the Terra Museum of American Art (Chicago, IL).
2. **The story:** Terra, a wealthy industrialist (and Reagan's ambassador-at-large for cultural affairs), built a single-collector museum of American art to 1945. Its only original directors were Terra and his son. After his 1996 death, a governance vacuum became open legal war between the board and his widow, Judith Terra; the museum closed in 2004, and core works went to the Art Institute of Chicago. The entity became a grantmaking foundation.
3. **The human center:** Judith Terra (the contested driver of the museum's fate) against a board with no succession plan; the founder who left a $450M-scale estate but no working governance.
4. **The tension:** How does a museum with roughly $450M behind it simply disappear?
5. **The turn:** Money was never the problem — governance was. Terra had a fortune and still failed, because control was never designed to outlive him. It is the sample's clean counter-example to "endowment = survival."
6. **The ending / current state:** Museum dissolved 2004; core collection at the Art Institute; a successor grantmaking foundation holds ~$614.5M (FY2025) — which is emphatically *not* museum durability.
7. **Why it could be great:** A courtroom-drama shape (widow vs. board), a counterintuitive engine (rich museums can die of governance), and a genuinely surprising ending (the "museum" now a checkbook). Human conflict at the center.
8. **What Nariway could learn:** The single sharpest case for the thesis that governance design — independent board + succession locked before death — predicts survival better than money. Founder-sole control is the failure mode.
9. **Evidence strength:** Medium on documentation, high on narrative clarity. The case *file* (`cases/terra.md`) is a one-line stub; the coded detail lives in `cases/report-dataset.md` #7 (spot-verified, primary Illinois appellate opinion + ProPublica). **Uncertain / flagged:** a reported "50-year stay-in-Illinois" settlement term appears on Wikipedia but is *absent* from the appellate opinion — unresolved. Museum-era financials/attendance unknown. Primary sources to obtain: the full Illinois appellate opinion (1013152), the litigation docket, the AIC transfer agreement.
10. **Access opportunity (inferred):** Judith Terra's status is not stated in the vault. Trusts/estates attorneys and the Illinois AG's office were parties to the donor-intent litigation (inference: a route to the legal record, not confirmed interviewees).
11. **Visual potential (inferred):** A Michigan Avenue museum building existed; American-art works now at the AIC are publicly held (inference: imagery likely obtainable, not confirmed in vault).
12. **Research readiness:** Thin as a file, strong as a story spine. Needs original reporting from the court record up, but the shape and stakes are fully established.

### 3. The Corcoran Gallery of Art — 126 years, then divided among the winners
1. **Collection / person / institution:** W.W. Corcoran and the Corcoran Gallery of Art (Washington, DC).
2. **The story:** One of the oldest US private-collection museums (1869). After ~126 years, its endowment eroded below roughly half a year of operating coverage; in 2014 a court dissolved it by *cy pres*. Its 19,493 objects were distributed — 8,300+ to the National Gallery as "The Corcoran Collection at the NGA," ~10,862 to 22 other DC-area institutions — and its school and building went to George Washington University.
3. **The human center:** The "Save the Corcoran" group who fought the dissolution in court; Judge Robert Okun, who approved it; a board presiding over a slow-motion funding collapse.
4. **The tension:** Watching a beloved civic institution run out of runway in slow motion — and the fight over whether it had to end.
5. **The turn:** The endowment slide (from $23.2M in 2009 to $14.1M in 2013) was the real signal, but the *book* net assets were rising during the failure because incoming money was restricted acquisition funds — health and distress looked alike on the balance sheet.
6. **The ending / current state:** Dissolved 2014; collection dispersed across the NGA and 22 institutions; building absorbed by GWU. A "survived-the-founder-then-dissolved-on-money" case.
7. **Why it could be great:** A grand, civic-scale death — bigger than a single-collector story — with a public court fight, a named opposition movement, and a subtle, teachable financial irony.
8. **What Nariway could learn:** The slow-clock failure mode: funding collapse over decades, invisible on headline net assets because restricted acquisition money masks operating erosion. A collection can be "rich on paper" while dying.
9. **Evidence strength:** Strong. `report-dataset.md` #8 is spot-verified with primary ProPublica FY2014 figures and Art Newspaper distribution counts. Case file (`cases/corcoran-gallery.md`) is a stub pointing to the dataset. Uncertain: year-by-year endowment detail beyond the two anchor points. Primary sources: the 1869 deed of trust, the dissolution ruling, the NGA accession list.
10. **Access opportunity (inferred):** No living founder. "Save the Corcoran" members and dissolution-case parties are public (inference: interviewable). NGA and GWU stewards named institutionally, not individually, in the vault.
11. **Visual potential (inferred):** The Flagg Building (1897) and dispersed works are well documented publicly (inference; not confirmed in vault).
12. **Research readiness:** Strong on the arc, needs primary-document reporting to deepen. The financial irony is already articulated in the dataset.

### 4. The de la Cruz Collection — closed the Sunday she died, never reopened
1. **Collection / person / institution:** Rosa and Carlos de la Cruz and the de la Cruz Collection (Miami, FL).
2. **The story:** The de la Cruzes built a free, 30,000-sq-ft private contemporary-art museum in Miami's Design District (opened 2009), with real depth in Ana Mendieta and Félix González-Torres. Rosa died on a Sunday in February 2024 — the museum's usual closed day. Her husband Carlos, the surviving co-owner, simply never reopened it and, within months, consigned the collection to Christie's; the first sale alone (May 2024) made $34.4M. The building was sold separately to ICA Miami for $25M.
3. **The human center:** Carlos de la Cruz — living, and the sole decision-maker as surviving spouse — choosing immediate liquidation over continuation.
4. **The tension:** A free museum that a city loved, gone within a year of a death, with no board, will, or plan to slow it down.
5. **The turn:** By Carlos's own paraphrase of Rosa's philosophy, art "will either go to an institution, which might not be able to show them; to your kids, who might not want them; or to an auction house" — and he chose the third. A deliberate, unsentimental choice, made fast, and controversial in Miami.
6. **The ending / current state:** Collection dispersed at auction; building now doubles ICA Miami's footprint. Closed-dispersed.
7. **Why it could be great:** The fastest, cleanest closure in the whole sample, a vivid human decision, a real city-loses-something ache, and a quotable philosophy at its heart. The surviving-spouse-as-first-decider theme (a Nariway obsession) made flesh.
8. **What Nariway could learn:** With no independent board or instrument, a single grieving spouse holds total discretion — and "sell" wins by default when no one has planned otherwise. The surviving spouse is the first decision-maker (market-intelligence C8).
9. **Evidence strength:** Medium. Secondary-only (WebFetch blocked); the case file is thorough but every figure is `secondary`. **Unresolved:** the first sale ($34.4M) already exceeded the ~$30M pre-sale estimate for the *whole* collection — a gap the case flags, not resolves; whether any 501(c)(3) ever existed is unconfirmed. Primary sources: Miami-Dade probate for any Rosa de la Cruz will/trust, Christie's full multi-tranche results.
10. **Access opportunity:** **Carlos de la Cruz is living** and has given press interviews (the vault cites Miami Herald/WLRN coverage) — a plausible, high-value interview (inference from his quoted statements). ICA Miami leadership (building buyer) named institutionally.
11. **Visual potential (inferred):** Christie's sale catalogues imply professional lot photography of major works; the Design District building is documented (architect John Marquette named). Inference, not confirmed.
12. **Research readiness:** Strong story, medium documentation. One probate pull and a Carlos interview away from a deeply reported piece; the arc is fully mapped in the case file.

### 5. Emily Fisher Landau — she closed her own museum, then $424M sold, and ~1,000 works vanished from the record
1. **Collection / person / institution:** Emily Fisher Landau and the Fisher Landau Center for Art (Long Island City, NY).
2. **The story:** Fisher Landau began collecting in the late 1960s (reportedly funded partly by an insurance payout after a jewelry theft), built a major postwar/contemporary American collection, and opened a museum in a converted parachute factory in 1991. In 2010 she gave ~367–419 works to the Whitney. In 2017 — while alive and in control, age ~96 — she quietly closed the museum. After her 2023 death at 102, her estate sold ~111 lots at Sotheby's for $424.7M (a Picasso alone hit $139.4M).
3. **The human center:** Fisher Landau herself — a Whitney patron who made three very different disposition choices across 13 years — and the unnamed estate/executor process that chose the auction over another gift.
4. **The tension:** Why give hundreds of works away, then sell the rest for a record sum — and what happened to the museum in between?
5. **The turn:** The arithmetic is the story: ~111 auction lots made 5–8x the dollar value of the far larger Whitney *gift* — and the 2010 gift plus 2023 sale account for at most ~530 works of a collection reported at 1,200–1,500. **The fate of ~700–1,000 works is undocumented in any source found.** A genuine mystery sits inside a tidy-looking legacy.
6. **The ending / current state:** Museum closed 2017; collection split between a museum gift and a record-setting estate auction; a residual foundation now runs near-zero, consistent with passing through sale proceeds. Closed-dispersed.
7. **Why it could be great:** A rare "orderly-looking" failure with a real unsolved puzzle at its center (the missing works), a record-breaking sale, and a founder who controlled her own ending — the opposite of Terra's chaos.
8. **What Nariway could learn:** Founder-controlled closure while living is a distinct, orderly failure mode — but the *ultimate* disposition still ran through an estate with apparently no locked plan, so the biggest value went to auction "by default." Plan the collection's fate, not just the building's.
9. **Evidence strength:** Medium. Secondary-only; financial figures from search snippets. **Named gaps:** the will (unread — "the single highest-value primary source missing"), the reason for the 2017 closure (unsourced), collection-size reconciliation, and the ~700–1,000 missing works. Primary sources: her will/probate (NY or Palm Beach), the foundation's governing docs, Sotheby's full results, the Whitney checklist.
10. **Access opportunity (inferred):** Family co-trustees named in the vault — Candia Fisher (daughter, "helps manage" the Center, Whitney committees) and Winston Fisher — are plausible interviews (inference from named roles). No confirmation of willingness.
11. **Visual potential (inferred):** Sotheby's *An Era Defined* catalogue implies extensive lot photography; the parachute-factory museum is described. Inference, not confirmed.
12. **Research readiness:** Strong story engine, medium evidence. The missing-works thread is a reportable investigation in itself; needs the will and Sotheby's results.

### 6. Marciano Art Foundation — dark within days of a union drive
1. **Collection / person / institution:** Maurice and Paul Marciano (Guess Jeans co-founders) and the Marciano Art Foundation (Los Angeles).
2. **The story:** The brothers opened a free contemporary museum in a converted 1961 Masonic Temple in 2017. In late October 2019, visitor-services staff (≈$14.25/hr, no benefits) announced a unionization drive. Within days the foundation ended its shows early, laid off the staff citing "low attendance," and by December declared the closure permanent — despite ~$50M in assets and both founders very much alive.
3. **The human center:** The laid-off gallery workers and their union; the Marciano brothers making a fast, board-thin decision.
4. **The tension:** Why does a museum with $50M in the bank and living founders go dark overnight — and was it really about attendance?
5. **The turn:** Not a money failure and not a succession failure — a labor-and-governance rupture. The foundation settled a class action ($205K + $70K fees); the NLRB opened an investigation. The "low attendance" rationale was contested (admission was free; the lobby reportedly still solicited submissions for a February show).
6. **The ending / current state:** After ~5 years dark (with the building lent to Gagosian and nonprofits in between), a quiet, scaled-down reopening began in 2024 with a first director hired. Coded `distressed` — the honest middle read.
7. **Why it could be great:** A labor story inside an art-world story, genuinely contested causation the piece can hold open rather than resolve, and a rare failure mode with no money or death behind it. Timely (art-institution labor).
8. **What Nariway could learn:** Failure modes are plural — a foundation can be well-capitalized, its founders alive, and still go dark on governance/labor grounds. Board-thin, founder-sole decision-making is the fragility.
9. **Evidence strength:** Medium. Secondary-only, but richly sourced across multiple outlets in the case file. **Unresolved:** layoff headcount (60 vs 70 vs 80), two inconsistent financial vintages, and whether the 2024 reopening is genuine recovery. Primary sources: 990-PF filings, the NLRB docket, the class-action settlement, the foundation bylaws.
10. **Access opportunity (inferred):** **Both founders living**; director Hanneke Skerath named (2024); AFSCME/DC36 organizers and named laid-off workers are public parties (inference: interviewable). No confirmation.
11. **Visual potential (inferred):** The Millard Sheets Masonic Temple conversion (architect Kulapat Yantrasast/wHY) and the Kusama/Party shows imply strong imagery. Inference.
12. **Research readiness:** Medium. Story fully mapped; needs the labor-side reporting (workers, NLRB) and a 990-PF pull to firm up.

### 7. The Vogel Collection — a postal clerk, a librarian, one salary, fifty states
1. **Collection / person / institution:** Dorothy and Herbert Vogel (New York → all 50 states).
2. **The story:** Herbert was a postal clerk; Dorothy a Brooklyn reference librarian. On his salary alone (they lived on hers), they built one of the great minimalist/conceptual collections — 4,000–5,000 works, befriending Sol LeWitt, Richard Tuttle, Lynda Benglis. Facing an apartment that could no longer hold it, they gave ~1,100 works to the National Gallery (1992), then designed "Fifty Works for Fifty States": exactly 50 works to one museum in every state (2008–09).
3. **The human center:** The Vogels themselves — the sample's only zero-wealth collectors, whose taste and discipline, not money, built something museum-scale.
4. **The tension:** How do two civil servants on modest salaries end up with a collection the National Gallery wants — and what do you *do* with it when you have no money to house it?
5. **The turn:** Their poverty *was* the strategy. With no wealth to build a museum, total dispersal into 51 existing institutions became the plan — and by construction it may be the most durable disposition in the entire dataset (no single point of failure, no balance sheet to erode), executed before either founder died.
6. **The ending / current state:** Thriving as distributed; works permanently inside 51 institutions. Herbert died 2012, Dorothy 2025.
7. **Why it could be great:** The most human, most counterintuitive, most *lovable* story in the vault — and it quietly demolishes the "art collecting is for the rich" frame. A gift to any reader.
8. **What Nariway could learn:** Governance beats endowment even at $0 endowment: dispersal into pre-endowed institutions is arguably the most durable model in the sample. Distinctiveness, not scale of wealth, is the north star.
9. **Evidence strength:** Medium-high on narrative, medium on numbers. Secondary-only, but the arc is well documented. **Gaps:** lifetime totals don't reconcile (the 1,100 + 2,500 tranches leave a several-hundred-to-1,000-work gap); any works retained at Dorothy's Nov-2025 death are not yet in coverage. Primary sources: the NGA gift agreements, a full lifetime acquisition accounting, Dorothy's obituaries.
10. **Access opportunity (inferred):** Both founders deceased (recently — Dorothy Nov 2025). The NGA, the NEA/IMLS 50-states program staff, and recipient-museum curators are named institutionally (inference: interviewable). A prior documentary about the Vogels exists in the culture (not cited in the vault — do not rely on it).
11. **Visual potential (inferred):** The couple, their crammed apartment, and works across 50 states are heavily documented publicly (inference; the vault does not itemize images).
12. **Research readiness:** Strong story, medium documentation. Needs a lifetime-acquisition reconciliation and fresh reporting, but the emotional and thematic core is fully in hand.

### 8. di Rosa Center — the vineyard-country collection that started selling itself, then stopped
1. **Collection / person / institution:** Rene and Veronica di Rosa and the di Rosa Center for Contemporary Art (Napa, CA).
2. **The story:** The di Rosas turned a 217-acre Napa property into a foundation and museum devoted specifically to Northern California / Bay Area art — 1,600+ works. After Rene's 2010 death it survived but drifted into distress; in 2019 it *proposed* deaccessioning, then **halted it in 2020** — no works were ever sold. In January 2026 the 217-acre campus was listed for $10.9M (collection excluded).
3. **The human center:** Rene di Rosa's fiercely regional vision, and the post-founder board trying to keep a land-heavy, thinly endowed institution alive.
4. **The tension:** A regional collection on beautiful, expensive land — can the mission outlast the cost of the ground it sits on?
5. **The turn:** The widely repeated "di Rosa sold a Bechtle" premise is **false** — the deaccession was announced and then reversed, $0 raised (the Bechtle in question is at the Whitney). The real story is land outrunning endowment, not a fire sale.
6. **The ending / current state:** Distressed and still operating; operating entity ran negative net assets (−$2.49M FY2024); campus listed for sale, collection retained. Unresolved.
7. **Why it could be great:** A live, unresolved story (rare — most cases have ended), a vivid setting (wine country), and a correction of a widely believed false narrative — catnip for a well-reported piece.
8. **What Nariway could learn:** Land and buildings are the durability trap for a thinly endowed regional thesis; "the campus is the liability" echoes the building-vs-collection question across the sample. A protected-in-perpetuity *land* trust did not protect the *collection*.
9. **Evidence strength:** Medium. `report-dataset.md` #9 is spot-verified (primary ProPublica figures; Art Newspaper correction of the false deaccession premise); case file is a stub. **Uncertain:** collection size (1,600 vs 2,200), the endowment figure, the campus-sale outcome (live). Primary sources: the Land Trust of Napa County agreement, the foundation 990s, the listing/sale record.
10. **Access opportunity (inferred):** Founders deceased. Board and staff are named institutionally; the story is live enough that current stewards may comment (inference).
11. **Visual potential (inferred):** A 217-acre Napa campus with outdoor sculpture and a residence — strong landscape/site imagery likely (inference).
12. **Research readiness:** Medium. Needs current on-the-ground reporting because the outcome is unfolding; the false-premise correction is already a reportable hook.

### 9. Souls Grown Deep — the collection built to disappear into the canon
1. **Collection / person / institution:** William Arnett and the Souls Grown Deep Foundation (Atlanta → national).
2. **The story:** Arnett assembled ~1,000+ works of Black vernacular art of the American South — Gee's Bend quilts, Thornton Dial, Lonnie Holley. Rather than build a monument museum, the foundation *disperses* the work into major institutions (500+ works by 110+ artists to 20+ museums since 2016: the Met, the de Young, the High), paired with advocacy and an artist resale-royalty program. It thrives past Arnett's 2020 death.
3. **The human center:** Arnett — a controversial, obsessive white collector-advocate for Black Southern artists (a fraught, debated relationship worth handling honestly) — and the artists and descendants whose work is entering the canon.
4. **The tension:** Can a collection's highest ambition be to *give itself away* into other institutions — and what does it mean that a white collector shaped this Black-vernacular canon?
5. **The turn:** The anti-monument. Distinctiveness and public benefit, not market value or a namesake building, are the entire thesis — and depletion by design is treated as success, not failure.
6. **The ending / current state:** Thriving as a dispersal-and-advocacy engine; endowment declining by design (net assets ~$3.18M, FY2025).
7. **Why it could be great:** A morally complex human center (Arnett is genuinely debated), a radical model (give it away), and living artists/descendants at the heart. Rich, contested, and current.
8. **What Nariway could learn:** Public benefit can be the explicit thesis; dispersal-into-the-canon is a legitimate, durable pathway that needs no building. Also a case study in the ethics of who assembles and controls a marginalized community's canon.
9. **Evidence strength:** Medium. `report-dataset.md` #1 is spot-verified with primary ProPublica figures and museum press; the case file (`cases/souls-grown-deep.md`) is a stub. There is also a **partner file** `crm/partners/Souls Grown Deep Foundation.md` (not read in full here) that may hold contact intelligence. Uncertain: founding size (1,200 vs 1,300). Primary sources: gift/purchase agreements, the resale-royalty program terms.
10. **Access opportunity (inferred, strong):** A dedicated CRM partner file exists (`crm/partners/Souls Grown Deep Foundation.md`), implying Nariway has or is building a relationship — a plausible route to the foundation, and via it to living artists and Arnett-family figures (inference; confirm in that file).
11. **Visual potential (inferred):** The works themselves (quilts, Dial's assemblages) are visually extraordinary and widely exhibited/published (inference).
12. **Research readiness:** Medium-strong. The dispersal model is fully mapped; the human/ethical reporting (Arnett, artists) is where original work is needed. The partner file may shorten the access path.

### 10. Budi Tek and the Yuz Museum — a dying collector's plan that never closed
1. **Collection / person / institution:** Budi Tek and Yuz Museum Shanghai / Yuz Foundation (Shanghai, China).
2. **The story:** Tek, an Indonesian-Chinese businessman and one of the foremost collectors of contemporary Chinese art, founded Yuz Museum in 2014. Diagnosed with pancreatic cancer, he spent his final years trying to secure the museum's future through a formal joint foundation with LACMA (from 2018). The cross-border structure never closed — reportedly undone by deteriorating US-China relations and pandemic delays. He died in March 2022 at 65.
3. **The human center:** Tek, racing his own illness to lock in a succession; his daughter, Justine Alexandria Tek, who became director of both the museum and the foundation.
4. **The tension:** A collector who did everything right — planned years ahead, sought an institutional partner — and still couldn't get it done in time. What happens then?
5. **The turn:** The plan failed, but the museum didn't. Informal family succession filled the gap; in 2023 (after his death) the museum built an entirely new venue. "Did the founder plan for succession" and "did succession actually work" turn out to be different questions.
6. **The ending / current state:** Stable, trending toward survival; daughter directing; new building opened 2023; programming continued with LACMA and Qatar Museums. Financial durability unmeasurable (no China 990-equivalent).
7. **Why it could be great:** A mortality-and-legacy story with a ticking clock, an international frame the vault otherwise lacks, geopolitics as a plot force, and a daughter's succession as the emotional resolution.
8. **What Nariway could learn:** Intent alone, without a *completed* instrument, doesn't secure a collection — but informal family succession can still catch it. Cross-border succession structures carry geopolitical risk US-only planning never sees. A distinct "attempted-and-blocked" governance outcome.
9. **Evidence strength:** Medium-low on facts, strong on shape. Secondary-only; **flagged errors** in the source synthesis (a "50 years collecting" figure is likely wrong; 2014 opening unconfirmed this session). Financials systematically unavailable (China regime). Primary sources: LACMA's original announcement and any statement on why it collapsed; Chinese foundation-registration filings; Yuz's own history page.
10. **Access opportunity (inferred, strong):** **Justine Alexandria Tek** is named as current director and has given an interview cited in the vault (artnet) — a plausible, high-value interview (inference). LACMA figures who negotiated the joint foundation are named institutionally.
11. **Visual potential (inferred):** Two museum buildings (original + 2023 Panlong Tiandi venue), a major contemporary collection — strong imagery likely (inference).
12. **Research readiness:** Medium. The human arc is compelling and mostly assembled; facts need a careful re-verification pass (several flagged errors) and the LACMA side reported out.

### 11. Credit Suisse → UBS — what happens to a bank's art when the bank itself disappears
1. **Collection / person / institution:** The Credit Suisse Art Collection, absorbed into UBS (Zürich). `origin: corporate`. *(Note: UBS is Alina's own former employer — a personal angle, flagged.)*
2. **The story:** In March 2023 Swiss authorities brokered an emergency, government-backed takeover of a distressed-but-not-bankrupt Credit Suisse by UBS (completed June 12, 2023). Credit Suisse's ~13,500-work collection — Swiss-heavy (Hodler, Vallotton), plus oddities like model ships inherited from its own 1980s First Boston deal — was absorbed whole into UBS's larger (~40,000-work) globally-curated collection. UBS is now working through a multi-year integration, reportedly selling some works to employees at a discount, proceeds to charity.
3. **The human center:** Mary Rozell (named in the vault as UBS's global head of the art collection), inheriting and triaging a rival bank's collection that doesn't match her own.
4. **The tension:** Two great corporate collections, mismatched in taste, language, and software, forced into one overnight — who and what survives the merger?
5. **The turn:** Not survival, not liquidation — messy absorption. The Credit Suisse collection persists as an *asset* but dies as an *identity*; the friction is curatorial incoherence, not money. Corporate collections layer through M&A the way family collections layer through marriages.
6. **The ending / current state:** Pivoted / still integrating ~3 years on. Whether UBS ever formally retires the "Credit Suisse Collection" name is unresolved.
7. **Why it could be great:** A live, high-profile financial event (the Credit Suisse rescue) told through its art — an unusual, citable, and Alina-adjacent angle on "what becomes of a collection." A named human (Rozell) doing the actual work.
8. **What Nariway could learn:** The corporate "what becomes of it" trigger is M&A/failure, not mortality — and the distressed-absorption cell behaves differently from bankruptcy (Lehman/Enron) or voluntary retreat (Deutsche Bank). A contrast class with maximal governance.
9. **Evidence strength:** Medium-low. Secondary and often single-sourced (dimsumdaily.hk, Zarastro Art). No 990-equivalent (Swiss). **Flagged:** the ~13,500 / 40,000 figures are single-sourced; UBS's own founding narrative is conflated with the PaineWebber lineage in press. Primary sources: UBS annual/CSR disclosure, the WSJ "UBS Likes Credit Suisse, Just Not All Its Artwork" piece, The Art Newspaper's 2023 coverage.
10. **Access opportunity (inferred, strong):** **Mary Rozell** is named and is a public figure; UBS is Alina's former employer (a personal network angle — flagged as inference/opportunity, not a confirmed door).

### 12. Grounds For Sculpture — the Johnson & Johnson heir who gave his park away while he was still alive

### 13. Marjorie Merriweather Post & Hillwood — the museum the Smithsonian gave back
1. **Collection / person / institution:** Marjorie Merriweather Post (Postum/General Foods heiress) and Hillwood Estate, Museum & Gardens (Washington, DC).
2. **The story:** Post built the largest collection of Russian imperial art outside Russia (plus 18th-c. French decorative arts) at her Washington estate. 4. **The tension:** What does it mean when even the Smithsonian says a gift is too expensive to accept?

6. **The ending / current state:** Thriving house museum, ~49 years on, across multiple directorships.
7. **Why it could be great:** A glamorous, specific character; a genuinely surprising beat (the Smithsonian returns a gift); and a Mar-a-Lago subplot that gives it contemporary resonance. "The museum says no" made literal.
8. **What Nariway could learn:** A museum can refuse a bequest when the endowment can't carry the operating burden — the "museum says no" thesis in its purest documented form (`decision-map`). Funding-gap, not taste or governance, is the friction.
9. **Evidence strength:** Strong on the arc. Spot-verified; net assets primary (FY2024 $389,682,862), the ~$200M endowment and $10M/$450K figures secondary. Uncertain: collection size, attendance, the true endowment (Schedule D). Primary sources: the Smithsonian Archives file on the return, Post's will and foundation instruments, the FY2024 990 PDF.

### 14. Ogden Museum — the founder who sued the university he gave it to

### 16. The Fisher Collection at SFMOMA — the museum they couldn't build, and the 100-year loan instead
1. **Collection / person / institution:** Doris and Donald Fisher (Gap Inc. co-founders) and SFMOMA (San Francisco).
2. **The story:** The Fishers assembled one of the largest US private collections of postwar/contemporary art. Their plan for a standalone museum in San Francisco's Presidio met community opposition and was dropped; in 2009 they instead placed 720+ works on a 100-year loan to SFMOMA (from an initial 25 years, renewable in 25-year increments), retaining ownership. Donald died weeks after the 2009 agreement; the collection anchored SFMOMA's 2016 expansion.
3. **The human center:** The Fishers, pivoting from a defeated vanity-museum dream to a novel century-long partnership; Doris, who lived to see the 2026 reinstallation and died weeks before it.
4. **The tension:** When the public won't let you build your museum, how do you still guarantee your collection a public life?
5. **The turn:** Defeat became innovation. The blocked Presidio museum forced the 100-year-loan structure — ownership stays with the family, public display is guaranteed for a century inside a major institution. It has already outlasted both founders.
6. **The ending / current state:** Thriving; dedicated Fisher galleries; full reinstallation April 2026, weeks before Doris's May 2026 death.
7. **Why it could be great:** A civic-conflict backstory (the Presidio fight), a genuinely inventive legacy instrument, and a poignant timing beat (Doris dying just before the reinstallation).
8. **What Nariway could learn:** The long-term loan is a powerful third way — public life without the cost and fragility of a standalone building, ownership retained. Community opposition can be the mother of a better structure.
9. **Evidence strength:** Strong on structure. Spot-verified; the 100-year term and 720+ works are primary (SFMOMA). Uncertain: private-agreement terms, any care endowment, the 720-loaned vs ~1,100-total reconciliation. Primary sources: the 2009 loan agreement, which Fisher entity holds title.

11. **Visual potential (inferred, strong):** Blue-chip works in the Snøhetta expansion's dedicated galleries — extensively documented (inference).

### 18. The Pearlman Foundation — a 49-year loan ends in a three-museum circulating consortium
1. **Collection / person / institution:** The Henry and Rose Pearlman Foundation (New York; loaned at Princeton for 49 years).
2. **The story:** Henry Pearlman built a tight, Cézanne-centered Impressionist/Post-Impressionist collection (Degas, Manet, Matisse, Modigliani, Van Gogh, Soutine). His foundation (est. 1955) loaned it to the Princeton University Art Museum for 49 years (1976–2025). In August 2025 the trustees ended the single-host loan and permanently split 63 works across three museums — 29 to the Brooklyn Museum, 28 to MoMA, 6 to LACMA — with the three agreeing to a **standing mutual-loan obligation** to lend to one another.
3. **The human center:** The foundation's trustees (decades after Henry's 1974 death) choosing a genuinely novel architecture over a simple gift or an auction.
4. **The tension:** After half a century in one home, how do you divide a beloved, coherent collection without breaking it?
5. **The turn:** They invented a third thing — not one recipient, not a scattering, but three independent owners permanently bound to circulate works among themselves. A self-perpetuating loan network rather than a one-time distribution.
6. **The ending / current state:** Dispersed (physical transfer after a 2026 LACMA farewell show); durable by construction, with a novel ongoing mechanism.
7. **Why it could be great:** A structural innovation worth explaining, a coherent jewel-box collection, and a quiet, deliberate anti-auction ending — a rare "they did it thoughtfully" story.
8. **What Nariway could learn:** Dispersal is not one mechanism — Pearlman's circulating consortium is a third architecture (vs. Vogel's scattering and Daskalopoulos's shared title). A model for keeping a coherent collection publicly alive without a building or a single owner.
9. **Evidence strength:** Medium-low. Secondary-only; **the correct EIN was never even located** (only similarly-named unrelated foundations surfaced). **Unresolved:** whether 63 works is the whole holding; whether the mutual-loan arrangement is legally binding or a stated intention; Rose Pearlman's death date; whether the 1955 founding (19 years pre-death) was a deliberate lock-in. Primary sources: the 1955 trust instrument, the tri-museum gift agreement, the correct 990.
10. **Access opportunity (inferred):** No living founder. **A CRM prospect file exists** (`crm/prospects/Henry & Rose Pearlman Foundation.md`), implying Nariway tracks it — a possible route (inference; confirm in that file). Curators at the three museums and Princeton are named institutionally.
11. **Visual potential (inferred, strong):** Cézanne, Van Gogh, Modigliani — canonical, heavily reproduced works (inference).
12. **Research readiness:** Medium. The novel mechanism is the story and is already articulated; needs the gift agreement and the foundation's own records (EIN first).

### 20. Storm King — three generations, and the painting museum that became a sculpture landscape
1. **Collection / person / institution:** Ralph E. Ogden, H. Peter Stern, and John P. Stern; Storm King Art Center (New Windsor, NY).

9. **Evidence strength:** Strong. Spot-verified with primary 990s (FY2024 net assets $83,968,728; IRS ruling 1961). Uncertain: authoritative accession count (only "~100+"), attendance, the David Smith negotiation detail. Primary sources: founding deeds, the Ogden Foundation transfer terms, Storm King's own John Stern oral history.
10. **Access opportunity (inferred, strong):** **John P. Stern** (grandson, ran it into the 2010s) is named; the institution maintains an oral history — plausible interview and archival access (inference).
11. **Visual potential (inferred, strong):** ~500 acres of monumental sculpture in the Hudson Valley — one of the most photogenic sites in the sample (inference).
12. **Research readiness:** Strong. Governance/financials well documented; the founding pivot and family saga are the reporting, and an oral history already exists to mine.

## "Strategically important but probably dull"

These matter to Nariway's thesis — several are load-bearing for the corporate sub-typology or the data-availability finding — but would likely make weak *articles*, because they lack a human center, a real turn, or a scene. Named explicitly so the editorial and strategic maps stay separate.

- **JPMorgan Chase Art Collection** (`cases/jpmorgan-chase-collection.md`). Strategically vital: the proof that surviving M&A ≠ surviving bankruptcy (65 years, four mergers, no disruption). Editorially inert: "nothing happened, continuously." No decision-owner, no crisis, no scene — and its one dramatic claim ("began selling parts in the 2020s") *could not be corroborated* and must not be asserted.
- **Deutsche Bank / PalaisPopulaire** (`cases/deutsche-bank-collection.md`). Strategically the clean "corporate voluntary retreat" cell (closing a Berlin exhibition house, keeping ~55,000 works). But it's a corporate strategy memo, not a story — no protagonist, the collection isn't even dispersed, and the "building stopped paying for itself" beat is already told more vividly by CAM Raleigh and Rubin.
- **The Broad "lending library"** (`cases/broad.md`). Strategically the anti-warehouse benchmark (9,000+ loans since 1984). But it's a *system* working smoothly — thriving, well-endowed, uncontroversial. The interesting tension (private-museum tax scrutiny) is a policy sidebar, not a narrative.
- **Thoma Art Foundation** (`cases/thoma.md`). Strategically a sharp methodology lesson (the net-assets ratio can measure the wrong same-family entity; a $1.2B figure that can't safely be attributed). But "which of two foundations holds the collection?" is an accounting puzzle, not a human story — a great footnote, a poor feature.
- **Michener Art Museum** (`cases/michener-art-museum.md`). Strategically useful as the schema-breaking civic-founding case (Bucks County government + an arts nonprofit founded it; Michener was donor/namesake, not founding collector — "no identifiable person" decides). That very quality — no collector-protagonist — is exactly what makes it a weak article.

*(Honorable mention: **Lehman** and **Enron** corporate-bankruptcy dispersals have more inherent drama than the four above and could support a piece *together* as a "corporate forced-sale" pairing — but individually each is a thin, decision-owner-less liquidation with the human drama located in the *bankruptcy*, not the *art*.)*
