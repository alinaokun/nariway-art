# Artobiography — editorial direction (relaunch, 2026-08)

*Set by Alina 2026-08-16 after reading the [[editorial-candidate-packet]]. The packet revealed that the research is not just a taxonomy of collection models, it holds real stories the moment the people are centered. This doc is the editorial identity for the relaunch. Distinct from [[format-v0.1]] (the old single-feature build, now superseded for year one by the conversation format below). Not for the website yet.*

> **Format pivot (2026-08-17, Alina's decision). Artobiography is now conversation-first for year one.** The division of labor is settled: **Nariway researches collections → the flagship report publishes what we learn from them (the patterns) → Artobiography talks to people.** Desk research tells us *what happened*; conversations tell us *why*. So the three openers below are no longer standalone written features, they are **conversations** (the deep research already done becomes the briefing that makes each one sharp, plus report material). This also protects the proprietary asset: we stop giving the research away collection-by-collection. Interviews are the format *now*, not a permanent rule, a rare standout feature stays allowed. Tagline: ***Artobiography, conversations about the lives of art collections.*** The format's intellectual structure is "The Artobiography conversation format" below.

## The editorial promise (internal)
> **Artobiography tells the stories of people who built remarkable collections and the choices that determined what happened to them.**

Notice what is absent: no "legacy," "stewardship," "public access," "collection strategy," "research," no Nariway terminology. Just **people, collections, choices, and consequences.** Broad enough to hold a collection that becomes a museum, gets auctioned, stays in the family, disappears, is donated, travels, is fought over, or is divided among fifty states.

## The story engine
> Someone built something → eventually something had to happen to it → somebody made a choice → there were consequences.

## The core principle
**The people make us care first. Then the larger ideas emerge.** No reader should have to be taught about 501(c)(3)s, endowment ratios, or governance before they care. Judge a piece **editorially** (is it a great story?), kept separate from its **Nariway learning value** (does it teach the thesis?). The best article and the most useful case are not always the same collection. Keep those two judgments apart, always.

**Not morality tales. Understand before judging (the first-year rule).** We do not write about the "right" way to preserve a collection, and Artobiography is not in the business of grading collectors' legacy decisions. Auction can be a legitimate answer; dispersal can be; keeping it in the family can be; giving it away can be; building a museum can be a *terrible* answer. The interesting question is only ever **why someone chose what they chose, and what happened next.** We ask: what did these people build? What did they want? What choices did they make? What happened because of those choices? A collector's decision to sell, or to let a museum end, is a decision **to understand, not a failure to explain.** (Same guardrail as [[field-definition]]: Nariway must be able to conclude "sell was right" or "keep it private.") This neutrality also makes Nariway more credible, not less.

**Joy and wonder count too.** If every issue is lawsuits, death, tax, and institutional collapse, the publication turns grim fast. The slate needs light (the Vogels) as well as tension (Terra, Fisher Landau).

**The Artobiography contribution (a selection test).** Before choosing a story, ask: *what can Artobiography add that isn't already in the standard telling?* Not every piece must be investigative. The contribution can be a fresh interview, uncovered archival material, a financial analysis, a connection drawn across several cases, or simply a question earlier writers never asked. But a piece should earn its place by adding a layer, not by retelling a told story. Vogel is the template, the human story and the extraordinary decision are known; the contribution is the third layer, *what happened next?* (tested, not assumed, in [[vogel-fifty-states-afterlife]]). This is what keeps the publication from being a well-written summary of what is already on Wikipedia.

## The Artobiography conversation format (the distinctive spine)
Hundreds of art-world interviews already exist, and they mostly ask about taste, the market, favorite works, or the personality. An Artobiography conversation is recognizably different because it only ever interrogates **the life of a collection**, how it is built, cared for, shared, transferred, changed, or given a larger life, whoever the subject is. The subject is never the person for their own sake; it is what they can teach us about collections as things with lives and futures.

**The recurring five movements (a structure, not a script, adapt per subject):**
1. **From objects to a collection.** When did this stop being individual purchases (or, for a professional, an isolated case) and become a *collection* with its own identity? When did buying become *building*? *(The underexplored question, what happens before a collection becomes "important." Phillip Collins-shaped territory.)*
2. **The first thought of a future.** When did "what happens to it" first arise, and who raised it, the collector, a spouse, an heir, an advisor? What did they want it to become? *(The Nariway question, asked personally.)*
3. **The decision and its roads not taken.** What was considered and rejected, and why this model over another? What turned out harder than expected? *(Decisions and alternatives, not just outcomes, the "why" no database holds.)*
4. **What a collection owes.** The values question. What does the person believe a collection owes, to the artists, the public, the family, the culture it came from? What does "stewardship" actually mean to them? *(Where Phillip Collins' "what a collection owes its culture" and a ninth-generation steward's own definition both live.)*
5. **What the record can't tell us.** The one thing only this person knows, the surprise, what they'd tell their younger self, what the public got wrong. *(Layer 2 made human.)*

**The closing branch (every time):** "Who sees a completely different part of this that I should understand, and would you introduce me?" This is what compounds the network ([[problem-discovery]]).

**The disciplines that ride on top** (unchanged, and they are what protect it): **understand before judging** (never grade the decision, above); the **credibility test** (only publish a conversation you'd run if Nariway had no advisory business, [[conversations-series]]); and **two separate judgments** (is it a great conversation, and does it teach the thesis, the best interview and the most useful case are not always the same person).

**Who belongs** is wide by design, collectors, heirs, museum directors, registrars, curators, foundation founders, attorneys, tax and finance people, conservators, collection managers, architects, lenders, insurers, advisors, and people inventing new models, but every subject must illuminate how collections are built, cared for, shared, transferred, changed, or given a larger life. That test, not fame, decides who runs. The operating research spine derived from these movements is the **five lenses** (Trigger → Choice → Friction → Structure → Learning) plus custom questions per person, with the evidence discipline and the First-10 plan, all in [[conversations-series]] ("The research-integrated conversation program"). That superseded the earlier three-fixed-plus-two set for outreach.

1. **The Vogel Collection** — *give it away* · a **human story.** A postal clerk and a librarian built a museum-scale collection on one salary in a New York apartment and put it into museums across America (National Gallery + *Fifty Works for Fifty States*). Warm, surprising, about collectors not an institution, near-zero reputational danger, and it demolishes "collecting is for the rich" in the opening issue. It quietly carries the deepest Nariway idea, **a collection can have a larger life without becoming a museum.** **Confirmed opener.** Working titles: *The Collection That Went Everywhere* / *How Two New Yorkers Gave Their Collection to America.*
   - *Reporting refinement (2026-08-16), do not soften this back.* The afterlife investigation ([[vogel-fifty-states-afterlife]]) tested the access claim and found distribution created durable public **custody** in 50 states but **not** demonstrable sustained public **view** ("widely distributed, unevenly kept, largely unaudited"). One recipient (SIU Carbondale) actually lost the capacity to steward its gift. So the honest, and *stronger*, thesis is not the fairy tale. It is that **no comprehensive 15-year follow-up audit of the recipients has been identified in our research to date**, which makes "did the most famous democratizing gift in American art stay visible?" an open, reportable question. Carry the "larger life" line with that tension, not as settled triumph. (Language discipline, per Alina, hold the claim to *"no audit identified in our research,"* never *"nobody has ever checked"* or *"scoop."*)
2. **Fisher / SFMOMA** — *lend it for a century* · a **decision story.** The Fishers wanted their own Presidio museum; community opposition killed the plan; instead they placed 720+ works on a 100-year loan to SFMOMA while retaining ownership. The failed plan produced the interesting outcome. Working title: *The Museum They Didn't Build.* With Vogel, the reader starts to see the pattern without being taught it, distribute / lend, **there is no one answer.**
3. **Storm King** — *build it in the landscape* · a **reported place + family story**, and the one Alina **can physically report.** Ralph Ogden and Peter Stern founded it in 1960 intending a Hudson River School *painting* museum, then realized monumental sculpture in the landscape was the more distinctive idea; three generations, 66 years, John Stern reachable, archives on site, and it is a **local drive** from Montvale (already a coded case, [[storm-king]]/[[ogden]], and on [[regional-visits]]). This is where the Layer-2 "who can tell us this story?" muscle gets exercised for real, going, photographing, interviewing, rather than reconstructing from a screen.

## Two research layers now required for every serious candidate
The packet exposed a real bias: the research is excellent on **institutional outcomes** and thin on **people**. Fine for Nariway; not fine for Artobiography. So every serious editorial candidate now gets a **second research layer**:
- Layer 1 (existing): *what sources exist?*
- Layer 2 (new): **who can tell us this story?** Who was in the room? Who knew the collector? Who advised the family, negotiated the gift, fought the board, catalogued the collection, packed the paintings, inherited it, tried to save the museum, decided to close it? Access and people become first-class research, not an afterthought. This is where Qwoted fits (below).

## The division of labor (settled 2026-08-17)
- **Nariway researches collections** — the coded case dataset, the dossiers, investigations like [[vogel-fifty-states-afterlife]]. Desk research: *what happened.*
- **The flagship report publishes what we learn** — the patterns across 50, then 75, then 100 cases (what happens to private collections, which models endure, what makes them fail). Where the proprietary research becomes authority, not given away collection-by-collection.
- **The conversations get at *why*** — the collector's intent, the family's disagreement, the lawyer's regret, what stewardship means to a ninth-generation heir. What the database cannot tell us. *Refined 2026-08-17 (see "The publishing architecture" below): the conversations live on **Nariway** as a compounding owned asset, and **Artobiography becomes the layer where Alina makes sense of them** for readers, not the archive itself.*

The two methods compound: desk research says *what*, interviews say *why*, and Nariway gets smarter from both.

## The publishing architecture (refined 2026-08-17, a working hypothesis to test, not a redesign)
Alina's sharper split. Adopted as a **working hypothesis to validate with the first 3-5 conversations**, do not rebuild anything around it yet.
- **Nariway.com is the permanent home of the conversations.** Each is published there **as soon as it is ready**, at whatever cadence the research produces (one on Tuesday, two on Thursday, none the next week). The cadence follows the work, not a calendar. Call the property **Conversations**, not "interviews" (which sounds transactional in this high-trust world). The strategic core: this makes the growing body of first-person knowledge a **Nariway-owned asset that compounds**, rather than material given away on Substack, the same logic that moved the deep research into the report.
- **Artobiography (Substack) becomes the synthesis layer, not the archive.** Every week or two it does the one thing a link roundup cannot: **Alina makes sense of what she is finding.** An edition takes an editorial *question* she is noticing ("why collectors wait too long to decide," "the museum isn't always the answer," "what families misunderstand about giving art away") and draws on two or three conversations that illuminate it from different angles, linking to the full pieces on Nariway. The same conversations get **repurposed under different questions** over time. This returns Artobiography to what made the original work, **Alina's visible curiosity**, not articles manufactured to fill a calendar.
- **The high bar (this protects the property).** Publishing every Qwoted respondent would make Nariway look like a contributor mill, the opposite of the reputation we want. So **a research conversation is not automatically a published Conversation.** Every published one must teach something about how collections are built, governed, transferred, shared, preserved, divided, or given a larger life that desk research could not have taught nearly as well. Talk to many; publish only the ones that clear the bar.
- **Metadata from the first conversation** (cheap now, painful to retrofit). Tag each so the archive can later be browsed without rework: **perspective/track** (Builder / Inheritor / Steward / Advisor / Institution / Inventor), **questions touched** (succession, donation, governance, tax, public access, family, lending, foundations, museums, universities), **outcome discussed** (remained private / foundation / museum / university / long-term loan / gift / sale / dispersal / traveling), and **stage** (building → planning → deciding → transitioning → stewarding).
- **The report synthesizes both assets.** Case research is *what happened* to 50+ collections; conversations are *why*, from the people who decide. The interviews **generate evidence for the report**, they do not give it away.
- **Site work is a separate project.** Adding a Conversations section and a `Research | Conversations | Artobiography | About` nav happens in the **nariway-rebuild** Claude Code project (the two vaults don't share memory), and only *after* the first conversations prove the format. Not now.
- **Sequencing (Alina, 2026-08-17):** the first Substack Note, and Artobiography's posting cadence generally, is deliberately **deferred** until the conversation/interview model is settled and it is clear how conversations translate into Substack posts. Do not treat "post the first Note" as an outstanding or highest-leverage move before then. Conversations first, then their editorial translation.
- **What this does NOT change:** the conversation *format* still governs each conversation (the five movements, the 3+2, the signature question). And keep the **3 recurring questions from conversation #1**, not purely-custom-then-template, or the first five (exactly the ones you most want to compare) won't be comparable. The full operational template (invitation, layout, sharing assets) waits until 3-5 conversations prove the spine.
