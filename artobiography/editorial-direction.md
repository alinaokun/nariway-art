# Artobiography — editorial direction (relaunch, 2026-08)

*Set by Alina 2026-08-16 after reading the [[editorial-candidate-packet]]. The packet revealed that the research is not just a taxonomy of collection models, it holds real stories the moment the people are centered. This doc is the editorial identity for the relaunch. Distinct from [[format-v0.1]] (how a single piece is built); this is what we publish and why. Not for the website yet.*

## The editorial promise (internal)
> **Artobiography tells the stories of people who built remarkable collections and the choices that determined what happened to them.**

Notice what is absent: no "legacy," "stewardship," "public access," "collection strategy," "research," no Nariway terminology. Just **people, collections, choices, and consequences.** Broad enough to hold a collection that becomes a museum, gets auctioned, stays in the family, disappears, is donated, travels, is fought over, or is divided among fifty states.

## The story engine
> Someone built something → eventually something had to happen to it → somebody made a choice → there were consequences.

## The core principle
**The people make us care first. Then the larger ideas emerge.** No reader should have to be taught about 501(c)(3)s, endowment ratios, or governance before they care. Judge a piece **editorially** (is it a great story?), kept separate from its **Nariway learning value** (does it teach the thesis?). The best article and the most useful case are not always the same collection. Keep those two judgments apart, always.

**Not morality tales. Understand before judging (the first-year rule).** We do not write about the "right" way to preserve a collection, and Artobiography is not in the business of grading collectors' legacy decisions. Auction can be a legitimate answer; dispersal can be; keeping it in the family can be; giving it away can be; building a museum can be a *terrible* answer. The interesting question is only ever **why someone chose what they chose, and what happened next.** We ask: what did these people build? What did they want? What choices did they make? What happened because of those choices? A collector's decision to sell, or to let a museum end, is a decision **to understand, not a failure to explain.** (Same guardrail as [[field-definition]]: Nariway must be able to conclude "sell was right" or "keep it private.") This neutrality also makes Nariway more credible, not less.

**Joy and wonder count too.** If every issue is lawsuits, death, tax, and institutional collapse, the publication turns grim fast. The slate needs light (the Vogels) as well as tension (Terra, Fisher Landau).

## The relaunch slate — the first three (revised 2026-08-16)
**The first three establish what Artobiography *is*, they are not the three highest-scoring stories.** The opener's job is to make collectors, museum directors, attorneys, advisors, and family-office people think *"I'd be comfortable talking to her,"* never *"if I talk to her, could my family's decisions become her next investigation?"* So the first year leads with the **constructive register** (people who found a larger life for a collection), not the cautionary one. The three also deliberately test three **different kinds of storytelling**, which teaches Alina what she most enjoys writing:

1. **The Vogel Collection** — *give it away* · a **human story.** A postal clerk and a librarian built a museum-scale collection on one salary in a New York apartment and put it into museums across America (National Gallery + *Fifty Works for Fifty States*). Warm, surprising, about collectors not an institution, near-zero reputational danger, and it demolishes "collecting is for the rich" in the opening issue. It quietly carries the deepest Nariway idea, **a collection can have a larger life without becoming a museum.** **Confirmed opener.** Working titles: *The Collection That Went Everywhere* / *How Two New Yorkers Gave Their Collection to America.*
2. **Fisher / SFMOMA** — *lend it for a century* · a **decision story.** The Fishers wanted their own Presidio museum; community opposition killed the plan; instead they placed 720+ works on a 100-year loan to SFMOMA while retaining ownership. The failed plan produced the interesting outcome. Working title: *The Museum They Didn't Build.* With Vogel, the reader starts to see the pattern without being taught it, distribute / lend, **there is no one answer.**
3. **Storm King** — *build it in the landscape* · a **reported place + family story**, and the one Alina **can physically report.** Ralph Ogden and Peter Stern founded it in 1960 intending a Hudson River School *painting* museum, then realized monumental sculpture in the landscape was the more distinctive idea; three generations, 66 years, John Stern reachable, archives on site, and it is a **local drive** from Montvale (already a coded case, [[storm-king]]/[[ogden]], and on [[regional-visits]]). This is where the Layer-2 "who can tell us this story?" muscle gets exercised for real, going, photographing, interviewing, rather than reconstructing from a screen.

## Saved for later (strong, but not to lead with)
- **de la Cruz** — *moved off the opener (Alina's call, 2026-08-16).* The dossier ([[dossier-de-la-cruz]]) confirmed the hazard: Carlos is a **living, recently bereaved** central character whose contested decision is already publicly second-guessed, and the most provocative version depends on things we do not know (was there a plan, an instrument, a conversation). Excellent *investigative* territory **someday**; wrong way to introduce a publication to the exact community it needs to trust it. The reporting already done stands and waits.
- **Hillwood** — *what happens when the Smithsonian doesn't want your museum?* Post willed it a $10M endowment; the economics didn't work; the Smithsonian returned it; her own foundation became the durable answer. Carries the crucial lesson **giving away a great collection isn't enough, someone has to be able to afford the gift** — the piece where finance quietly matters. Strong **fourth**.
- **Budi Tek / Yuz Museum** — *try to make it endure.* Succession planned but never closed (LACMA joint foundation stalled on US-China relations and the pandemic); a story about **time** and the limit of control. Research thin (2/5); a later reported piece.
- **Barnes Foundation** — a founder's iron-clad deed judicially rewritten so the institution could survive. Among the strongest eventual pieces; too famous and institution-heavy to *open* with.
- **Rubin** — challenges the assumption that public access needs a permanent building.
- **Fisher Landau** — not an article yet, a **reporting project**: ~700-1,000 works of a 1,200-1,500-work collection unaccounted for in the record.

## Two research layers now required for every serious candidate
The packet exposed a real bias: the research is excellent on **institutional outcomes** and thin on **people**. Fine for Nariway; not fine for Artobiography. So every serious editorial candidate now gets a **second research layer**:
- Layer 1 (existing): *what sources exist?*
- Layer 2 (new): **who can tell us this story?** Who was in the room? Who knew the collector? Who advised the family, negotiated the gift, fought the board, catalogued the collection, packed the paintings, inherited it, tried to save the museum, decided to close it? Access and people become first-class research, not an afterthought. This is where Qwoted fits (below).

## The division of labor (getting clearer)
- **Artobiography = the storyteller.** What did they build, want, choose; what happened.
- **Nariway = the thinker, and eventual advisor.** What do all these stories, taken together, teach about giving privately owned art a larger life. The harder synthesis happens behind the scenes, not in the essays.
