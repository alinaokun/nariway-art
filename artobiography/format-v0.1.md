*Locked after the three-way stress test (McNay / Souls Grown Deep / Onera). Built from: Structure B's **hidden analytical question**, Structure A's **chronological warmth**, and Artobiography's **existing voice**. No further newsletter teardown; no more opening exercises. Next work is building the research base, not iterating this. Supersedes §6 of [editorial-structure-study](editorial-structure-study.md).*

## The editorial promise
> **The stories behind remarkable collections — and the people who bring them into public view.**
>
> *(plainer alternative: "…and how they enter public life.")*

The warmer version is primary on purpose: "the people who bring them into public view" **encodes the human-center rule in the tagline**, and it covers the cases where the subject is an *institution creating access to a collection it doesn't own* (Onera), a *dispersal* (Souls Grown Deep), a loan, a foundation, a failed museum — even the writer herself. It is human-first, not research-territory.

## The hidden engine
Every piece is driven by an analytical question **the writer knows and the reader meets as tension** — never printed as a hypothesis. *(McNay: control-vs-adaptation. Souls Grown Deep: does a collection have to stay together to matter? Onera: is access more radical than ownership?)* The reader just feels a problem that wants resolving.

## The human-center rule (locked)
**Every issue has a human center — but it need not be the collector.** It can be the collector, a founder, an heir, a curator, a museum director, an artist, or **you**, entering through your own discovery of the place. An institution-first subject still needs a person on the page — and sometimes that person is the writer. *(Onera is strongest entered through your own experience of seeing* America Abroad *— what it felt like to encounter something you normally couldn't access.)*

## The architecture — two layers

**Layer 1: the invisible skeleton** (writer's scaffolding; never printed as-is; beats collapse and merge freely):

`Human entry → The Collection → Why it matters → The Tension → The Choice / the model → What happened / what exists now → (larger meaning, woven) → Concrete close`

- The **larger meaning** ("what it tells us") is a **writing function, not a visible or required section.** Weave it through the last one or two sections so the insight *emerges*; never a designated "analysis" block.
- The skeleton is **collapsible**: a short Onera piece may use 3–4 beats; a rich Barnes piece 7. A photo-dispatch uses none.

**Layer 2: the visible subheads** — Artobiography's **evocative present-tense titles** ("The House She Would Never See Finished"), *never* the functional labels above. The skeleton is felt, never seen.

## Rules (locked)
- **Interleave analysis; never label it.** No "Lesson Learned / What This Means" blocks. State a fact, interpret it in a sentence right there, keep moving. Intelligence is discovered through the narrative. This is cultural writing.
- **Prose may not outrun the research.** The writing must not assert more than the case packet has *verified*; contested or unverified claims are hedged or cut. *(Caught in testing: McNay's will restrictions were unverified — don't write "the terms by which her taste would outlive her" until the will is obtained. FAPE is an independent nonprofit, not government-owned — don't write "your government's art.")*
- **No mandatory numerical clinch.** End on **one concrete thing — a fact, image, quote, outcome, or number — that makes the significance tangible.** Sometimes that's $100M in net assets; sometimes a single clause in a will, one artwork, an abandoned building, or a collection divided among fifty institutions.
- **Length 1,200–1,800 words, story-driven** — not padded to a floor, not cut to a template.
- **Voice = Artobiography's warm enthusiast-guide** — first-person entry through your own experience, emotional/thematic reading over formal criticism, the subject's own words woven in.

## The two-format system
- **Artobiography (tentpole)** — a deeply researched collection story, the architecture above.
- **From the Museum** (recurring, short) — a visual dispatch from a place visited.
- **A Collection Worth Knowing** (recurring, 300–500 words) — a discovery that doesn't warrant a full case.
