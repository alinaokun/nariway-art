# Problem Discovery — the evidence phase (playbook + log)

The central question of the next phase: **is there an important, recurring, inadequately-owned decision *before* a collector chooses what happens to a significant collection?** External evidence is now a **first-class standing input**, alongside case research. This is a habit (~2 substantive conversations a week for ~2 months), not a market-research project. Deliberately not elaborate. Belief shifts route to [[what-we-now-believe]].

## The real goal (not "15 interviews")
Reach the point where Alina can draw the **current decision process from memory**: trigger → first call → initial advisors → decision → specialists → implementation, and under each stage, **who owns it, who pays, where it breaks, what happens too early or too late, what is missing.** Then compare that to the [[decision-map]]. **The gap between the two may be Nariway.** Completing conversations is not the goal; being able to draw that map confidently is.

## The four questions, in order (do not skip to price)
1. **Does the situation occur?** Does a collector actually reach the moment of "what eventually happens to all of this," beyond estate planning? How often, and what triggers it (age, a spouse's death, uninterested children, storage, tax, a move, a museum inquiry, legacy)?
2. **What happens next today?** Who gets the first call, an estate attorney, wealth advisor, art advisor, family office, museum, appraiser? Then what? Who coordinates? **Is someone already performing the role we think is missing?**
3. **Where does the current process fail?** Look for friction, especially friction **nobody clearly owns.** ("The lawyer can implement whatever they decide; getting the family to decide is the hard part" is gold. "Usually their art advisor handles this" challenges us. "The family office quarterbacks it" may be our incumbent.)
4. **Does the unowned friction get important enough that someone pays to remove it?** Only push H7B after 1–3. Not "would you pay $30k" (weak). The evidence to want: "actually, I have a client dealing with exactly this."

## How to run a conversation
- **Opening (researcher, not seller):** "I'm researching what happens when serious collectors start thinking about the long-term future of their collections, before they decide to donate, sell, create a foundation, build a museum, or something else. Have you ever been involved in a situation like that?" Then let them tell the story.
- **The best move, "tell me about the last one":** not "how do collectors usually handle succession?" but "think of the last collector you worked with facing this, what happened?" Then follow the chronology (trigger, first call, what they thought they wanted, who got involved, who coordinated, where the family disagreed, what was harder than expected, whether the solution differed from what they first imagined, whether anything important had no owner, when another person at the table would have helped).
- **Hunt for disconfirmation.** Try to *destroy* "nobody owns this." Ask art advisors "when clients weigh sale vs donation vs family transfer vs foundation vs loan vs an institution, who helps them think it through?" Ask family offices "would you bring in an independent person, or is that redundant?" Ask attorneys "is there a missing role, or is this handled fine?" Want uncomfortable answers.

## Two techniques that compound
- **"Who else should I talk to?" — relentlessly, and branch across professions.** Not "another estate attorney," but "you've seen this from the legal side; who sees a completely different part of it I should understand?" Attorney → collection manager → art advisor → family-office CEO → museum director → foundation → insurer. After ~20 conversations the **same names start recurring**, and that convergence identifies who actually sits at the center of the ecosystem.
- **Artobiography as the relationship engine.** Interviewing someone who lived a transition you are writing about is warmer and more natural than "customer discovery," and it is exactly what a serious writer does. "I'm researching a piece on how the Fisher Collection reached SFMOMA, I'd love to understand what happened between the Presidio idea and the 100-year arrangement." People enjoy talking about work they are proud of. Mid-conversation: "do situations like this come up often?" Now it is Nariway market research without pretending to be. The editorial engine *is* the relationship engine. This technique now has a concrete form, the [[conversations-series]] (Artobiography's revived written-interview series), where practitioner interviews double as logged rows here. Credibility test to keep it honest: only publish an interview you would still run if Nariway had no advisory business.

## Field-test questions (double-duty, added 2026-08)
The upcoming conversations should test **both** the business (would anyone hire this) **and** the intellectual thesis (is there an undefined field, see [[field-definition]]). Weave these in, they are more valuable than "would you pay $30k":
- "When you think about a private collection over its *entire life*, is there anyone whose job is to think about the collection as a whole?"
- "Who helps a collector explore what the collection *could become*, before they've decided on a museum, foundation, donation, loan, or sale?"
- "Where does responsibility become fragmented? What decisions fall *between* professions?"
- "What possibilities do collectors commonly not know they have?"
- "At what point do collectors start thinking about sharing their collection?"
- "What would you call this problem?"
The answers reveal whether there is a business *and* whether there is a field. A recurring "no one does that" is the strongest signal for both.

## The Collection Transition Market Map (build the real TAM bottom-up, added 2026-08-16)
The market cannot be sized from art-market reports (see [[pitch-deck]] slide 3b). Build it from the interviews, by investigating **actual transition events**, not hypotheticals. For every relevant conversation, get the story, *"tell me about the last collector you worked with who had to decide what would happen to a collection,"* then fill five cells:
| Cell | What to learn |
|---|---|
| **Trigger** | What made them start thinking about the collection's future? (age, death, inheritance, business sale, move, divorce, kids involved, it outgrew the home, a museum inquiry) |
| **Buyer** | Who felt the problem strongly enough to act? (collector, spouse, heir, family-office exec, trustee, attorney) |
| **Current solution** | Who did they call first, and who ended up controlling the decision? |
| **Spend** | Where did money already flow? **The killer question: "who got paid?"** (reveals existing budgets, the real TAM) |
| **Gap** | What important part stayed unowned, no one coordinating *before* the specialists were chosen? |
Two sizing questions for every interview: *"At roughly what point does a private collection become complicated enough that the owner needs to think about its future as a collection, rather than about individual works?"* and *"how many collectors like that do you encounter?"*

**Target output (after ~30-50 interviews), stronger than any report:** *"Each year ~X families enter one of five predictable transition events; they already spend ~Y across Z fragmented services; no independent party owns the decision before those services are chosen. Nariway's wedge is that unowned decision."*

**The pull signal to watch (not vanity metrics):** not "interesting idea," but "I have a family you should talk to" → "I wish you'd been around three years ago" → "can I introduce you?" → **"can you help us with this?"** Unsolicited movement toward a real collection problem is the only signal that counts, not Qwoted counts, subscribers, or followers.

## The demand side — the collectors themselves (Person on the Street, added 2026-08-20)
Everything above is **supply-side** (practitioners who witnessed transitions). The gap: we know what professionals *think collectors need*, not what collectors *experience*. Qwoted's **"Person on the Street"** feature sources everyday people (not experts), and Qwoted's team will help build the query, so it can reach the **cardiologist, attorney, executive, or academic who happens to collect art** but whose profile would never surface in an "art succession" search. This is Nariway's first **demand-side** evidence, and it tests H7A/H7B and the "who is the customer" question from the side we have never heard. Run it in PARALLEL with the expert conversations, and ahead of another expert query. (Alina's instinct + external review.)

**Make it exploratory, NOT succession-first.** Do not impose the thesis ("find me collectors with a succession problem"). Succession is one moment in a longer relationship. Explore the whole arc: **identity** (when does buying art become "a collection"? do they even call themselves a collector?) → **understanding** (is what I built distinctive/coherent/interesting beyond its value? how would I know?) → **sharing** (I'd like people to see some of this; what are my options; whom do I even call?) → **stewardship** (what should I document/care for?) → **transition** (what happens when I'm gone or don't want the responsibility?) → **family** (does anyone else care as much as I do?).

**Keep the PUBLIC query dead simple** (a 12-question call loses everyone): *"Do you own art that matters to you? I'd like to hear how you think about your collection and its future."* Plus, explicitly: **"You do not need to consider yourself a serious art collector."** That last line is the point, the people we learn most from may not identify as collectors at all. Do NOT say "significant collection" or require a dollar value (we don't yet know what "significant" means, and value may be the wrong measure). The full exploratory brief goes to Qwoted's team; the public call stays simple.

**The golden question, once talking:** *"Is there anything about your collection you wish you had someone to help you figure out?"* Then STOP. Do not offer categories (valuation, succession, loans, museums, foundations, access). Let them name it. If several independently say "I don't know whether what I built matters," that's a finding. If they say **"nothing, my art advisor handles everything," that's a finding too**, it disconfirms H7A, which is exactly what we want.

**RESEARCH, not a Conversation invite, and not a sales pitch.** Separate **participation** from **publication**: it is research for Nariway, and no personally identifiable comment is published without permission (candor requires it; also the public-sources / no-private-family-circumstances guardrail in [[research-program]]). An extraordinary, public-comfortable story gets a SEPARATE [[conversations-series|Conversation]] invite. And do NOT pitch advisory when someone says "I don't know what to do", this round is to learn whether the problem is real and how it feels, not to close (premature selling contaminates the evidence). Log responses as demand-side rows (anonymized); route belief shifts to the [[what-we-now-believe|Evidence Base]] (H7A, H7B, the customer question).

**It may redefine the customer.** We have pictured "a serious collector, significant collection, major transition." This may surface another group entirely, the person with 75 coherent regional works who doesn't know it matters, or someone with 20 works who just wants a few exhibited, modest market value but high distinctiveness/public-potential (the H4/H5 and [[field-definition]] bet). If so, the opportunity shifts from "help a $20M collector choose foundation vs museum" toward "help people see the public potential of what they've already assembled", which reshapes the [[pitch-deck]] market sizing. Do not decide yet; this query is how we find out.

## The signal ladder (Toi and the CFO watch for the progression)
"Interesting idea" (weak) → "I've seen this problem" → "this happens all the time" → "here's where it goes wrong" → **"nobody really owns that"** → "I know someone dealing with this now" → "you should speak with them" → "what would you do for them?" → "what would that cost?" → **someone asks Nariway to help with a real situation** (the evidence we want). No feasibility study is pitched anywhere in that sequence; the market reveals what the first product should be.

## Lessons on running these (updated as we learn)
- **Lead with the one-line answer, then tell the story if invited.** The Dawn call (2026-08-13) mismatched because Alina, treating it as a warm catch-up, narrated the *journey* (how she got here), and Dawn heard a lack of clarity about what Nariway does and who it serves. The fix is not to become transactional; it is to **open with the crisp line already written in [[positioning]]** ("I focus on what happens to significant private art collections over time, and the different ways they move into public life. Founder of Nariway, publisher of Artobiography"), and *then* let the journey follow. Warm and concise are not in tension. You can be both.
- **Prefer subjects who lived a transition over subjects whose practice is business development.** People who founded a foundation or managed a collection are proud to walk you through what happened; someone whose whole business is a pipeline will, understandably, steer toward their own offering. No judgment of them, just a selection rule. Warmth and non-transaction are why the [[conversations-series|written interview]] frame works better than "customer discovery."
- **The forcing question is fair, the false binary is not.** "Are you doing this for fun or to make money?" is worth answering plainly (money, [[nariway-cfo|one million a year]]). But "thought leadership OR money" is a false choice, Nariway's model is money *through* credibility and relationships, with the editorial front door as the acquisition engine, not a hobby. Name that as a deliberate go-to-market, not indecision.
