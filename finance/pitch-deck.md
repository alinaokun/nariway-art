### 1. Nariway, in one line
Strategic advisory on **what becomes of a significant private art collection** — the decision every great collector eventually faces and almost none plan for. Art is the domain; the work is high-stakes strategic planning about legacy. Founder, Alina Okun. Editorial front door, [[format-v0.1|Artobiography]].

### 2. The problem
A collection is a biography until its owner dies. Then, almost overnight, it becomes an estate, and a lifetime of choices must be re-decided, fast, by people who did not make them. The collector assumes a grateful museum will take it all. **Increasingly, the museum says no** (no space, no budget, no fit; storage is already overcrowded). The alternatives, foundation, gift, loan, dispersal, sale, are a portfolio of decisions, and **no independent party is standing beside the collector to help.** The auction house is the only one with a standing offer, so "sell" wins by default, not design.

### 3. The opportunity (why the prize is large)
- **~$992B** of art and collectibles will change hands over the next decade, mostly by inheritance (C1). UHNW art holdings **$2.56T rising to $3.47T** by 2030 (C9).
- This is not liquidation, it is a vast **sorting and governance process** happening privately, years before any sale.
- **~$54T** of US wealth passes first to surviving spouses, **over 95% to women** (C8) — the first decision-maker the market overlooks.
- The 2026 auction rebound is being carried by **estates, not speculation** — the visible tail of the same wave ([[market-intelligence]] #2).

### 3b. The market, honestly sized (triangulated, NOT published)
No one publishes the number we want, how many collectors control collections substantial enough that continuity, family, access, governance, donation, institutionalization, or dispersal has become **material**. So we triangulate, and we are explicit that these are **estimates to investigate, not statistics for a deck**.
- **Three concentric circles.** *Institution-scale* ($25-100M+, may already have managers, foundations, museum plans), few, and NOT the core market. Do **not** use the 446 private-museum founders as the market, that is the extreme visible outcome, not the population with the problem. *Decision-scale* ($5-20M, sometimes less where cultural importance outruns value), the interesting middle: a 68-year-old couple, 120 works over 35 years, three heirs of differing interest, museum loans, and no plan. *Approaching-decision* (younger, still building), the future.
- **The qualifier is not collection value. It is complexity × transition × intention.** A $100M collection owned by a decided 45-year-old has no Nariway problem; a $7M collection built over 40 years by a 78-year-old who wants it to "mean something" has a large one. The trigger is the moment collecting stops being about acquiring objects and becomes about deciding what the collection should *become*.
- **Rough exploratory range:** ~20,000-75,000 collector households globally, ~5,000-20,000 US, past that point. Estimates, not facts.
- **A small market is plenty.** At a bounded **$25-75K** "Collection Futures Study" (name/price not locked), **20-40 engagements = $1M**. Nariway diagnoses, designs alternatives, and coordinates the specialists; it does not do all the downstream work. **So market size is not the binding risk. Willingness-to-pay (H7B) and solo capacity are.** The real TAM will be built bottom-up from the interviews (the Collection Transition Market Map in [[problem-discovery]]), not from any art-market report.

### 4. Why now
The Great Art Transfer is underway and accelerating. The decisions are being made now, unadvised or badly advised, and the demographic wave only grows. **80% of collectors intend to pass their collections to children or spouses** (Art Basel/UBS 2025), so the problem is not "which museum?" but "what happens when control of this thing I built for 30 years changes?" And the problem is becoming **economically visible**: in Feb 2026 **Bank of America launched an art-consulting service** for wealthy clients, explicitly tied to the coming generational transfer ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/finance/bofa-offers-art-consulting-wealthy-clients-collectors-increase-loans-demand-2026-02-18/)). Not bad news, a signal the wave is real, and a prompt to find the part banks and advisors structurally do not own.

### 5. What Nariway does
Independent strategic advice on the future of a collection, and **only that** — not buying, not selling. It frames and sequences the decision, coordinates the licensed specialists (estate, tax, appraisal, insurance, museum relations), and designs the right combination of pathways for a specific collection and family. Independence is the product: an adviser paid only on a sale has reached a conclusion before the analysis begins.

### 6. Products & services
- **Bespoke advisory** — the core engagement (per collector / family / estate).
- **The flagship report, "What Becomes of Great Art Collections"** — an authoritative annual data study that makes Nariway the source and drives inbound (the scalable leg). → [[flagship-report]]
- **Artobiography** — the editorial front door and audience engine (1,472 followers, growing with nothing published).
- **The knowledge base / case dataset** — proprietary IP that could productize into benchmarking.

### 7. The unique insight (the moat)
A proprietary, coded dataset of **how significant collections actually entered public life and how they fared** (targeting 50+ cases). It already yields findings no one else has, most notably: **governance design at founding predicts survival better than endowment size** — a collector with an independent board and a locked succession outlasts one with far more money but sole control. That is the kind of counterintuitive, defensible intelligence a serious report is read for, and advice a collector cannot get anywhere else.

### 8. The landscape (and the white space)
- **Art-market reports** (Deloitte, Art Basel/UBS, TEFAF) — cover sales and prices, a different lane.
- **Art advisors** (incl. [[comparables|Nina Surtees / smART]]) — acquisition, curation, lifestyle. Not disposition or legacy strategy.
- **Wealth advisors, family offices** (incl. [[Dawn Mari La Monica|Dawn / DML]]) — manage the money and the room; do not do art. Referral channel, not competitor.
- **Estate attorneys, appraisers** — licensed pieces of the puzzle Nariway coordinates, not the strategist.
- **The white space:** independent, art-fluent, finance-literate strategy on *what becomes of the collection*. No one owns it.
- **Why can't the attorney, art advisor, auction house, family office, or bank do this? Incentives and position, not competence.** The auction house benefits from a sale; the museum wants the gift; the art advisor works acquisition and disposition; the attorney solves legal structure; the accountant solves tax; the museum consultant enters once a museum is already contemplated; the bank's art service sits inside a lending relationship. No one's mandate is to **determine the best future for the collection *before* deciding which solution or specialist is needed.** That is the wedge: **independent decision architecture, before execution begins**, and independent of the answer (Nariway can conclude keep it private, don't build the museum, sell half and endow the rest). Far more defensible than "holistic advice."

### 9. Business model
Solo bespoke advisory realistically caps around **$300–700K/yr**, so the **$1M+ target needs a portfolio with a scalable leg** ([[business-model-ledger]]): advisory + the report/data product + selective media. The free authoritative report is the top-of-funnel engine (à la a research firm's flagship), converting attention into advisory and, eventually, a paid benchmarking product off the same dataset.

### 10. Where we are (honest traction)
- **Validated:** the market thesis and the positioning, corroborated independently by outside analysts ([[market-intelligence]]).
- **Built:** the case dataset (30 of 50 coded to report grade), the report underway, the voice and editorial engine, a warm audience.
- **Still untested (the one that matters):** **willingness to pay.** No paid engagement yet. The next milestone is real discovery conversations, not more building. The CFO flags this every day for a reason.

### 11. Why Alina
Ex-finance and strategy, plus a curatorial writer's eye. She took Sotheby's Institute's art-investment course and came away rejecting art-as-investment as too opaque, an *informed* conviction that art's meaning is legacy and stewardship, not a price. That is precisely the lens this work needs, and few have both halves.

### 12. The vision — two futures, one wedge
- **The boutique (achievable now).** The trusted independent advisor to collectors facing a transition. Small client base, high fees, excellent margins; ~20-40 engagements a year is the million. A terrific outcome on its own, and the stated goal.
- **The category (optional upside).** Owning the *transition and lifecycle of private collections*. Advisory is the wedge; the report is authority; **every engagement adds to a proprietary dataset of collection transitions that does not exist in one integrated place today**. The flywheel: research → advisory → data → better decisions → more clients → more data. Artobiography and Qwoted feed the top; client work eventually feeds the private, experiential layer. That is a company, not a consultancy.
- **The key move: both futures share the identical next 90 days** (the transition-decision wedge + the discovery interviews), so no choice is required now, and the **platform/infrastructure of the bigger version must NOT be built yet** (that is how you spend two years perfecting a solution to a problem collectors don't yet believe they have). Build the wedge; let the market's *pull* reveal which ceiling is real.
- Become **the** name a collector, family office, or estate attorney reaches for when the question is "what happens to it next," in a lane no one else is standing in.
