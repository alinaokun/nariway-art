# Learnings Log — one new thing a day

*A running stream of genuinely new things learned from Nariway's research, the POF-style "I had no idea that was a thing" nuggets Alina wants every single day. Maintained by the daily check-in: each morning it surfaces ONE new concept, finding, structure, or pattern from the research corpus (preferring the day's new material; on a quiet day, mining the deep backlog of coded cases and market intel Alina has not been shown yet) and appends it here, newest first, so nothing repeats. Featured in the check-in email under "Today I learned." Distinct from [[what-we-now-believe]] (how our MODEL of the business shifts); this is the steady drip of interesting facts and concepts. There is far more in the corpus than has been surfaced, so this never has to run dry.*

---

- **2026-08-17 — The report's central claim finally has a real number behind it: 13 of 13 non-founder-sole institutions survived their founder, versus 10 of 13 founder-sole ones (77%), out of 26 testable cases.** Every prior run had asserted "governance beats endowment" case-by-case, in prose, as new examples arrived; this run pulled the coded fields directly from all 50 files instead. The honest asterisk makes it more credible, not less: the 3 founder-sole "failures" aren't one clean pattern — two are death-triggered governance collapses (like Terra), the third is a structurally different living-founder closure (Fisher Landau) that isn't really the same failure mode at all. -> [[report-dataset]] Pattern 14
- **2026-08-16 — A collection's own tax filing can measure the wrong entity entirely.** The Kinsey Collection's public 990 belongs only to a small educational foundation ($649K net assets) while the actual collection is owned and toured by the family's separate for-profit-adjacent enterprise — so the "1.83x" ratio a naive read would pull describes almost nothing about the real operation's durability. The Thoma Foundation shows the same trap in reverse: a $1.2B net-assets figure sits on one entity, but until it's confirmed that's the collection-holding entity (not a same-family sister foundation), the report codes it `unknown` rather than risk attributing the wrong balance sheet. Matters because every well-capitalized family foundation in the dataset carries a milder version of this same risk. → [[report-dataset]] Pattern 10
- **2026-08-15 — A foundation's governance can be locked in over a decade before the museum even exists.** Ernst Beyeler chartered Fondation Beyeler in 1982 — 15 years before the museum opened and 28 years before his 2010 death — the earliest, most complete pre-death governance lock-in the case dataset has found. It handed to a fully independent board with no family option at all (he had no children), and 16 years on it is thriving and expanding. It's the sharpest evidence yet for the report's core claim that governance, not family or endowment size, is what determines whether a collection outlives its founder. → [[fondation-beyeler]]
- **2026-08-14 — Private Operating Foundations (POF).** A private foundation that *operates* a museum (rather than only grantmaking) can earn tax treatment closer to a public charity *and* let the founding family keep substantial control. The concrete answer to the collector's "keep control and still go public" tension. → [[Private Museums and POFs - Erskine (Forbes)]]
- **2026-08-14 — Corporate collections die by bankruptcy and M&A, not mortality.** Lehman Brothers' art collection was auctioned after the 2008 collapse, the canonical corporate dispersal, now a coded case. A whole class of collections (bank and company holdings) faces "what becomes of it" on a different trigger than an individual's death. → [[candidate-universe]]
- **2026-08-14 — For a corporate collection, the building can be the liability, not the collection.** Deutsche Bank is closing PalaisPopulaire (its Berlin exhibition house) and reverting its ~55,000-work collection to rotating loans, evidence the standalone venue, not the art, is what stopped paying for itself. The clean corporate echo of CAM Raleigh.
- **2026-08-13 — Governance beats endowment for survival (provisional).** Terra had roughly $450M behind it and still failed, because its only original directors were the founder and his son, so his death was a control vacuum. The survivors had independent boards and succession set before death. Money did not decide it.
- **2026-08-13 — Institutions fail in four distinct shapes, on different clocks.** Governance-fast (Terra, at the founder's death), funding-slow (Corcoran, over 126 years), founder-controlled closure while still alive (Fisher Landau, 2017), and labor/governance rupture with no money problem at all (Marciano, ~$50M in the bank, dark for four years).
- **2026-08-13 — Durability usually cannot be measured from outside.** The true endowment sits in IRS Schedule D (not pullable at scale) and headline net-asset ratios are inflated by buildings and land. How rarely a collection's durability is verifiable from public data is itself a finding.
- **2026-08-12 — The first person who decides is often a widow.** Roughly $54T of US wealth passes first between spouses, and over 95% of that goes to women (Cerulli). The market fixates on young heirs and overlooks the surviving spouse who actually holds the first decision. → [[claims-register]] C8
