# What We Now Believe — the intellectual history of Nariway

Not a research log. A short running record of **how our model of the business has actually changed**, and why. Its job is to fight confirmation bias and memory loss: after roughly every five cases or five external conversations, Toi asks not "what did we research?" but **"what does Alina believe today that she did not believe a month ago?"** and adds an entry. Keep it terse. A belief that never changes is not evidence of being right; it may be evidence of not looking. (Idea credit: external review, Aug 2026.)

Format per entry: **We used to believe X. [What changed.] We now believe Y.**

---

## August 2026

**On the core problem.** We used to believe *dispersal* was the primary succession problem, that collections mostly hit the market when their owner dies. The transfer data showed the opposite: **retention is the norm** (most inherited art is kept). We now believe the larger problem is **unmanaged, unadvised transition**, the decision made badly or by default, not the sale itself.

**On the opportunity.** We used to believe the opportunity was **helping collectors build new museums**. The case work showed standalone founder-museums are a **small, fragile tail**. We now believe Nariway may instead own the **earlier decision, what form public legacy should take**, before lawyers, architects, and tax structures lock the answer in.

**On what predicts survival.** We had no view. From ~34 coded cases we now believe (provisionally) that **governance design at founding predicts survival better than endowment size**, an independent board and a locked succession outlast far more money under sole control. To test at 50.

**On the market's motion.** We now believe **"sell" wins by default, not by design**: the 2026 auction rebound is carried by estates, and when no independent party coordinates the decision, the auction house is the only one with a standing offer. That sharpens *who owns the decision* (H7A) into a competitive question.

**On our own writing.** We used to believe we could write good Substack Notes by inventing topics and polishing them. They read stiff and manufactured every time. We now believe Notes must come from **Alina's real published essays**, in her real voice, following what proven practitioners actually recommend.

**On ourselves (the honest one).** We now believe the biggest risk is no longer too little structure but **too much**: it has become more satisfying to build Nariway than to operate it. The internal sophistication is far ahead of the external footprint, one live discovery call, no paid client, willingness-to-pay untested. The next phase is evidence, not architecture.

**On the family-office channel (first live evidence, Dawn call 2026-08-13).** We used to treat family offices as a promising **warm referral gateway** into collector legacy decisions. Our first live conversation, with a specialist who has worked them full-time for two years, called them a **"very tough crowd"** she finds genuinely hard. We now believe the family-office lane is a **hard, transactional arena, not a soft entry**, and that Nariway should **lead with the art-world relationships** (collectors, foundation founders, curators, museum leaders) where Alina has native credibility and [[conversations-series|Artobiography]] is a natural door, treating family offices as a **downstream** channel rather than the front line. One data point, but a credible one; test it in the next conversations.

**On method (the temperament question).** Dawn models a **hyper-transactional** style, every call strategic, a reconnect turned into a coaching pitch. We now believe that style would **backfire in the art world**, which runs on trust and discretion, and that a **relationship-and-credibility-led go-to-market is correct for Nariway**, not a failure of nerve. The caveat the CFO keeps: warmth is the *acquisition method*, not permission to avoid the hard question. It still has to reach "someone asks Nariway to help with a real situation."

## The Evidence Base — the living scoreboard (upgraded 2026-08-20)
The hypothesis-keyed record of what Nariway believes and how sure it is, and the thing that (compounded and verified over time) becomes proprietary intellectual capital. **Every case and every Conversation updates it.** The after-every-conversation capture ([[conversations-series]]) and the cloud research agent both feed it. Same verification bar as the [[what-becomes-of-great-art-collections|report]], evidence is sourced or marked unknown, numbers link to [[claims-register]]. **A belief with no disconfirmation target is a bias, not a finding**, so each entry names what would flip it and the next best test. Keep entries terse. Confidence: low / medium / high, all provisional.

**H1 · The transition is typically *unmanaged*, not that collections disperse.** Confidence: medium-high. *For:* retention is the norm (C3, ~72% keep; ~80% intend to pass to heirs, C12); the problem is the decision made by default. *Against:* the "great estate rush" shows real dispersal at death when no coordinator exists. *Cases:* corporate dispersals (Lehman, Enron) vs the family retentions. *Conversations:* Colleen, Erskine, Findley on what families actually do. *Unknowns:* how often "unmanaged" yields a bad outcome vs a fine one. *Would flip it:* families routinely managing the transition well unaided. *Next test:* the advisor/attorney conversations.

**H2 · Collectors overestimate museums' willingness to take collections intact; the receiving end is closing.** Confidence: medium. *For:* museums decline gifts on space/budget/fit/forever-cost (report §4.5). *Against:* donation *intentions* stay common (C2), so it is narrower than it looks. *Cases:* Hillwood (Smithsonian returned it). *Conversations:* **Leslie Anderson is the direct test** (why a museum declines). *Unknowns:* the actual decline rate and drivers. *Would flip it:* museums readily accepting intact collections. *Next test:* Leslie.

**H3 · Standalone founder-museums suit only a minority (a small, fragile tail).** Confidence: medium-high. *For:* founder-museums are a small tail; lighter models (loan, foundation, dispersal) are rising. *Against:* none strong yet. *Cases:* Barnes (constraint-locked), MoMA (absorbed), the failures Terra/Corcoran, vs Fisher→SFMOMA (loan), Vogel (dispersal), Anderson→Stanford (university). *Conversations:* Gretchen (durability), Cortney (nomadic, no building at all). *Unknowns:* the true pathway rate across the full universe. *Would flip it:* standalone museums surviving at the same rate as lighter models. *Next test:* the disconfirmation research pass; Gretchen.

**H6 / H8 · Governance design at founding predicts survival better than endowment size; permanence often requires surrendering control.** Confidence: medium (strengthening). *For:* Pattern 14 (first fully-counted governance table); Terra failed with ~$450M under sole control; survivors had independent boards and locked succession; Poldi Pezzoli's 145-year survival. *Against:* not yet tested on the full set; small-n. *Cases:* Barnes (control judicially rewritten to survive) vs MoMA (independent board from founding). *Conversations:* Gretchen, Erskine (foundation governance). *Unknowns:* whether it holds at 75-100 cases; true endowment still unpulled (WebFetch blocked). *Would flip it:* well-governed institutions failing at the same rate, or richly-endowed sole-control ones surviving. *Next test:* the depth/verification pass; more cases.

**H7A · No single professional owns the pre-decision (the unowned decision).** Confidence: low (THE core untested thesis). *For:* the incentives argument (auction house benefits from sale, museum wants the gift, attorney does structure, none owns "what should this become before we pick a specialist"). *Against:* none yet from the market; possible incumbents are family offices / art advisors. *Cases:* McNay = the prepared exception (strong founder preplanning); most cases lack it. *Conversations:* **Colleen Boyle is the direct test** ("who coordinates the whole decision, where are the gaps in ownership?"). *Unknowns:* whether an incumbent already coordinates end-to-end. *Would flip it:* a discovered incumbent doing this satisfactorily. *Next test:* Colleen, then every advisor/attorney. *Actively try to disprove this in every conversation.*

**H7B · Someone will pay an independent party to own that decision (willingness to pay).** Confidence: low (the commercial crux, only market contact proves it). *For:* the surrounding industry is monetizing adjacent services (BofA art consulting 2026; 51%→ wealth managers offer art services, C10). *Against:* the pre-decision may have no natural fee event (unlike a transaction); $0 earned, no paid engagement yet. *Cases:* n/a (market question). *Conversations:* watch for the pull signal ("I have a family you should talk to" → "can you help us?"). *Unknowns:* who pays (collector vs family office vs attorney) and on what fee event. *Would flip it (confirm):* someone asking Nariway to help with a real situation. *Next test:* the discovery conversations; a first paid engagement.

**Sell wins by default, not by design.** Confidence: medium. *For:* the 2026 auction rebound is carried by estates; when no independent party coordinates, the auction house is the only one with a standing offer. *Against:* some estates plan deliberately. *Cases:* de la Cruz (fast sale after a death), the estate-rush coverage. *Conversations:* Colleen, Erskine. *Would flip it:* estates routinely reaching planned, non-sale outcomes. *Next test:* the advisor conversations.

**Public ownership is not public access; "public life" is multidimensional.** Confidence: medium-high (a discovered distinction). *For:* Vogel gave 2,500 works into public custody across 50 states, yet sustained public *view* is unproven ([[vogel-fifty-states-afterlife]]); conversely a long-term loan (Fisher) can yield high public view without public ownership. *Cases:* Vogel, Fisher, the freeport paradox (art held in the dark, C15). *Conversations:* Alexis (visible-in-a-workplace vs accessible), Sage (available vs cared-about), Kate Bryan. *Unknowns:* which dimensions predict a "durable public life." *Would flip it:* ownership reliably delivering visibility. *Next test:* the availability-vs-access answers from the first wave; score cases on the [[field-definition]] dimensions.

**H4 / H5 · Distinctiveness and the gap a collection fills predict public potential better than market value.** Confidence: low (insufficient evidence, and it is what we *hope* to find, so treat with suspicion). *For:* four vivid examples, not a predictor. *Against:* untested at scale. *Would flip / confirm it:* a scored comparison across cases showing distinctiveness out-predicts value for durability/resonance. *Next test:* score the coded set on distinctiveness vs value.

**Channel · Who initiates and who pays.** Confidence: low. *For:* nothing direct. *Against/complication:* the family-office-as-easy-gateway assumption weakened by the Dawn call (hard even for a full-time specialist); the art-world relationships look like the warmer front door. *Would resolve it:* private-client attorneys or family offices proving to be the real initiator/referral gateway, or the collector being the direct buyer. *Next test:* Colleen, Erskine, Findley, and any family-office contact.

### The variables framework (emerging, discover-don't-invent)
The longer-term asset ChatGPT flagged: a rigorous way to examine which variables make a particular future appropriate for a particular collection, across **Collection** (coherence, significance, distinctiveness, scale, conservation burden, documentation), **Collector** (intent, time horizon, resources, desired involvement, willingness to relinquish control), **Family** (interest, capability, governance, generational alignment), **Institutional fit** (scholarly value, collection gaps, storage, curatorial capacity, geographic relevance), **Public potential** (who benefits, why accessible, what experience), and **Durability** (governance, operating economics, succession, leadership dependence, property burden). **Do NOT build this top-down.** Per [[field-definition]], the variables must be *discovered* from the cases and conversations, which is what the Evidence Base above accumulates. When enough evidence shows *which* variables actually predict durable outcomes, this crystallizes into Nariway's proprietary IP. Not yet; let it emerge.

## The open question we must become obsessive about
Does the market recognize this problem strongly enough to **hire someone specifically to own it** (H7A), and if so, **who initiates and who pays** (H7B, the collector, or the private-client attorney / family office as gateway)? Not yet mature. The next belief-shifts should come from the **first-wave and cold Conversations now in flight** ([[conversations-series]]), especially Colleen (ownership of the decision) and Leslie (the receiving side). **And a new DEMAND-SIDE test is opening (2026-08-20):** a Qwoted Person-on-the-Street query to hear from collectors *themselves* whether they experience this decision as unowned or underserved (see [[problem-discovery]] "The demand side"). Experts tell us how the machinery works; collectors may tell us what it does not solve, the first evidence on H7A/H7B from the buyer's side, and it may redefine who the customer is.
