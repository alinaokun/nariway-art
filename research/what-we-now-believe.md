# What We Now Believe — the intellectual history of Nariway

Not a research log. A short running record of **how our model of the business has actually changed**, and why. Its job is to fight confirmation bias and memory loss: after roughly every five cases or five external conversations, Toi asks not "what did we research?" but **"what does Alina believe today that she did not believe a month ago?"** and adds an entry. Keep it terse. A belief that never changes is not evidence of being right; it may be evidence of not looking. (Idea credit: external review, Aug 2026.)

Format per entry: **We used to believe X. [What changed.] We now believe Y.**

---

## September 2026

**On whether a private institution voluntarily abandoning its own building always signals financial distress — the twenty-sixth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-09-04, forty-fifth run.** We used to treat this dataset's "building vs. collection" comparison set ([[rubin-museum]], [[cam-raleigh]], [[rauschenberg-foundation-hq]], [[deutsche-bank-collection]]) as showing a consistent pattern: when an institution gives up a dedicated physical space while keeping its collection intact, some real funding or cost pressure sits behind the decision, even where the institution frames it as strategic (Deutsche Bank's own "refocus" language, CAM Raleigh's explicit cost line, Rubin's closure, Rauschenberg's real-estate divestment). This run deliberately searched for the sharpest available counter-case — a financially healthy private foundation closing a building it did not need to close — and coded [[zabludowicz-collection]] specifically to test it. Anita and Poju Zabludowicz's foundation closed its 16-year-old London exhibition space in December 2023, and its own most recently available accounts (FY ending 29 December 2024) show a **£211,430 surplus that same year**, on total income of £690,136 against expenditure of £478,705 — not the picture of funding exhaustion every prior case in this comparison set shows. The foundation's own stated reason is a deliberate access/reach strategy pivot (expanding free loans to institutions and digital programs over maintaining one fixed building), and a spokesperson explicitly denied any connection to a 2021 artist boycott campaign over the founders' ties to Israel, a controversy this run could not independently confirm or rule out as a contributing factor either way. We now believe this is a real, if modest, disconfirmation of the comparison set's implicit generalization: closing a dedicated public building does not always signal underlying financial distress, even inside a population (private, founder-controlled art foundations) where every prior tested case did show it. This narrows rather than reverses the broader institutional-economics finding that "money kills slowly" (Pattern 2) — Zabludowicz's own solvency is exactly why this is a genuine counter-case rather than a confirming one with different framing — while leaving open whether the closure is truly costless to the foundation's public-access mission, since the belief this evidence base tracks about "public ownership is not public access" would predict some real access erosion even from a financially clean, voluntary closure. *Unknowns:* whether the FY2024 surplus is representative of the foundation's full 16-year operating history or a wind-down-year anomaly (only one fiscal year's figures were located this run); whether the 2021 boycott controversy had any undisclosed bearing on the decision despite the foundation's own denial; whether the foundation's post-closure loan-based access model actually sustains comparable public visibility to the closed building, the same mechanism question the fourth and twenty-first hunts (Souls Grown Deep, Vogel, Queens College) have tested for ownership-based access. *Next test:* a primary-source pull of Zabludowicz Art Projects' multi-year accounts (once WebFetch unblocks) to establish whether FY2024 is typical; a follow-up check in 12-24 months on whether the foundation's loan volume and visibility actually match its own stated ambition, converting this from a governance/solvency finding into an access-mechanism one too.

**On whether independent, professional, well-funded governance is sufficient for durability, not just necessary — the twenty-fifth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-09-03, forty-fourth run, second run today.** We used to read H6/H8's governance finding (Pattern 14's 13/13 non-founder-sole survival rate; Terra's founder-sole collapse; Corcoran's slower financial exhaustion) as showing that independent, professionally-run governance is the key variable predicting whether an institution survives — every prior confirming case shares this shape, and every prior failure lacked it. This run coded [[mowaa]], the Museum of West African Art in Benin City, specifically because it stress-tests that reading from a new angle: a project with **no individual collector-founder at all**, governed instead by a dedicated UK-registered charity (Legacy Restoration Trust) with named, credible institutional co-funders — the British Museum, the German government, the Mellon Foundation — and a marquee architect (Sir David Adjaye), arguably more independent and professionally credentialed at founding than any confirming case in the evidence base, none of them a single controlling family or founder. It has stalled anyway: a November 2025 protest, asserting the Oba of Benin's traditional authority over the Benin Bronzes the museum was built partly to house, disrupted the public-opening preview, and Edo State's government revoked the project's Right of Occupancy over its own land, citing "overriding public interest." As of this run, the full public opening remains postponed indefinitely, with no resolution reported. We now believe this is a real, if narrow, disconfirmation of an assumption H6/H8 has never stated outright but has implicitly built on: that good governance is not just necessary for survival (which every prior test still supports) but *sufficient* for it. MOWAA shows a mechanism no prior case in this evidence base has surfaced — an institution can have governance at least as independent and professional as this dataset's best confirming cases, and still be stopped by a party with no board seat or governance role of any kind, asserting a competing claim over the underlying cultural patrimony and land the institution physically sits on. This does not touch the belief's core claim about *internal* failure modes (founder-death vacuum, financial exhaustion, bankruptcy-triggered liquidation), all of which MOWAA is free of; it adds a fourth, *external* failure mode — contested political/customary authority over title or patrimony — that sits entirely outside anything the case-template's governance fields (`governance_control_at_founding`, `decision_owner`, `constraints_documented`) are built to measure. *Unknowns:* whether the dispute resolves in MOWAA's favor once the land-title and authority questions are settled, which would soften this from a durability failure to a durability delay; whether a comparable case exists where contested customary/traditional authority stopped an already-well-governed Western or non-African institution, which would test whether this is a general risk or specific to postcolonial repatriation contexts where multiple legitimate claims to the same patrimony coexist by design. *Next test:* a follow-up check on MOWAA's status once the dispute resolves one way or the other; a dedicated search for a second "well-governed institution stopped by external/political contest, not internal governance failure" case, ideally outside the repatriation context, to test whether this is a general fourth failure mode or specific to contested-patrimony situations.

**On whether a credentialed advisory profession, not just a state panel or a bank desk, already owns the pre-decision — tested against the field's own membership body (the twenty-fourth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-09-03, forty-third run).** We used to read H7A's twenty prior hunts (four private-sector, one state-level) as having found real, credentialed-adjacent activity — Citi/JPMorgan's bank-embedded teams, three national tax-payment panels, a proliferating boutique wave — none of which cleared the belief's own flip condition (independent, and owning the strategic pre-decision itself, not just execution). This run's professional-ecosystem deepening (see [[market-intelligence]] #10 fifth addendum) surfaced the strongest credentialed candidate yet, tested directly rather than by accident: the **Association of Professional Art Advisors (APAA)**, an invitation-only body with **185+ member advisors across 33 cities in 11 countries**, requiring a minimum five years as a firm principal plus an advanced art-history or business-of-art degree — a real, gatekept credential, unlike anything found in the prior nineteen hunts. More pointedly, APAA's own mission materials do not confine the credential to acquisition and sale: members are described as helping collectors "elevate private collections into cultural legacies" through **estate planning**, language that reaches directly into the pre-decision territory H7A is about. On its face this is the closest any hunt has come to a credentialed profession already owning the question. It does not clear the bar on closer reading, for a reason distinct from every prior near-miss: not a conflict-of-interest problem (Citi's bank-embedded incentive, H7A's usual disqualifier) and not a narrow-slice problem (Korea's tax panel, scoped to one mechanics question) but a **standardization** problem — APAA's own no-commission ethics rule (already on file, [[market-intelligence]] #10) shows the *field* recognizes conflicted advice as a hazard, yet nothing located this run describes a standardized, credential-wide *process* a member follows to evaluate the full range of pathways (museum vs. gift vs. sale vs. retain) before a specialist is engaged — the estate-planning language reads as one experienced advisor's own judgment call, applied case by case, not a professional protocol every APAA member is trained and certified to run, the way a CPA follows GAAP or an appraiser follows USPAP. We now believe this sharpens H7A for a fifth time without breaking it: a 185-member, credentialed, invitation-gated field whose own scope already touches succession is real and is the strongest candidate found to date, but "the estate-planning conversation happens" is not the same claim as "a standardized method for making it is professionally required" — the belief's core claim (no credentialed, standardized *process* owns the pre-decision end-to-end) survives contact with the closest-fitting incumbent yet. *Unknowns:* whether any APAA training material, conference session, or published methodology actually standardizes a pre-decision framework across the membership, as opposed to individual advisors independently reaching similar practice by experience — not established by a WebSearch-only pass; whether APAA's 185-member figure undercounts non-member advisors doing equivalent work outside the credential, the same open question this evidence base has never resolved for the boutique wave either. *Next test:* a direct APAA-member interview or case study asking exactly this — is there a shared, taught pre-decision methodology, or is "estate planning is in our scope" doing less work than it sounds like; whether APAA's own conference/education materials (not located this run, WebFetch still blocked against artadvisors.org) describe any standardized framework.

**On whether H3's built-vs-lighter-model survival-rate finding holds up at double the sample size, and what a full recount surfaced that no single case ever could (the twenty-third deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-09-02, forty-second run, second run today).** We used to treat H3's 2026-08-21 finding, that built-institution pathways (found-standalone-museum, found-house-museum, found-art-park) survive at a 92% rate (23/25) statistically indistinguishable from lighter loan/touring/dispersal models' 90% (9/10), as resting on a real but modest sample (n=70 coded cases at the time). Rather than hunt for one more external counter-case on an already heavily-tested belief, this run tried to break the finding a different way: re-running its own original test on the dataset's own numbers, now at 138 cases, almost exactly double. The finding held up, and tightened: built-institution pathways now show a **93.9% definitive-outcome success rate (31/33)** — still only [[terra]] and [[di-rosa]] as failures — against lighter models' **92.3% (12/13)**, with [[pearlman-foundation]] the sole failure; the gap narrowed from ~2 points to ~1.6. This is a failed disconfirmation attempt that *strengthens* confidence through replication at scale, exactly the honest outcome the standing instruction anticipates when a belief is simply correct. But the same full recount, done for its own sake rather than to test any named hypothesis, surfaced a genuinely new, unplanned pattern: the dataset's `hybrid`/branched-pathway category, now 26 cases (up from a negligible share of the original 70-case sample, a product of this dataset's own growing sophistication at coding branched dispositions, not of the underlying population shifting), shows a materially lower success rate, **78.9% (15/19)** — four failures ([[brauer-museum-valparaiso]], [[cam-raleigh]], [[fisher-landau]], [[herbert-lust-collection]]) against 15 successes, roughly 15 points below either clean-pathway bucket. We now believe two things follow. First, H3's core claim is on firmer ground than before, not because a new argument was found but because the same argument survived a much larger, honestly-drawn (if still hand-picked) test. Second, and this is the new finding: a case's pathway *branching itself* may carry real information the current framing has never asked about — whether a disposition splits across multiple pathways may correlate with, or even indicate, an unresolved, contested, or financially strained transition, rather than being a neutral coding artifact of this dataset's own improving methodology. Two of the four hybrid failures ([[fisher-landau]], [[cam-raleigh]]) show documented financial or founder-death pressure in their own case files, consistent with this reading; two ([[brauer-museum-valparaiso]], [[herbert-lust-collection]]) involve litigation or contested consignment. This is not yet a hypothesis, only a pattern worth naming for a future run to test properly (does branching *predict* failure, or merely co-occur with the same underlying distress that also predicts failure through other channels already tracked — funding gaps, governance conflict, contested wills). *Unknowns:* whether hybrid's lower rate would survive its own disconfirmation hunt, the same courtesy every other finding in this evidence base has received; whether the category is even a single population, a branched disposition triggered by strength (MALBA's founder-driven acquisition, itself hybrid-coded) looks nothing like one triggered by distress (Fisher Landau's closure-then-auction). *Next test:* a dedicated pass isolating why each hybrid failure actually failed, to see whether branching is a cause, a symptom, or coincidental to the same fundamentals (funding, governance, founder-control) this evidence base already tracks; whether the hybrid success rate holds as the category keeps growing, now the fastest-growing single pathway value in the dataset.

**On whether financial distress alone destroys a collection, or only distress resolved a specific way (the twenty-second deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-09-02, forty-first run).** We used to read Corcoran's court-ordered dissolution (good governance, still killed by financial exhaustion) alongside Lehman Brothers' complete 2010 art liquidation (parent bankruptcy) as converging evidence that severe financial distress reliably destroys a collection regardless of governance. This run coded [[monte-dei-paschi-collection]] specifically to test that reading against the sharpest available counter-case: Monte dei Paschi di Siena, the world's oldest bank, survived a 2013-2017 crisis severe enough to hand the Italian state a 68% ownership stake — arguably a more severe distress event than Lehman's, by ownership-dilution measure — and its 500+-year art collection was never liquidated. We now believe the operative variable is not distress severity but the specific legal mechanism it resolves through: **insolvency resolved via bankruptcy-estate liquidation** (Lehman) reaches the art because a creditors' committee is legally obligated to sell assets for cash, while **distress resolved via recapitalization or state rescue** (MPS) does not trigger any comparable liquidation process, leaving the collection untouched even under equal or greater financial severity. This narrows the belief rather than reversing it: Corcoran's mechanism (financial exhaustion with no rescuer available at all) stands undisturbed, since a mid-sized nonprofit museum has no "too big to fail" rescue option the way a systemically important bank does — the open question this run leaves is whether the recapitalization-vs-liquidation distinction holds for an ordinary, non-systemic corporate collection, or whether MPS's survival depended specifically on being too consequential to let fail.

**On whether "curated placement predicts sustained access" generalizes beyond a philanthropic dispersal program to an ordinary university-gift acceptance (the twenty-first deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-09-01, fortieth run, second run today).** We used to read the fourth hunt's finding (2026-08-22) as a settled mechanism inside the "public ownership is not public access" belief: when placement is curated and matched to genuine institutional interest (Souls Grown Deep's model), donation-based ownership reliably produces sustained public access; when placement is blanket and uncurated (Vogel's model), it does not. That finding's own test population was a dedicated *dispersal program* choosing where to place work, not an ordinary museum or university *accepting* a single gift on its own initiative — this run searched directly for whether an institution that actively, willingly accepted an intact collection (the H2 "insider exception" pattern this evidence base has now confirmed seven times over) still delivers durable access once the initial exhibition moment passes. It found a real, sourced counter-example: **Queens College, part of the City University of New York, has held works by Anni Albers, Picasso, Alice Neel, and Vito Acconci in its Art Library collection and on campus for decades, and a May 2024 Art Newspaper investigation, drawing on current and former staff, documented damage to works both on view and in storage, missing objects, and insufficient cataloguing and protection resources, with storage and accessibility problems dating to the mid-1970s.** A former Queens College art librarian is quoted directly: "Chronic lack of funding is the root of all neglect," and a separate former librarian said insufficient staffing and minimal administrative support "eventually amounted to relative neglect." This is a case the college *actively wanted* (not a forced or reluctant acceptance) that nonetheless produced the same access-erosion outcome the belief's Vogel side describes, over a much longer horizon (roughly 50 years) than any case in this evidence base has tracked before. We now believe the curated-vs-blanket distinction from the fourth hunt is real but incomplete: it correctly separates two *placement strategies*, but this run's finding shows a third, independent variable — sustained institutional funding and staffing commitment *after* acceptance — that can erode access even where placement was genuinely curated (Queens College chose to hold this specific collection, unlike Vogel's one-size-fits-all model) and even absent any single dramatic crisis or governance vacuum of the kind Terra or Corcoran show. This sharpens rather than reverses the belief: "public ownership is not public access" now has at least two distinct failure mechanisms behind it — mismatched/uncurated placement (Vogel) and curated placement followed by chronic underfunding of stewardship (Queens College) — and the report's forthcoming access-mechanism table should carry both, not treat curated placement alone as sufficient. *Next test:* whether a second university or public-museum acceptance case shows the same chronic-underfunding erosion, to establish whether this is a university-sector-specific risk (public institutions facing the same funding pressure Pattern 11's [[ogden]] case already showed) or a general risk that also threatens well-resourced private museums; a primary-source check (Queens College's own conservation/collections-care budget history, not located this run) would sharpen the funding-mechanism claim beyond the Art Newspaper's staff-interview account.

**On whether a genuinely arm's-length museum acceptance has now been found, after two prior candidates each turned out to carry an undisclosed tie (the twentieth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-09-01, thirty-ninth run).** We used to read the sixteenth hunt's finding, that both prior H2 "arm's-length" candidates (Artur Walther, [[kirk-edward-long-famsf]]) turned out on closer inspection to carry a real prior institutional or personal-professional tie, as evidence that a truly cold acceptance might not exist in this evidence base at all — the belief's own "next test" line asked directly for one. This run searched fresh 2026 museum-gift announcements specifically hunting for a collector with no board seat anywhere in the receiving field and no located curatorial relationship, and found a promising lead: Nancy Delman Portnoy's March 2026 gift of 25+ works to the Syracuse University Art Museum. Portnoy is a New York-based collector, gallerist, and educator who sits on the boards of the New Museum, the Bronx Museum of the Arts, and the New School's Vera List Center for Art and Politics — a genuinely well-connected figure in the art-board world, but **none of those boards is Syracuse**, and no source located this run places her as a Syracuse alumna, trustee, or prior donor. One apparent tie initially looked promising and then dissolved on verification, worth recording as a method note: a Syracuse trustee, Elliott I. Portnoy (Dentons' global CEO, a Syracuse alumnus), shares her surname, but a direct check of his own public biography found his spouse is Estee Portnoy, not Nancy Delman Portnoy — a coincidental surname match, not a family tie, and the kind of false-positive this belief's own insider-exception reading needs to keep ruling out explicitly rather than assuming. What the sourced reporting does show is a different, softer connective mechanism than either the trusteeship (Heckler, Caplan, Hsu-Tang) or the multi-year curatorial-partnership (Long/Barryte) shapes already coded: the gift is explicitly credited to having been **facilitated by a Syracuse alumna, Elizabeth "Liz" C. Tenenbaum**, acting as a connector between the donor and the university, with no indication Tenenbaum herself is a board member either. We now believe this is the closest the evidence base has come to a genuinely arm's-length acceptance — no trusteeship, no multi-year curatorial relationship, no financial sweetener located — but it still is not a fully cold approach: a named human connector (an alumna with her own Syracuse tie) brokered the introduction, meaning the "insider exception" may need a third category alongside "donor is the insider" and "donor has a standing curatorial partner": a donor with **no** tie of her own, reached via someone **else's** institutional tie. This narrows, rather than closes, the search: a truly unmediated cold approach — no trustee, no curator, no connector with their own tie to the institution — still has not been found after three candidates now examined. *Next test:* a primary-source check (Syracuse University Art Museum's own gift-acknowledgment materials or a development-office interview) on whether Tenenbaum's role was a formal development-staff introduction or an informal personal connection, which would sharpen whether "reached via a connector" is meaningfully different from the institutional-tie pattern this belief already describes, or just a more diffuse version of the same thing.

**On whether Maine's Attorney General has exercised, or shown any sign of exercising, its charitable-trust oversight power over the Star of Hope Foundation's 2026 property sale (a direct check on the fifteenth hunt's own named "next test," 2026-09-01, thirty-ninth run).** We used to leave this an open, unresolved question: the fifteenth hunt found the Star of Hope Foundation's board voting to sell Robert Indiana's Vinalhaven home against his will's stated "museum quality... open to the public" instruction, with Maine's Attorney General holding a real, sourced charitable-trust oversight power that had not yet been tested against this specific sale. A direct search this run confirms the Attorney General's oversight role is real and reported alongside the sale announcement itself (Bangor Daily News, Press Herald: "the attorney general has oversight in the matter"), but found **no report of any actual review, objection, inquiry, or challenge** to the 2026 sale from that office — the foundation's own managing director frames the sale as a mission-driven pivot ("these properties are not our purpose — supporting artists is"), and coverage located this run treats the transaction as proceeding without legal friction. We now believe this sharpens rather than resolves the fifteenth hunt's finding: an Attorney General's oversight power existing on paper, and reported as existing, is not the same as that power being exercised, and this evidence base should not assume oversight equals enforcement absent a specific action — the same distinction the fourth hunt already drew for museum boards' own "independent" governance (see [[report-dataset]]'s Pattern 1). The practical reading strengthens H8's "enforceability, not scope, predicts durability" refinement: eight years after Indiana's death, with a legitimately constituted board and no litigation or regulatory intervention in sight, a documented written constraint appears set to be overridden with no external check materializing at all. *Next test:* whether the AG's office issues any statement once the sale actually closes (both properties are still listed, not yet sold, as of this run); a formal public-records request to the Maine AG's charitable-trust division would be the only way to confirm silence reflects a deliberate non-intervention decision versus simply not yet having reviewed the matter.

## August 2026

**On whether a large, credentialed, long-established institution (not a boutique startup) already owns the H7A pre-decision (the nineteenth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-31, thirty-eighth run, second run today).** We used to read H7A's own "would flip it" instruction — a discovered profession or institution that already owns the FULL pre-decision end-to-end, credentialed or institutionally recognized — as tested only against boutique firms (Art Fiduciary Advisors and peers, all newly founded, single-practitioner-scale) and, as of the eighteenth hunt, one narrow state mechanism (Korea's tax-payment panel). This run searched a different, larger-scale private-sector candidate directly: major private banks' in-house art advisory teams. **Citi Private Bank's Art Advisory & Finance practice has operated continuously since 1979** — forty-seven years, decades predating the "boutique wave" this evidence base dated to 2026-08-22 — led by a named team (Betsy Bickar) of trained art historians drawn from museums, galleries, and auction houses, and its own public materials describe a genuinely broad service bundle: acquisition and sale strategy, collection administration, **and** consultation on **estate and philanthropic planning** for a client's collection, alongside art-secured lending. JPMorgan Private Bank runs a comparable practice. On its face this looks closer to the credentialed, institutionally-backed, full-service advisor H7A's own flip condition describes than anything found in the prior eighteen hunts. It does not clear that bar on closer reading, for two distinct reasons. First, **independence**: Citi's team sits inside a bank whose own business model profits from art-secured lending and assets under management, the exact structural conflict-of-interest H7A's core "incentives argument" already names (an auction house benefits from sale, a bank benefits from lending against the collection rather than seeing it leave for a museum) — this is a service that exists, but not an *independent* one, the specific word H7A's own statement uses. Second, **scope**: nothing in Citi's own description of the practice claims to make or own the actual strategic question this belief is about — should this collection become a museum, a scattered gift, a sale, or stay in the family — as opposed to executing whichever of those a client has already chosen (acquisition strategy, administration, lending, and "consultation" on estate/philanthropic planning read as adjacent-execution services, not as the pre-decision itself). We now believe this sharpens H7A for a second consecutive run rather than breaking it: a large, 45-year-old, credentialed private-banking practice offering a broad art-advisory bundle is real and genuinely under-weighted in this evidence base's prior "no one owns this" framing, but it demonstrates the belief's own distinction (a bank-embedded team is not independent, and administration/execution advice is not the same as owning the strategic pre-decision) rather than refuting it. *Next test:* whether any Citi/JPMorgan client-facing case study or trade-press profile describes the team actually making, rather than executing, a client's institutional-form decision (museum vs. gift vs. sale) — not established either way by this run's WebSearch-only pass; a primary interview or first-person account (Kelly Cahn, still the next scheduled test) remains the strongest way to resolve this without further speculation.

**On whether a state can institutionalize even a slice of the pre-decision H7A claims no one owns (the eighteenth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-31, thirty-seventh run).** We used to state H7A ("no credentialed, standardized profession owns the pre-decision") having already found a proliferation of individual boutique firms independently converging on the pitch, but no discovered profession that owns it end-to-end, satisfactorily or by default. This run searched a different axis entirely: not a private profession, but a *state*. Coding [[lee-kun-hee-collection]] — the Samsung family's 2021 donation of 23,181 works to Korea's national museums, made alongside a separately paid $10.8 billion cash inheritance tax bill — surfaced South Korea's 2022/2023 legal reform (prompted directly by the political pressure of the Lee Kun-hee case and the 2020 Kansong Art Museum forced auction) allowing beneficiaries to settle inheritance tax **directly with donated art**, adjudicated end-to-end by a standing seven-member appraisal panel convened by the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism (see [[market-intelligence]] #8 fourth addendum, C78). This is a real, state-institutionalized apparatus that owns a defined decision — is this collection's tax bill payable in art, and at what value — from submission through appraisal through acceptance, with no private coordinator improvising the role the way Erskine's "alter ego to the collector" or the boutique firms named 2026-08-22 do. We now believe this is a genuine, if narrow, complication to H7A, not a disconfirmation of it: the Korean panel owns exactly one slice of the pre-decision (a tax-payment mechanics question), not the broader question this belief is actually about (should this collection become a museum, a scattered gift, a sale, or stay in the family at all) — a question Korea's own law leaves entirely to the family, the same as everywhere else this belief has been tested. Tellingly, the Lee Kun-hee donation that helped provoke the reform **did not itself use the mechanism it inspired**: it predates the law and functioned as an ordinary charitable gift paid alongside, not instead of, cash tax. The honest reading is that H7A's claim holds up once again under a genuinely new kind of test (a national government, not just private industry) for the decision it is actually about, while revealing that governments *can and do* institutionalize narrower, adjacent decisions (a tax-payment mechanism) when the incentive is specific enough (keeping culturally significant art from leaving the country) — the UK's Acceptance in Lieu (C45) and France's dation en paiement (C65) show the same pattern, now joined by a fourth national instance. *Next test:* whether any of these three tax-payment panels (UK, France, Korea) ever function informally as a *broader* legacy-planning resource for the families who use them — none of the sources checked this run describe that extension happening, but none has been asked directly either.

**On the Ohara Museum's own unresolved governance-timing question — the belief's own "next test" line, finally given a real if incomplete answer (the seventeenth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-30, second run today).** We used to carry Japan's Ohara Museum of Art as this evidence base's second genuinely inconclusive result (alongside the Gottlieb Foundation) in the long-running hunt for a founder-sole institution that survived decades past its founder's death with no independent-board correction — a real gap, not a confirming or disconfirming case either way, because no source found in the sixth hunt (2026-08-25) stated whether the museum's "public interest incorporated foundation" status existed from its 1930 founding or was adopted only later. This run searched specifically to close that gap and got a partial, genuinely new answer. Magosaburo Ohara personally founded and financed the museum in 1930 as, by every source's own description, a "private museum" (私立美術館) — no source states a board or foundation structure existed at that moment. When he died in January 1943, control did not pass to any independent board; it passed to his son, Soichiro Ohara, who ran and substantially expanded the museum through the 1950s-60s with no documented crisis, court case, or forced governance correction of the kind this belief's confirming cases (Terra, Corcoran, Dia) all eventually produced. The museum's shift to a formal "public interest incorporated foundation" is dated, by one secondary source, to **2011** specifically — a date that almost certainly reflects Japan's nationwide 2008-2013 mandatory relabeling of all zaidan hojin (foundation-type legal entities) under a reformed foundation law, not a deliberate governance correction inside this institution. Most strikingly, as of the museum's 2024 merger and rename to the Ohara Art Foundation, **Magosaburo's own great-great-granddaughter, Akane Ohara, serves as representative director, with her father, Kenichiro Ohara, as a board councillor** — meaning some line of the founding family has held the top governance seat continuously for very close to the museum's entire 95-year history, spanning a wartime founder death with no documented interruption. We now believe this is real, new texture rather than either a clean confirmation or a clean disconfirmation: unlike Terra or Wildenstein, Ohara shows no crisis, opacity-driven legal exposure, or collapse ever materializing from this multi-generational family continuity, which argues against reading "family holds the seats" as inherently unstable; but unlike Barnes, MoMA, or Gardner, it also never shows an *independent*, non-family board correcting or replacing family control at any datable moment, the specific pattern this belief's confirming cases share. The honest reading, alongside Sadberk Hanım's own three-generation Koç-family board (already coded as confirming), is that this evidence base's "independent-board" reading may need a sharper internal distinction than it has been drawing: a **multi-member, formally constituted board or foundation structure**, even one persistently dominated by one family across generations, appears to reliably avoid the founder-sole collapse mechanism (Terra's "founder's disease"), which is a different and narrower claim than "family involvement predicts failure." Ohara and Sadberk Hanım both look, on this reading, like confirming cases for the narrower claim rather than disconfirming ones — but the specific fact this belief's own "next test" line asked for (whether a *formal* foundation/board structure predates or postdates 1943) remains genuinely unresolved; what changed is that "no crisis, no correction, unbroken family continuity for 95 years" is now a documented pattern in its own right, distinct from anything previously coded. *Next test:* the exact year Ohara's board/foundation form was first established (the museum's own untranslated history pages and a 2009 Hosei University institute journal article, neither fetchable this run, are the most promising leads); whether "multi-member board, family-dominated across generations, no correction ever needed" (Ohara, Sadberk Hanım) and "multi-member board, family control replaced or diluted at some datable point" (McNay, Gardner) are two stable, equally durable sub-patterns or whether one predicts materially better outcomes than the other — not yet testable from two cases each.

**On whether the two candidate "non-insider" H2 acceptances hold up once checked more closely, the primary-source-adjacent test this belief's own "next test" line asked for (the sixteenth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-30).** We used to treat Artur Walther's gift to the Met and [[kirk-edward-long-famsf]]'s gift to FAMSF as two independent candidate instances of a museum accepting an intact collection from a collector with no located prior institutional tie, moving the insider-exception reading closer to, but not yet at, "confirmed." **On whether a documented founder constraint reliably survives the founder, once a successor governance structure is legitimately in place (the fifteenth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-29, second run today).** We used to read H8's core claim ("permanence often requires surrender") as being mainly about the *presence or absence* of binding constraints, sharpened once already by Poldi Pezzoli (constraint scope, narrow vs. broad, predicts whether a constraint survives). This run tested a different axis: whether a *specific, unambiguous, written* constraint survives once the founder is gone and a properly constituted successor board is running the institution, by coding [[star-of-hope-foundation]]. Robert Indiana's 2018 will directed that the Star of Hope Lodge, his home and studio for 40 years on Vinalhaven, Maine, be restored "to museum quality" and opened to the public; he had established the Star of Hope Foundation two years earlier, in 2016, specifically as the vehicle to carry this out. After years of estate litigation (including a dispute with the Morgan Art Foundation) delayed the Foundation's work, a fully-formed, legitimately governed board took over, resolved most of the disputes, and in August 2026, eight years after Indiana's death, voted instead to sell the Lodge and an adjacent property, redirecting the proceeds to general island arts funding. No litigation to enforce the will's restoration language has been reported. This is a materially different failure mode from every prior H8 case in the evidence base: Barnes's constraints were binding and were only modified through decades of court proceedings; Terra and Corcoran failed through governance vacuum or financial exhaustion, not a board declining a specific written instruction; Poldi Pezzoli's narrow constraint was honored. Star of Hope shows a *fourth* mechanism: a specific, documented constraint, with a dedicated funding vehicle created to execute it, simply set aside by a later, undisputed board vote, with no court test of whether the will's language was ever legally binding in the first place. We now believe H8's "founder constraints predict failure by scope, not presence" refinement (from Poldi Pezzoli) needs a further caveat: **scope alone does not determine durability — enforceability does, and a constraint with no party holding standing to enforce it (a state Attorney General's charitable-trust oversight power, untested here; or a family member willing to sue) may not survive contact with an ordinary board vote, however narrow and explicit the constraint's language.** This is a real, if single-case, complication to the "documented constraints matter" framing embedded in the case-template's own `constraints_documented` field, which has so far been coded as a predictor without asking who could actually enforce it. *Next test:* whether the Maine Attorney General's charitable-trust oversight office ever reviews or challenges the 2026 sale (which would convert this into a fifth, enforcement-tested mechanism); a second instance of a documented-but-later-overridden constraint, to see whether "no enforcing party" is the general mechanism or specific to this case's own governance history.

**On whether the same family that produced a base-rate "failure" case can also produce a "clean" one, at the same time, through a different sub-collection (the fourteenth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-29).** We used to read the thirteenth hunt's Thyssen-Bornemisza finding as a single data point: the ~1,600-work collection Baron Heinrich and Hans Heinrich Thyssen-Bornemisza assembled across six decades of sole family control failed to persist, sold outright to Spain in 1993 (775 works, $350M) after internal family conflict, the third of four tested purely-private instances to fail. A large part of it has been on renewable, **paid** loan to Spain's state-run Museo Nacional Thyssen-Bornemisza in Madrid since the early 2000s, and its revocability is not merely theoretical: when the loan agreement lapsed in January 2017 with no renewal in place, Cervera publicly threatened to withdraw the entire collection from Spain, a real, reported standoff resolved only in 2021 via a new 15-year, roughly €6.5M-a-year lease. We now believe this adds real texture to the base-rate question the thirteenth hunt opened, without simply moving the count: this is not a clean fifth instance in either direction, since Cervera's collection descends from the same family crisis that produced the 1993 failure (Cervera was herself a party to that conflict, per the thirteenth hunt's account) and her collection's OWN governance was never tested by that crisis, it was simply a separate asset outside it. What it does show is that "sole control" is not a single, family-wide condition, one Thyssen-family collection failed under internal conflict while a different, contemporaneously-held Thyssen-family collection has since persisted cleanly for over 30 years under the same surname, meaning any future base-rate count of this population should track individual collections and their own specific governance/conflict histories, not treat a family name as one data point. Cervera's arrangement is also a genuinely different shape from Niarchos's apparently uncompensated loan: hers is commercial, periodically renegotiated, and was once actually withdrawn from and fought over, showing the Niarchos-style equilibrium can be adversarial and paid, not only a gesture of quiet family goodwill. *Next test:* whether a truly independent fifth family (unconnected to Wildenstein, Nahmad, Thyssen, or Niarchos) tips the base rate further; whether Cervera's own son Borja Thyssen's 2026 Andorran legal matter over an unpaid loan (a much smaller-scale echo of the opacity-adjacent regulatory shadow first seen in Wildenstein) eventually touches this specific collection's governance, which would make it a fifth disconfirming data point rather than a complicating one.

**On whether a fourth purely-private, never-institutionalized family collection would establish a base rate for the "sole control can persist cleanly" question (the thirteenth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-28, second run today).** We used to treat the eleventh hunt's finding, that 2 of 3 tested purely-private family collections (Wildenstein, Nahmad) carried real, realized legal/regulatory fallout against only Niarchos's clean case, as suggestive but too small a sample to call representative, with the belief's own "next test" line asking directly for a fourth instance. It did not persist cleanly, but the mechanism is a *third* distinct failure mode, not a repeat of Wildenstein's or Nahmad's opacity-driven regulatory risk: internal family conflict during the founder's own lifetime. In 1983 Hans Heinrich handed the family's corporate and trust management to his son Georg Heinrich via a Bermuda-based trust, then accused him of misappropriating some £350 million; separately, the Baron's fifth wife, Carmen "Tita" Cervera, contested the paternity of that same son as part of a later attempt to dissolve a trust and gain control of assets, while another heir's own status as a co-heir to the collection was itself contested. We now believe this sharpens the base rate the eleventh hunt asked for: 3 of 4 tested purely-private, never-institutionalized family collections (Wildenstein, Nahmad, Thyssen-Bornemisza) have now failed to persist under sole control indefinitely, against 1 of 4 (Niarchos) that has; Niarchos looks more like the outlier with each additional test, not less. The Thyssen case also does something the other two did not: it shows the *same* mechanism this belief's core institutional finding already names for a governed institution (a family-conflict-driven governance vacuum, the Terra pattern) operating inside the purely-private population too, just resolved through an outright sale to a state rather than through institutional dissolution or a court case. *Next test:* a fifth purely-private instance would move this from "3 of 4" toward an actual, if still small, base rate; whether family-conflict-driven transitions (Thyssen) and opacity-driven legal crises (Wildenstein, Nahmad) are two genuinely separate risks or two faces of the same underlying "sole control eventually meets a crisis it cannot self-correct" mechanism is not yet answerable from three data points.

**On whether the Niarchos-style "sole control plus revocable loan" equilibrium generalizes, or was itself the outlier (the eleventh deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-27, second run today).** We used to treat the tenth hunt's Niarchos finding as opening a genuine third equilibrium alongside "surrender control and last" and "keep control and risk opacity/collapse": sole family control persisting cleanly for decades *and* delivering real public access via a revocable loan, with no scandal attached. **On whether sole family control can persist for decades AND still deliver real public access, without ever surrendering ownership (the tenth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-27).** We used to treat the 2026-08-26 Wildenstein finding as the working answer to H6/H8's own "next test" instruction (try purely private, never-institutionalized family art trusts as their own population): sole control *can* persist indefinitely outside the public-institution population this belief is scoped to, but only by trading the death/vacuum risk for a different, opacity-driven regulatory risk. We now believe H1 needs a two-part statement rather than one: **unadvised is indeed the statistical norm** (now with a hard number behind it, not just an inference from retention data), but **unadvised does not automatically mean badly-managed** — a family can, and in at least one well-documented case does, run its own multi-generation succession successfully with no external coordinator at all. This narrows the implicit "unmanaged → bad outcome" reading the same way the H2/H3 passes narrowed their own claims, without breaking the belief's central, now better-evidenced claim that formal, advised planning is rare.

**On whether distinctiveness actually predicts durability (the sixth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-24).** We used to hold H4/H5 ("distinctiveness/coherence predicts public-institution potential better than market value") at `low` confidence purely because it was untested — four vivid examples, not a real comparison, and explicitly flagged as "what we hope to find, so treat with suspicion." This run ran the first real cross-case test: every coded case's `collection_coherence` (tight-single-thesis / coherent-multi / broad-survey) against its `outcome_category`, excluding six too-new-to-judge `emerging`/`pivoted` cases, the same exclusion rule H3's disconfirmation pass used. If coherence drove durability, `broad-survey` collections (deliberately the LEAST distinctive/coherent category) should fail noticeably more often than `tight-single-thesis` ones. They do not: tight-single-thesis cases succeed at 87.5% (14/16: Chinati, Cisneros, the Dalí Museum, Storm King, Vogel and others, against Ganz and Pearlman as the two failures), coherent-multi at 77.3% (17/22), and broad-survey, the least-coherent bucket, at **78.6% (11/14)** — Crystal Bridges, JPMorgan Chase, MONA, Progressive, Thoma, and UBS all thriving, with only Enron, Lehman Brothers, and Marciano failing. The gap between the most- and least-coherent buckets is real but modest (about 9 points, on samples of 14-22), and broad-survey collections essentially match coherent-multi. We now believe the strong form of H4/H5, that coherence/distinctiveness is a *major* predictor of institutional survival, does NOT hold up under this dataset's own numbers; a genuinely broad, encyclopedic collection with real money and governance behind it (Crystal Bridges, JPMorgan) survives about as reliably as a tightly-focused single-thesis one. This is a real but partial disconfirmation, not a full reversal: the test only measures coherence against *survival*, not against the belief's actual comparison object (coherence vs. *market value* specifically, which this dataset does not code systematically), and every case in the sample was hand-selected for variation rather than drawn at random, so a true predictive test of H4/H5 as originally stated has still never been run.

**On whether a genuinely obscure, non-marquee sole-control museum can survive its founder for decades (the eighth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-25).** We used to treat H6/H8's own "next test" instruction — widen the hunt for a richly-endowed, sole-control survivor past marquee historical museums (Barnes, Norton Simon, Isabella Stewart Gardner, all three already checked and all three confirming) toward less-documented, still-operating institutions — as still open. This run acted on it directly: two smaller, non-marquee house museums, **The Hyde Collection** (Glens Falls, NY) and the **Taft Museum of Art** (Cincinnati, OH), were checked specifically to try to break the belief further. Both disconfirmed the disconfirmation attempt instead: Hyde's founder, Charlotte Pruyn Hyde, placed the museum under a trustee board by her own 1952 trust instrument, now 17 members, from the moment of her 1963 death and public opening; Taft's founders, Charles Phelps and Anna Sinton Taft, formed the Cincinnati Institute of Fine Arts as a Board-of-Trustees-governed vehicle in 1927, five years before the museum even opened. We now believe this sharpens rather than weakens the emerging pattern: a fourth and fifth widened search, this time deliberately choosing smaller, less-famous institutions rather than the era's most-scrutinized ones, still could not locate a founder-sole museum that simply persisted for decades with no governance transition at all — every clean example found, marquee or obscure, was placed under an independent board *by the founder's own founding instrument*, not by a later correction forced on a struggling sole-control institution. The nearest open candidate this run instead surfaced by accident: coding [[weisman]] more closely revealed a `mixed`-board structure (a named non-family officer alongside the founder's widow) rather than confirmed sole control, so even this dataset's best-looking near-miss resolves toward the same pattern on closer inspection, not against it. *Would still flip it:* a genuine founder-sole (no board named anywhere, at any point) museum surviving multiple decades past the founder's death — five searches in, still not found.

**On whether a pre-death coordinator actually redirects an estate away from the auction default (the seventh deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-24, second run today).** We used to state "sell wins by default, not by design" with no disconfirmation target ever actually tested, the belief's own *Would flip it* line ("estates routinely reaching planned, non-sale outcomes") had sat unaddressed since it was first written. A dedicated hunt this run for a well-planned estate that reached a deliberate non-sale outcome did not need to look outside our own dataset: [[pearlman-foundation]] is exactly this case. Henry Pearlman established the Foundation in **1955, nineteen years before his 1974 death** — a genuine pre-death coordinating structure, not a post-death scramble — and the eventual 2025 disposition was **63 works split as an outright gift** across three museums (Brooklyn, MoMA, LACMA) with a standing mutual-loan consortium attached, not a single lot reaching an auction house. We now believe the belief needs a caveat about *timeline*, not just outcome: even with the earliest kind of pre-death structure in this sample (19 years advance), the actual disposition still took **70 years** to execute (1955→2025) and spent most of that time as a single-host loan rather than a resolved gift — planning redirected the *outcome* away from sale, but did not make the *process* fast. The core claim (default, unmanaged transitions tend toward sale) survives; the unstated assumption that planning, once in place, resolves quickly does not. We used to treat H3's "standalone museums are a small, fragile tail" as one claim. A dedicated attempt to break it this run split it into two questions we had been answering together: how often collectors choose to found one (still genuinely rare, unchanged), and how often the ones that *do* get founded then fail (a question we had been answering by impression, not by counting). A full pull of `pathway` × `outcome` across all 70 coded cases found built-institution pathways (standalone museum, house museum, art park) reaching a **92% thriving/stable rate (23 of 25 definitive-outcome cases)**, statistically indistinguishable from lighter loan/touring/dispersal models' **90% (9 of 10)** — directly meeting the belief's own stated disconfirmation target ("standalone museums surviving at the same rate as lighter models"). We now believe the "fragile tail" framing was quietly conflating rarity-of-founding (still true) with fragility-once-founded (not supported once actually counted in our own dataset) — a real, if partial, disconfirmation. We are not fully reversing it, though: this is a hand-picked, diversity-selected sample, and we flagged a real survivorship-bias risk cutting the other way (a standalone museum that failed and dissolved decades ago, leaving no current web footprint, is much harder for a WebSearch-built candidate pool to find than one still open today) — so treat this as narrowed confidence pending a source built specifically to surface the failures we currently can't see, not as settled.

We now believe the operative variable is not distress severity but the **specific legal mechanism the distress resolves through**: Lehman's Chapter 11 filing placed the collection inside a bankruptcy estate a creditors' committee was legally obligated to liquidate for cash, while MPS's rescue was a **recapitalization** (state equity injection, later resold down) that never triggered any asset-liquidation process reaching the art at all. This is a real, if narrow, disconfirmation of the *strong* reading of "financial distress destroys collections" — it shows distress alone is insufficient; only insolvency resolved specifically through liquidation (not rescue, recapitalization, or restructuring) appears to reach a corporate collection — while leaving the Corcoran mechanism (financial exhaustion with no rescuer at all) untouched, since Corcoran had no state-scale rescue option available to it the way a systemically important bank did. *Unknowns:* whether a mid-sized, non-systemically-important corporate collection facing a rescue-not-liquidation event would fare the same as MPS (a "too big to fail" bank may be a poor proxy for an ordinary company); no art-specific balance sheet for the MPS collection was located, so its financial treatment during the crisis is inferred from the absence of any liquidation reporting, not a direct confirmation the bank's board considered and rejected selling it. *Next test:* find a mid-sized (non-systemic) corporate collection whose parent underwent a rescue/restructuring short of full liquidation, to test whether the recapitalization-vs-liquidation distinction holds outside the "too big to fail" case; watch for any Monte dei Paschi board disclosure discussing the art collection's treatment during 2013-2017, which would convert this run's absence-of-evidence into a direct confirmation. Also watch for any AG statement once the Star of Hope sale actually closes (both properties remained listed, not sold, as of this run); a formal public-records request would be the only way to confirm that silence is deliberate. **A twenty-sixth hunt (2026-09-04, forty-fifth run) tested a different boundary of this belief — not whether governance form predicts survival, but whether closing a building always means the same thing — and found a genuine, if narrow, complication rather than a new failure mechanism.** [[zabludowicz-collection]], a 16-year-old private foundation under sole founder/family control (the same `governance_control_at_founding: founder-sole` value this belief's core cases use), closed its London exhibition space in December 2023 while its own FY2024 accounts show a real £211,430 surplus that year — not the funding exhaustion this dataset's existing "building vs. collection" comparison set (Rubin, CAM Raleigh, Rauschenberg, Deutsche Bank) has shown in every prior case. This does not touch H6/H8's core survival claim directly (the foundation and collection continue; no institution died), but it weakens an adjacent, previously untested assumption this evidence base had been carrying implicitly: that a private institution's building closure is itself a proxy for financial or governance strain. The same run's [[art-car-museum]] case, by contrast, is a clean, fresh *confirming* instance of H6/H8's core mechanism — a founder-sole institution with no dedicated foundation or succession instrument, closing permanently within roughly a year of its second founder's death, found specifically by searching for exactly this kind of non-marquee failure (an explicit response to this belief's own "systematic, non-press-dependent count of failed museums" unknown) — with a genuinely new coda: a fast, voluntary, litigation-free family rescue into a related nonprofit (the Orange Show Center for Visionary Art, where a Harithas family member already sits on the board), rather than Terra's grantmaking pivot or Corcoran's court process. *Next test:* whether the non-marquee-failure search axis (deliberately searching "closed private museum" rather than general museum news) surfaces a large enough sample to move H3's survivorship-bias unknown from a named risk to an actually-measured one; whether Zabludowicz's own post-closure loan volume matches its stated access ambition, which would bear on the separate "public ownership is not public access" belief.

### The variables framework (emerging, discover-don't-invent)
The longer-term asset ChatGPT flagged: a rigorous way to examine which variables make a particular future appropriate for a particular collection, across **Collection** (coherence, significance, distinctiveness, scale, conservation burden, documentation), **Collector** (intent, time horizon, resources, desired involvement, willingness to relinquish control), **Family** (interest, capability, governance, generational alignment), **Institutional fit** (scholarly value, collection gaps, storage, curatorial capacity, geographic relevance), **Public potential** (who benefits, why accessible, what experience), and **Durability** (governance, operating economics, succession, leadership dependence, property burden). **Do NOT build this top-down.** Per [[field-definition]], the variables must be *discovered* from the cases and conversations, which is what the Evidence Base above accumulates. When enough evidence shows *which* variables actually predict durable outcomes, this crystallizes into Nariway's proprietary IP. Not yet; let it emerge.
