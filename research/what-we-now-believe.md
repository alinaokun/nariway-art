# What We Now Believe — the intellectual history of Nariway

Not a research log. A short running record of **how our model of the business has actually changed**, and why. Its job is to fight confirmation bias and memory loss: after roughly every five cases or five external conversations, Toi asks not "what did we research?" but **"what does Alina believe today that she did not believe a month ago?"** and adds an entry. Keep it terse. A belief that never changes is not evidence of being right; it may be evidence of not looking. (Idea credit: external review, Aug 2026.)

Format per entry: **We used to believe X. [What changed.] We now believe Y.**

---

## August 2026

**On the core problem.** We used to believe *dispersal* was the primary succession problem, that collections mostly hit the market when their owner dies. The transfer data showed the opposite: **retention is the norm** (most inherited art is kept). We now believe the larger problem is **unmanaged, unadvised transition**, the decision made badly or by default, not the sale itself.

**On the opportunity.** We used to believe the opportunity was **helping collectors build new museums**. The case work showed standalone founder-museums are a **small, fragile tail**. We now believe Nariway may instead own the **earlier decision, what form public legacy should take**, before lawyers, architects, and tax structures lock the answer in.

**On what predicts survival.** We had no view. From ~34 coded cases we now believe (provisionally) that **governance design at founding predicts survival better than endowment size**, an independent board and a locked succession outlast far more money under sole control. To test at 50.

**On the market's motion.** We now believe **"sell" wins by default, not by design**: the 2026 auction rebound is carried by estates, and when no independent party coordinates the decision, the auction house is the only one with a standing offer. That sharpens *who owns the decision* (H7A) into a competitive question.

**On our own writing.** We used to believe we could write good Substack Notes by inventing topics and polishing them. They read stiff and manufactured every time. We now believe Notes must come from **Alina's real published essays**, in her real voice, following what proven practitioners actually recommend.

**On ourselves (the honest one).** We now believe the biggest risk is no longer too little structure but **too much**: it has become more satisfying to build Nariway than to operate it. The internal sophistication is far ahead of the external footprint, one live discovery call, no paid client, willingness-to-pay untested. The next phase is evidence, not architecture.

**On the family-office channel (first live evidence, Dawn call 2026-08-13).** We used to treat family offices as a promising **warm referral gateway** into collector legacy decisions. Our first live conversation, with a specialist who has worked them full-time for two years, called them a **"very tough crowd"** she finds genuinely hard. We now believe the family-office lane is a **hard, transactional arena, not a soft entry**, and that Nariway should **lead with the art-world relationships** (collectors, foundation founders, curators, museum leaders) where Alina has native credibility and [[conversations-series|Artobiography]] is a natural door, treating family offices as a **downstream** channel rather than the front line. One data point, but a credible one; test it in the next conversations.

**On method (the temperament question).** Dawn models a **hyper-transactional** style, every call strategic, a reconnect turned into a coaching pitch. We now believe that style would **backfire in the art world**, which runs on trust and discretion, and that a **relationship-and-credibility-led go-to-market is correct for Nariway**, not a failure of nerve. The caveat the CFO keeps: warmth is the *acquisition method*, not permission to avoid the hard question. It still has to reach "someone asks Nariway to help with a real situation."

**On governance and failure (the first deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-20).** We used to state H6/H8 as if good governance were close to sufficient for a collection's institution to survive. A dedicated hunt for evidence to break it (Alina's new standing instruction) turned up a case already in our own dataset that we had been reading past: [[corcoran-gallery]] ran for 126 years under genuine independent, self-perpetuating board governance, no founder-sole control, no succession vacuum, and still ended in court-ordered dissolution in 2014 on pure financial exhaustion. We now believe governance design specifically prevents the *death/vacuum-triggered* collapse the belief was built to explain (Terra's clock), not institutional failure of every kind; money can still kill a well-governed institution, just slowly, the way it killed Corcoran across twelve decades instead of the few years it took to kill Terra. The narrower claim held up under a real search to break it; the broader, more comfortable version we had been sliding into did not, and we are stating that in writing rather than quietly dropping it.

**On which collectors museums actually turn away (the second deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-21).** We used to state H2 ("the receiving end is closing") as a roughly uniform description of how museums treat prospective donors. A dedicated hunt for museums *readily, enthusiastically* accepting a large intact private collection found five real 2020-2026 cases (Seattle Art Museum/Lang, the Met/Pritzker "Bluff Collection," the British Museum/Sir Percival David Foundation, Shepparton Art Museum/Gantner, Fort Wayne Museum of Art/Lowenbach) where a museum did exactly that. But four of the five donors were already structurally embedded in the receiving museum, a decades-long trustee, a board chair, or a foundation with a 15-year prior loan relationship, not an arm's-length collector with no prior tie. We now believe the closing door described by H2 applies most clearly to collectors *without* an existing institutional relationship; a collector who is already a trustee, a major repeat patron, or a long-term loan partner faces a meaningfully more open door, often with a financial sweetener attached (endowed funds, cash gifts) rather than in spite of one. This narrows H2's scope in the same way the Corcoran finding narrowed H6/H8, real evidence sharpened a claim we had been stating too broadly, rather than breaking it outright.

**On whether public ownership ever reliably delivers public access (the fourth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-22).** We used to state "public ownership is not public access" using the Vogel "Fifty Works for Fifty States" program as the clearest evidence: 2,500 works placed in museum custody across all 50 states, but a follow-up investigation found durable custody without demonstrable sustained view. A dedicated hunt this run for the opposite case, ownership that *does* reliably deliver visibility, found one already in our own dataset: [[souls-grown-deep]]'s Collection Transfer Program has placed 500+ works by 110 artists into more than 40 museums since 2016, and unlike Vogel's blanket one-museum-per-state distribution, the placements have produced repeated, verifiable exhibition life over two decades, a Whitney/traveling tour beginning 2002, a 2018 Met show ("History Refused to Die"), an 11-quilt acquisition anchoring the High Museum's own permanent collection display, and a 2025 exhibition ("Kith & Kin: The Quilts of Gee's Bend") still drawing new institutional interest, including a first-ever acquisition by Tate Modern. We now believe the belief needs a mechanism attached, not just an ownership/access binary: **when placement is curated and selective, matched to museums with genuine curatorial interest in the specific work (Souls Grown Deep's model), donation-based ownership does reliably produce sustained public access; when placement is a blanket, one-size-fits-all distribution regardless of institutional fit or interest (Vogel's model), it does not.** This is a real, sourced narrowing, not a reversal: the general finding that only ~20-30% of an art museum's collection is on view at any time (with major encyclopedic museums under 10%, per AAM-derived figures found this run) still describes the median case; Souls Grown Deep is the exception that reveals the mechanism, not proof the median has shifted.

**On whether families ever manage a transition well without a professional coordinator (the fifth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-23).** We used to state H1 ("the transition is typically unmanaged, not that collections disperse") without directly testing its implicit corollary: that an *unadvised* transition tends toward a worse outcome. A dedicated hunt this run for a family that managed succession well entirely on its own, no external advisor, attorney, or coordinator ever named in the decision chain, found one already in our own dataset: [[storm-king]] has passed through **three consecutive generations of purely family stewardship** (Ralph Ogden → son-in-law H. Peter Stern, 1974 → son John P. Stern, 2008) across 66 years, with `governance_control_at_founding: family-controlled` (not the independent-board pattern H6/H8 favors) and no advisor/attorney/coordinator role documented anywhere in the succession, and it is this sample's strongest financial performer among its found-art-park peers (net-assets-to-opex ≈9.0x, primary 990). **The other half of the search cut the other way and sharpened the belief's core claim instead of weakening it**: a dedicated search for hard survey data on planning rates found Deloitte's Art & Finance Report 2021 figure that just **over 1 in 10** collectors have formalized their estate documents with an advisor, and only **31%** of collectors who intend to leave art to family have actually discussed what they'll bequeath and resourced its care — direct, sourced confirmation that *informal* is overwhelmingly the norm, exactly as H1 already claimed. We now believe H1 needs a two-part statement rather than one: **unadvised is indeed the statistical norm** (now with a hard number behind it, not just an inference from retention data), but **unadvised does not automatically mean badly-managed** — a family can, and in at least one well-documented case does, run its own multi-generation succession successfully with no external coordinator at all. This narrows the implicit "unmanaged → bad outcome" reading the same way the H2/H3 passes narrowed their own claims, without breaking the belief's central, now better-evidenced claim that formal, advised planning is rare.

**On whether distinctiveness actually predicts durability (the sixth deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-24).** We used to hold H4/H5 ("distinctiveness/coherence predicts public-institution potential better than market value") at `low` confidence purely because it was untested — four vivid examples, not a real comparison, and explicitly flagged as "what we hope to find, so treat with suspicion." This run ran the first real cross-case test: every coded case's `collection_coherence` (tight-single-thesis / coherent-multi / broad-survey) against its `outcome_category`, excluding six too-new-to-judge `emerging`/`pivoted` cases, the same exclusion rule H3's disconfirmation pass used. If coherence drove durability, `broad-survey` collections (deliberately the LEAST distinctive/coherent category) should fail noticeably more often than `tight-single-thesis` ones. They do not: tight-single-thesis cases succeed at 87.5% (14/16: Chinati, Cisneros, the Dalí Museum, Storm King, Vogel and others, against Ganz and Pearlman as the two failures), coherent-multi at 77.3% (17/22), and broad-survey, the least-coherent bucket, at **78.6% (11/14)** — Crystal Bridges, JPMorgan Chase, MONA, Progressive, Thoma, and UBS all thriving, with only Enron, Lehman Brothers, and Marciano failing. The gap between the most- and least-coherent buckets is real but modest (about 9 points, on samples of 14-22), and broad-survey collections essentially match coherent-multi. We now believe the strong form of H4/H5, that coherence/distinctiveness is a *major* predictor of institutional survival, does NOT hold up under this dataset's own numbers; a genuinely broad, encyclopedic collection with real money and governance behind it (Crystal Bridges, JPMorgan) survives about as reliably as a tightly-focused single-thesis one. This is a real but partial disconfirmation, not a full reversal: the test only measures coherence against *survival*, not against the belief's actual comparison object (coherence vs. *market value* specifically, which this dataset does not code systematically), and every case in the sample was hand-selected for variation rather than drawn at random, so a true predictive test of H4/H5 as originally stated has still never been run.

**On which kind of museum actually fails (the third deliberate disconfirmation pass, 2026-08-21, second run today).** We used to treat H3's "standalone museums are a small, fragile tail" as one claim. A dedicated attempt to break it this run split it into two questions we had been answering together: how often collectors choose to found one (still genuinely rare, unchanged), and how often the ones that *do* get founded then fail (a question we had been answering by impression, not by counting). A full pull of `pathway` × `outcome` across all 70 coded cases found built-institution pathways (standalone museum, house museum, art park) reaching a **92% thriving/stable rate (23 of 25 definitive-outcome cases)**, statistically indistinguishable from lighter loan/touring/dispersal models' **90% (9 of 10)** — directly meeting the belief's own stated disconfirmation target ("standalone museums surviving at the same rate as lighter models"). We now believe the "fragile tail" framing was quietly conflating rarity-of-founding (still true) with fragility-once-founded (not supported once actually counted in our own dataset) — a real, if partial, disconfirmation. We are not fully reversing it, though: this is a hand-picked, diversity-selected sample, and we flagged a real survivorship-bias risk cutting the other way (a standalone museum that failed and dissolved decades ago, leaving no current web footprint, is much harder for a WebSearch-built candidate pool to find than one still open today) — so treat this as narrowed confidence pending a source built specifically to surface the failures we currently can't see, not as settled.

## The Evidence Base — the living scoreboard (upgraded 2026-08-20)
The hypothesis-keyed record of what Nariway believes and how sure it is, and the thing that (compounded and verified over time) becomes proprietary intellectual capital. **Every case and every Conversation updates it.** The after-every-conversation capture ([[conversations-series]]) and the cloud research agent both feed it. Same verification bar as the [[what-becomes-of-great-art-collections|report]], evidence is sourced or marked unknown, numbers link to [[claims-register]]. **A belief with no disconfirmation target is a bias, not a finding**, so each entry names what would flip it and the next best test. Keep entries terse. Confidence: low / medium / high, all provisional.

**H1 · The transition is typically *unmanaged*, not that collections disperse — now split into two sub-claims, one sharpened, one narrowed (2026-08-23 disconfirmation pass).** Confidence: medium-high (the "unadvised is the norm" half strengthened with a hard number; the "unadvised → bad outcome" corollary weakened by a real counter-case). *For:* retention is the norm (C3, ~72% keep; ~80% intend to pass to heirs, C12); the problem is the decision made by default; **a 2026-08-23 dedicated hunt for survey data found Deloitte's Art & Finance Report 2021 figure that just over 1 in 10 collectors have formalized estate documents with an advisor, and only 31% of collectors intending to leave art to family have actually discussed what they'll bequeath and resourced its care** [secondary, Deloitte Art & Finance Report 2021 via search synthesis] — the belief's first hard number, not just an inference from retention data. *Against:* the "great estate rush" shows real dispersal at death when no coordinator exists; **the same 2026-08-23 pass, hunting specifically for a family that managed succession well with NO external advisor/attorney/coordinator ever in the decision chain, found [[storm-king]] already in our own dataset** — three consecutive generations of purely family stewardship (Ogden → son-in-law Stern, 1974 → son John Stern, 2008) across 66 years, `governance_control_at_founding: family-controlled` (not the independent-board pattern H6/H8 favors), no advisor role documented anywhere, and the strongest 990-verified financial performer among this sample's found-art-park peers (≈9.0x net-assets-to-opex). *Cases:* corporate dispersals (Lehman, Enron) vs the family retentions; [[storm-king]] as the clearest unadvised-and-thriving case found so far. *Conversations:* Colleen, Erskine, Findley on what families actually do. *Unknowns:* whether Storm King is a genuine, findable-in-numbers pattern or a single favorable outlier (the same survivorship-bias risk H3's disconfirmation pass flagged — a family that managed its own succession badly, with no press footprint, is much harder for this sample to find than one still thriving); how often "unmanaged" yields a bad outcome vs a fine one, at scale rather than in one case. *Would flip it further:* a systematic count of unadvised family successions (not just the successes visible in press) showing the good/bad split directly. *Next test:* the advisor/attorney conversations; a future run's full pull of `decision_owner`-at-succession × `outcome` across the coded set, the same method that produced H3's Pattern-14-style finding.

**H2 · Collectors overestimate museums' willingness to take collections intact; the receiving end is closing — for collectors without an existing institutional tie.** Confidence: medium (a 2026-08-21 dedicated disconfirmation attempt narrowed the belief's scope rather than breaking it). *For:* museums decline gifts on space/budget/fit/forever-cost (report §4.5); the Bank of America 90-95% decline quote; independent corroboration from The Art Newspaper's "collectors recruit new allies" piece, describing collectors needing intermediary firms just to get a museum to say yes. *Against:* donation *intentions* stay common (C2), so it is narrower than it looks; and **a 2026-08-21 dedicated hunt for museums readily accepting an intact collection found five real, sourced 2020-2026 cases** (Seattle Art Museum/Lang, the Met/Pritzker "Bluff Collection," the British Museum/Sir Percival David Foundation, Shepparton Art Museum/Gantner, Fort Wayne Museum of Art/Lowenbach) — but four of the five donors were already a long-serving trustee, a board chair, or a 15-year prior loan partner of the receiving museum, and several gifts came bundled with a cash sweetener (an endowed fund, a capital gift). *Cases:* Hillwood (Smithsonian returned it); the five 2020-2026 "readily accepted" cases above, read as evidence for an *insider* exception rather than against the belief generally. *Conversations:* **Leslie Anderson is the direct test** (why a museum declines). *Unknowns:* the actual decline rate and its drivers for a *true arm's-length* collector, still untested directly; whether the insider/outsider distinction holds at scale, not just in five cases. *Would flip it further:* an arm's-length collector, with no prior trustee/loan tie to the receiving museum, having a large intact collection readily accepted with no negotiation and no financial sweetener — not yet found despite a specific search. *Next test:* Leslie; and the Penn Cultural Heritage Center's National Survey of Museum Collecting Practices (the first field-wide, evidence-based US survey of acquisition/deaccession practice found this run; its collection window closed August 20, 2026, results expected 2027 — a source to check again next year, not usable yet).

**H3 · Standalone founder-museums suit only a minority (rarity of founding); survival once founded is a SEPARATE claim, now narrowed 2026-08-21.** Confidence: medium (down from medium-high — a dedicated disconfirmation attempt this run met the belief's own stated flip condition on the survival-rate half; the rarity-of-founding half is untouched and still holds). *For (rarity, unchanged):* founding a standalone museum at all remains rare relative to inheritance/retention/lighter models. *For (fragility, historically asserted):* Barnes (constraint-locked), the failures Terra/Corcoran. *Against (new, 2026-08-21):* a full `pathway` × `outcome` pull across all 70 coded cases (excluding six too-new-to-judge `emerging`/`pivoted` cases) found built-institution pathways (found-standalone-museum, found-house-museum, found-art-park; n=25) at a **92% thriving/stable rate (23/25)** — statistically indistinguishable from lighter models (long-term-loan, traveling-program, intentional-dispersal; n=10) at **90% (9/10)**. The sample's only two heavy-built failures are Terra and di Rosa against 23 successes; the only light-model failure is Pearlman against 9 successes. *Cases:* MoMA, Menil, Shelburne, Fondation Beyeler, Glenstone (heavy-built successes) vs Fisher→SFMOMA (loan), Vogel (dispersal), Souls Grown Deep (dispersal) — success on both sides of the pathway divide. *Conversations:* Gretchen (durability), Cortney (nomadic, no building at all). *Unknowns:* this is a hand-picked, diversity-selected sample, not a random draw of all attempted standalone museums — and a specific survivorship-bias risk cuts *against* this run's own disconfirming finding: a standalone museum that failed and dissolved decades ago, with little remaining web footprint, is structurally harder for a WebSearch-built candidate pool to find and code than one still operating today, so the true historical failure rate (including forgotten failures) is very likely higher than this sample's 8%. *Would flip it further:* a systematic, non-press-dependent count of failed/dissolved standalone museums (e.g., a state charity-registry dissolution list) directly testing the survivorship-bias concern. *Next test:* keep rarity-of-founding and survival-once-founded as two separately tracked sub-claims going forward, not one bundled hypothesis; the disconfirmation research pass; Gretchen.

**H6 / H8 · Governance design at founding predicts survival better than endowment size; permanence often requires surrendering control.** Confidence: medium (a dedicated disconfirmation attempt this run sharpened the belief's scope rather than breaking it). *For:* Pattern 14 (first fully-counted governance table, 13/13 non-founder-sole cases survived their founder); Terra failed with ~$450M under sole control; survivors had independent boards and locked succession; Poldi Pezzoli's 145-year survival; [[dia-art-foundation]] (new 2026-08-20) ran the experiment on itself, founder-sole control 1974-1985 produced the same governance vacuum the belief predicts, but the crisis hit while the controlling founder was still *living* (not at death) and was resolved by a forced board handoff before assets ran out, surviving rather than collapsing like Terra; **Erskine (T&E attorney, published 2026-08-21) independently names the mechanism — "founder's disease: the founder so dominates the Board and administration that there is no room for professional management, and when the founder is gone, the organization collapses" — a practitioner's articulation of exactly the Terra failure mode, from a seat outside the case data.** **A third dedicated hunt (2026-08-23, twenty-second run) for the mirror case — a richly-endowed, founder-sole, no-succession-plan institution surviving decades past a founder's death — checked the Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum specifically because its will-locked, Barnes-style display restrictions made it look like a strong sole-control candidate. It is not one**: Gardner's 1924 will placed the museum directly under a **self-perpetuating Board of Trustees** from the outset, not her personal heirs or a sole controller — governance sourced, spot-verified via the museum's own site — making it a third confirming case (alongside Barnes and Norton Simon) rather than the disconfirming one this run specifically searched for. *Against:* **a 2026-08-20 dedicated hunt for disconfirming evidence found a real case**: [[corcoran-gallery]], already in the dataset, ran 126 years under genuine independent, self-perpetuating board governance, no founder-sole vacuum, and still ended in court-ordered dissolution (2014) on pure financial exhaustion, good governance did not prevent institutional death, only a different, slower kind of it. *Cases:* Barnes (control judicially rewritten to survive) vs MoMA (independent board from founding) vs Corcoran (independent board from founding, still dissolved on money) vs Dia (founder-sole, crisis survived by a forced 1985 handoff) vs Gardner (board-governed from founding by design, not by later correction). *Conversations:* Gretchen, Erskine (foundation governance). *Unknowns:* whether it holds at 75-100 cases; true endowment still unpulled (WebFetch blocked); whether "good governance predicts survival" should be read narrowly (prevents a death/vacuum-triggered collapse specifically, which the evidence so far supports) or broadly (prevents institutional failure of any kind, which Corcoran directly disconfirms); this run's search checked only iconic, historically well-documented museums (Barnes, Norton Simon, Gardner) — a systematic, not just impression-led, sweep for a sole-control survivor has still never been run. *Would flip it further:* a second Corcoran-style case would confirm the belief needs a permanent scope narrowing, not just a caveat; a genuine richly-endowed sole-control long-term survivor, still not found after three searches across three of the era's most-scrutinized private museums, would weaken it from the other side — three independent failures to find one is itself a modest strengthening of the belief, though not proof of a universal law. *Next test:* the depth/verification pass; more cases; a fourth hunt should widen past marquee historical museums toward less-documented, still-operating sole-control institutions specifically, since the marquee set keeps confirming.

**H7A · No single profession owns the pre-decision (the unowned decision).** Confidence: low-medium (a 2026-08-22 dedicated disconfirmation attempt found no credentialed profession that owns this end-to-end, a real negative result that sharpens rather than breaks the belief), with the FIRST substantive cross-seat support (Erskine, published 2026-08-21). *For:* the incentives argument (auction house benefits from sale, museum wants the gift, attorney does structure, none owns "what should this become before we pick a specialist"); **Erskine (T&E attorney) independently confirms BOTH halves — (a) a real pre-structure decision exists ("identify the *Common Goal shared by all the stakeholders*" BEFORE recommending any trust/foundation/ownership structure), and (b) coordination is necessary and normally absent: professionals "see clearly within their specialties but dimly beyond them, unless someone coordinates and serves as an *alter ego to the collector*, Beta to the collector's Alpha."** **A 2026-08-22 dedicated hunt for a profession that already owns this end-to-end, run specifically to try to break H7A, found none**: no licensing body, membership organization, or peer credential exists for a defined "collection legacy strategy" role — APAA lists "Art Succession Planning" only as a single webinar topic, not a distinct practice area, and ACTEC has no art-specific legacy credential, treating art as one asset class inside ordinary T&E practice. *Against / the honest tension:* Erskine adds such coordinators DO exist "within family offices or among trusted professional advisors," so the role is *sometimes* filled; **and the same 2026-08-22 hunt surfaced a proliferation of individual boutique firms independently converging on exactly this pitch** — Art Fiduciary Advisors (Doug Woodham, ex-President Christie's Americas, explicitly framed as a fiduciary "working in partnership with" the collector's existing attorney/tax team), Legacy Art Partners, smART Advisory, Bultman Advisory, The Twentieth — none standardized, credentialed, or shown to have real market share, but a real, current market signal that solo entrants sense the same gap H7A describes and are independently testing paid demand for it (better read as new evidence for H7B than against H7A). **Refinement, sharpened further:** H7A is now specifically "no *credentialed, standardized profession* owns the pre-decision," not "no one ever coordinates" and not "no one is trying to sell this." The coordinating role is real but *improvised or individually entrepreneurial* — filled ad hoc by a family office/attorney, or pitched solo by a boutique practice, never yet institutionalized into a recognized profession with a credential, an association, or a training pathway. *Cases:* McNay = the prepared exception; most cases lack it. *Conversations:* Erskine (confirms the gap as improvised, a strong first attorney read); **Kelly Cahn (art advisor, the OTHER incumbent-candidate seat, Sept 10) and Colleen Boyle are the next direct tests.** *Unknowns:* whether the ad hoc "alter ego" is *adequate* or leaves room for a defined independent owner; whether collectors reliably know to appoint one (Erskine implies many do not); whether any of the boutique firms found this run (Art Fiduciary Advisors especially) achieve real scale or stay a single-practitioner niche. *Would flip it:* a discovered profession that already owns this end-to-end, satisfactorily and by default, credentialed or institutionally recognized — still not found after a dedicated search. *Next test:* Kelly, Colleen; a check on whether Art Fiduciary Advisors or a peer firm gains visible traction over the next few runs; keep trying to disprove in every conversation. *Actively try to disprove this in every conversation.*

**H7B · Someone will pay an independent party to own that decision (willingness to pay).** Confidence: low (the commercial crux, only market contact proves it). *For:* the surrounding industry is monetizing adjacent services (BofA art consulting 2026; 51%→ wealth managers offer art services, C10). *Against:* the pre-decision may have no natural fee event (unlike a transaction); $0 earned, no paid engagement yet. *Cases:* n/a (market question). *Conversations:* watch for the pull signal ("I have a family you should talk to" → "can you help us?"). *Unknowns:* who pays (collector vs family office vs attorney) and on what fee event. *Would flip it (confirm):* someone asking Nariway to help with a real situation. *Next test:* the discovery conversations; a first paid engagement.

**Sell wins by default, not by design.** Confidence: medium. *For:* the 2026 auction rebound is carried by estates; when no independent party coordinates, the auction house is the only one with a standing offer. *Against:* some estates plan deliberately. *Cases:* de la Cruz (fast sale after a death), the estate-rush coverage. *Conversations:* Colleen, Erskine. *Would flip it:* estates routinely reaching planned, non-sale outcomes. *Next test:* the advisor conversations.

**Public ownership is not public access; "public life" is multidimensional — now with a named mechanism for when ownership DOES deliver access (2026-08-22 disconfirmation pass).** Confidence: medium-high, refined not weakened. *For:* Vogel gave 2,500 works into public custody across 50 states via a blanket, one-museum-per-state distribution, yet sustained public *view* is unproven ([[vogel-fifty-states-afterlife]]); the general finding that only ~20-30% of an art museum's collection is on view at any time (under 10% at major encyclopedic museums) supports "ownership ≠ access" as the median case; conversely a long-term loan (Fisher) can yield high public view without public ownership at all. *Against / refining:* a 2026-08-22 dedicated hunt for ownership that *does* reliably deliver access found [[souls-grown-deep]]'s Collection Transfer Program — 500+ works placed in 40+ museums since 2016 via **curated, museum-by-museum placement matched to genuine curatorial interest**, not a blanket distribution — has produced repeated, verifiable exhibition life over two decades (a 2002 traveling tour, a 2018 Met show, an 11-quilt anchor acquisition at the High Museum's permanent galleries, a 2025 Met-adjacent exhibition, and a first Tate Modern acquisition). *Cases:* Vogel, Fisher, the freeport paradox (art held in the dark, C15) on one side; Souls Grown Deep on the other. *Conversations:* Alexis (visible-in-a-workplace vs accessible), Sage (available vs cared-about), Kate Bryan. *Unknowns:* whether Souls Grown Deep's curated-placement mechanism generalizes beyond one foundation's specific subject matter (Black Southern vernacular art, which arrived with unusual institutional demand already built by 2000s-2010s scholarship and exhibitions) or is itself a special case; which dimensions predict a "durable public life" more generally. *Would flip it further:* a curated-placement program in a less institutionally fashionable category showing the same sustained-access result — not yet tested. *Next test:* the availability-vs-access answers from the first wave; score cases on the [[field-definition]] dimensions; a second curated-placement case in a different collecting category.

### The variables framework (emerging, discover-don't-invent)
The longer-term asset ChatGPT flagged: a rigorous way to examine which variables make a particular future appropriate for a particular collection, across **Collection** (coherence, significance, distinctiveness, scale, conservation burden, documentation), **Collector** (intent, time horizon, resources, desired involvement, willingness to relinquish control), **Family** (interest, capability, governance, generational alignment), **Institutional fit** (scholarly value, collection gaps, storage, curatorial capacity, geographic relevance), **Public potential** (who benefits, why accessible, what experience), and **Durability** (governance, operating economics, succession, leadership dependence, property burden). **Do NOT build this top-down.** Per [[field-definition]], the variables must be *discovered* from the cases and conversations, which is what the Evidence Base above accumulates. When enough evidence shows *which* variables actually predict durable outcomes, this crystallizes into Nariway's proprietary IP. Not yet; let it emerge.
