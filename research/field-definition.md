# Field Definition — the intellectual project (v0.1)

**FIELD NAME: UNKNOWN.** Deliberately. Naming it early bends the research to fit the name. First establish the phenomenon, the unit of analysis, the boundaries, the lifecycle, the decisions, the principles, the results, and the professional gaps. Then ask what it is called, or whether it already has a name.

*Origin: an Aug 2026 reframe of Nariway from "a distinctive art advisory" to a possible **intellectual project**, is there an important, undefined field around the life of private art collections? The method is Drucker's, not the ambition. He did not invent management by naming it; he saw a discipline lying fragmented across professions, then integrated and tested it. **Build the field, don't announce it.***

## The one question above everything
> **How can a private art collection have a larger life?**

"Larger" is not "public." A larger life can mean more people see it, more scholarship comes from it, the artists in it gain recognition, works travel, a university uses it, it becomes an institution, parts enter existing museums, the family engages more deeply, or it is dispersed in a way that increases its influence. Everything Nariway does attacks this one question from a different direction.

## The phenomenon (hypothesis, not fact)
Private collections are built one decision at a time and can become more than the sum of their objects. But the professional infrastructure around them is fragmented, the advisor acquires, the appraiser values, the insurer protects, the conservator preserves, the registrar records, the attorney structures, the wealth advisor manages the money, the auction house sells, the museum evaluates gifts. Each profession owns one event. **Almost no one is responsible for the collection as a whole, over its life, and what it could become.** First hypothesis, to be disproved, not assumed.

## The unit of analysis (the key intellectual move)
The art world's default unit is the **artwork**. Wealth management sees an **asset**; estate planning, **property to transfer**; insurance, **risk**; conservation, **stewardship**. Nariway's unit is different: **the collection itself**, an entity deliberately assembled over time that develops its own identity, needs, economics, relationships, and possible futures. Change the unit from the artwork to the collection, and the timeframe from the transaction to the lifespan, and a new set of questions appears.

## The honest test (elbows vs skeleton)
Drucker's image: earlier writers described the elbow without the arm or the skeleton. Our test is the same, and we must run it before claiming anything. Parts already exist as professions. **Collection *management*** (registrars, collections specialists, ARCS) is real, but **operational**, cataloguing, storage, loans, care. The private-museum literature (Larry's List, Georgina Walker) documents outcomes. Estate and legacy planning handles the transfer. **What appears missing is the *strategic and developmental* view, what a collection could become over its whole life, matched to the collector's resources and intent, including forms that do not yet exist.** Action: a real literature review, and ask every practitioner *who studies the collection as a whole?* If someone already integrates it, join and advance their discipline. If it is all elbows and no skeleton, the field is real.

## The central tension (the most ownable research question)
**Private ownership and public access are not opposites.** The vocabulary makes them binary; reality is a **spectrum**, own and lend for ten years, open by appointment, tour, rotate through universities, fund access instead of a building, digital scholarship, structured public weekends. So a concrete, tractable, ownable question falls out:
> **How many ways can privately owned art be shared?**

Nariway should aim to give the world's best answer to that. It is answerable from the case evidence, and it is the natural spine of the [[flagship-report]] and of Artobiography.

### "Public life" is not binary (a distinction discovered from a case, 2026-08-16)
The Vogel afterlife investigation ([[vogel-fifty-states-afterlife]]) forced a sharper version of this. We had been using "public life" and "public access" loosely, as if giving art to the public settled it. Vogel breaks that. The Vogels moved 2,500 works into public *ownership* across 50 states, yet whether those works are *seen* is unproven, so **public ownership did not deliver public access.** The mirror case is Fisher ([[fisher-sfmoma]]): a long-term loan kept the art in *private* ownership yet plausibly produced far more public *view*. So the two cases cross, and the lesson is that "public life" is not one thing but a set of separable dimensions:
> **ownership · custody · visibility (frequency of display) · physical access · scholarship · education and teaching · geographic reach · number of people reached.**

A collection can score high on some and near-zero on others (Vogel, high on ownership/custody/geographic reach, unknown-to-low on visibility). This is exactly the kind of distinction the field should **discover from cases rather than invent in the abstract**, and it is testable across the whole [[report-dataset|database]], score each case on the dimensions and see whether "giving art a larger public life" resolves into a small number of real patterns. If it does, the field may have found one of its first genuine measurement frameworks. Do not over-formalize it yet; let more cases populate it first.

## The managerial problem (why the finance/strategy lens matters)
Once a collector asks "what could my collection become?", the answer stops being about art and becomes capital, governance, family, tax, ownership, risk, operations, people, property, institutions, time, and purpose. That is management, the coordination of resources toward a desired result. Nariway approaches collections not as an art historian asking what they *mean*, nor as a market professional asking what they are *worth*, but as a management thinker asking: **what is this collection capable of accomplishing, and what would it require?** That question is not native to the art world, and it is Alina's genuine differentiator.

## First principles (hypotheses to break, not doctrine)
- A collection can become more than property.
- Ownership and access are separate decisions.
- A museum is one answer, not the answer.
- Preserving the collection is not always preserving its value or influence.
- The future should be designed while the collector can still participate (their knowledge and relationships vanish after death).
- Money is necessary but does not create durability; governance, purpose, leadership, relevance, and adaptability do.
- Public value is not proportional to market value.
- There is no universally correct ending.
- **The best future for a collection begins with what it is capable of contributing, not with the structure the collector wants to build.**

## The method (one knowledge loop, not four projects)
**Observe → Explain → Invent → Apply → observe again.**
- **Observe** what collectors actually did (Artobiography + the [[report-dataset|case database]]).
- **Explain** the patterns (the report, [[what-we-now-believe]]).
- **Invent**, the distinctive frontier: not just cataloguing the existing menu but *expanding* it. What arrangements should exist that do not? (Shared infrastructure across private collections; a university hosting a private collection for 15 years; funding access rather than a building; structured public-access periods for private homes; rotating regional programs among small museums.) **Expand the menu, don't just study it.**
- **Apply**, eventually take one real collection, ask what it could become, and assemble the specialists to execute. This closes the loop. See [[decision-map]] (which currently begins late in a collection's life; the field begins earlier, at formation).

## Mission vs intellectual obligation (the guardrail)
Alina's **personal mission**: more people see the art now in private collections. Keep it, it answers *why this matters*. But the **discipline's obligation** is to determine what makes sense for the collection and its owner, and it must be able to conclude "keep it private," "don't build the museum," "your children should inherit it," "open six weekends a year." Holding the two apart is what keeps Nariway from being advocacy disguised as advice. (Same guardrail as the [[positioning]] north star and the [[nariway-vision-access|vision]]: the public benefit must be real, not assumed.)

## Drucker's five questions (answered honestly, mostly UNKNOWN)
- **Mission?** Not yet fixed. Personal mission known; the company sentence waits until the business is clearer.
- **Customer?** Hypothesis: a collector or family who controls a collection and wants to know what more it could do. But the person who *feels* the problem may be the next generation, a family-office exec, a trustee, or an estate attorney. Untested (H7A).
- **What does the customer value?** The giant unknown. "Preserve what I built" / "show me possibilities I don't know exist" / "help me share without giving it away" / "make sure my kids aren't stuck with a mess" / "tell me if my museum idea is crazy before I spend $50M." **This is what the conversations must discover** (H7B).
- **Results?** Not "12 engagements" (an output). Candidates: collections given new futures, works newly accessible, non-viable institutions avoided, new models created, collections still culturally useful after their founders.
- **Plan?** Learn. Publish. Test. Apply. Repeat.

## What this does NOT change (the discipline, read this last)
This is the highest-ceiling idea Nariway has had, which is exactly why it is dangerous, thrilling ideas are how "build instead of do" begins, and the CFO is right that the machine has grown enough. The saving grace, and it is real: **the field thesis and the business validation are the same inquiry.** The upcoming conversations test both (see the field-test questions in [[problem-discovery]]). So this raises the ceiling; it does not move the next step. Artobiography is the observe-and-explain engine; the conversations are the practitioner knowledge and the field test; the report is the pattern output; inventing new models is the frontier. **Do not write more of this document instead of writing an essay and having the conversations. This is a byproduct of the work, never a substitute for it.**
