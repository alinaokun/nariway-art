## The overarching question (2026-08)
> **How can a private art collection have a larger life?**

Above everything sits this one question (see [[field-definition]], the intellectual project). "Larger" is not "public", it can mean scholarship, recognition, travel, institutional life, family engagement, or influential dispersal. The master question below is one route into it. Every part of Nariway attacks the overarching question from a different angle: Artobiography (how have others done it?), Research (what patterns?), interviews (what do practitioners know that isn't written down?), Institution Building (what does each possibility require?), the Decision Map (how should the choice be made?), advisory (what should *this* collection do?).

## Master question

> **What happens to significant private art collections over time, what makes some of them suitable for public life, and what new institutional models can bring more privately held art into meaningful public view?**

Museums are one answer. Foundations, university partnerships, house museums, art parks, lending programs, seasonal exhibition spaces, appointment-only-to-public transitions, gifts (intact or scattered), sales, dispersal, retention — and models that don't have a name yet — are all inside the territory. The unit of analysis stays the **collection and its pathway**; the set of pathways is wide.

### Scope note — corporate and institutional collections (in scope, as a distinct category)
The public wedge stays **private-individual** collections, but the research explicitly includes **corporate collections** (UBS, Deutsche Bank, JPMorgan Chase, and the rest) as a separate, tagged sub-population, coded with the same schema under `origin: corporate`. They belong for three reasons. **They face the same "what becomes of it" moment**, only the triggers differ — a merger, a bankruptcy, a new CEO, a cost or return-to-office or ESG decision, a brand-strategy shift, rather than a death. **They are a contrast class:** the decision-owner is a committee or board rather than a person or family, and governance is maximal by design, so they test whether *governance-beats-endowment* still holds at the ceiling of formal governance. And **the record is unusually clean** — corporate dispersals (Lehman, Enron, Reader's Digest) and public-life models (Deutsche Bank's PalaisPopulaire, Bank of America's free lending program, corporate foundations like Cartier and Louis Vuitton) are well documented and citable. There is also a possible **adjacent B2B market** here: a company deciding what becomes of its collection needs the same independent strategic read Nariway imagines for a collector — noted, not pursued yet. Seed list in [[candidate-universe]].

## North star (a governing value, not a slogan)

> **Bring important art out of private storage and into meaningful public life.**

This is the research expression of Nariway's vision, canonical in [[positioning]] ("Why Nariway exists"). The collector receives a legacy. But the **public benefit has to be real** — this is the line that keeps Nariway from being "help wealthy people build monuments to themselves."

**The public-benefit test** (formerly conceived as a separate "Cultural Gaps" desk — folded in here on purpose): a collection's case for public life is strongest when it *fills a gap a region or field actually has* — a community, tradition, medium, or point of view that major institutions systematically leave out — not merely when it is expensive. Distinctiveness and public need, not market value, are the primary lens.

## The four inquiry areas (permanent questions — NOT yet standing desks)

Kept as questions. Each earns "standing, automated" status only when real work shows it needs continuous watching. Note they are **not the same kind of function:**

1. **Transfer — "What significant art is approaching a change in ownership?"**
   *Type: build-once model, refresh annually.* **Interrogated — see [transfer-scope-note.md](transfer-scope-note.md).** The $992B = 5% × $19.84T (UHNW share of a $31T decade transfer); it is *art **and collectibles***, global, **ownership transfer not sales**, resting on one unsourced 5% lever. Most inherited art is **retained** (72% keep at least some), but outcomes are **not exclusive** — donation *intentions* are widespread (Art Basel/UBS: ~49% have museum-gift plans, ~70% hope to donate some works eventually) even as *founding a new institution* stays rare. The model must treat each collection as a **portfolio of simultaneous dispositions** (retain + sell + donate + lend + institutionalize), not a single winner. Finite analysis, not radar.

2. **Models — "How is privately held art already being brought into public life?"**
   *Type: continuous scan (genuinely radar-like). Highest immediate value.* The Onera desk. Find every interesting model — small foundations, exhibition spaces, art parks, university partnerships, seasonal venues, lending programs, and especially **abandoned/changed museum plans** (e.g. Berkowitz → Longleaf Art Park). This is the desk that corrects the museum bias, so it runs *first*.

3. **Collection Intelligence — "Which collections may face a public-life decision?"** *(renamed from Collector Intelligence — the collection, not the wealthy person, is the unit.)*
   *Type: continuous map — proceed now.* Tracks the collection and its signals: focus, approx. size, geography, public exposure, existing institutional relationships, foundation, current access, public succession/legacy statements, family involvement where publicly discussed, recent loans/exhibitions, pathway signals. **One-page guardrail (sufficient to start):** *public sources only; no inferred private family circumstances, health, confidential wealth estimates, addresses, private contacts, or speculation about intentions; separate documented statements from Nariway inference.* Sources: ARTnews Top 200, museum boards, foundation sites & tax filings, public interviews, catalogues, press, loan records.

4. **Potential — "What kinds of collections create compelling, sustainable public experiences?"**
   *Type: analytical / emergent — accretes from cases.* Split into **two independent dimensions, scored separately:**
   - **Institutional viability** — *can* it support an institution? (collection size, financial support, facilities, conservation burden, governance, operating cost, endowment, staffing).
   - **Cultural opportunity** — *should* it become public? What can people see here they can't easily see elsewhere — a region, an overlooked artist group, a category, a historical story, a community, a medium, a tradition, a coherent point of view? *The best small institution may not have the best art; it has the strongest reason to exist.*
   Working hypothesis (**untested**): distinctiveness/coherence and cultural opportunity may matter more than market value.

## Hypotheses (funnel-ordered, all provisional)

- **H1** — Most significant private collections will *not* remain intact through intergenerational transfer without deliberate planning.
- **H2** — Collectors frequently overestimate existing museums' willingness to accept collections intact.
- **H3** — Standalone museums suit only a minority of collections capable of meaningful public impact.
- **H4** — Collection coherence/distinctiveness predicts public-institution potential better than market value.
- **H5** — Small collector-founded institutions can fill geographic and representational gaps large museums systematically leave open.
- **H6** — The best public-legacy structure depends as much on founder resources, governance, and community fit as on the collection itself.
- **H7A** *(market gap)* — Collectors lack a recognized *independent* advisor responsible for evaluating the full range of long-term collection pathways *before* a disposition/institutional decision is made.
- **H7B** *(willingness to pay — the commercial crux)* — Collectors and family offices will pay meaningful advisory fees for someone independent to own that decision. *You can prove H7A and still have no business: perhaps no one owns the decision because it isn't seen as a purchasable service — the attorney, art advisor, auction house and children simply sort it out. And unlike M&A (where a fee attaches to a transaction), the pre-decision may have no natural fee event — which is exactly why H7B must be tested, not assumed.*

- **H8** *(legacy vs. control — untested)* — Permanence often requires surrender. The collections that survive their founders may be those whose founders imposed *fewer* binding constraints (on display, sale, lending, governance, location), leaving future stewards room to adapt — so founder-constraint design may prove as predictive as endowment size. *(Barnes = the cautionary extreme of binding control; Neue Galerie→Met = a founder choosing continuity over independence.)*

*(The protocol's institutional-durability questions — endowment ratios, governance succession — are the later-funnel refinement of H3/H6 and are tested case by case.)*

### Evidence status — landscape sprint (2026-08)

**No structured case has been studied yet.** The below are *early signals from secondary sources*, not findings. Status vocabulary: **Untested · Insufficient evidence · Initial evidence for · Initial evidence against · Mixed.** Nothing is "supported" until we define what would count as support and study a substantive case base. This block exists to keep us honest — we have found better questions, not answers.

- **H2 (museums won't take it) — Initial evidence for.** Museums increasingly decline gifts; but donation *intentions* are common, so this is narrower than it looks. *Support would be:* N cases where a museum declined or constrained a gift. **Do not cite "6% donate" as proof.**
- **H3 (standalone museums suit only a minority) — Initial evidence for.** New-institution creation is rare relative to inheritance/retention. *Support would be:* the rate of standalone-museum vs. other pathways across the candidate universe.
- **H4/H5 (distinctiveness/gap over value) — Insufficient evidence.** Four vivid examples ≠ a predictor; this is precisely what we *hope* to find, so treat with suspicion. *Support would be:* a scored comparison across cases showing distinctiveness out-predicts value for durability/public resonance.
- **H7A (market gap) — Untested by us.** Competitors' websites don't count. Tested by the case-level **Decision Owner** field across ~30 cases.
- **H7B (willingness to pay) — Untested.** Only provable through market contact, not desk research.
- **H1 (collections disperse without planning) — Initial evidence against / restate.** Retention is the norm (72% keep; ~90% of Gen Z heirs kept; 80% plan to pass to family). Restated: *the transition is typically **unmanaged**, not that collections **disperse**.*

**Positioning note (deliberately NOT a decision):** the evidence downgrades "addressable market = museum-builders," but **rarity ≠ bad niche** — ~50 serious public-access projects/year in the US would be ample for a solo practice needing three (cf. M&A). So general succession-consulting is a **candidate wedge requiring validation (H7B)**, not a new positioning. **Do not reposition yet.**

## Cadence & automation stance

- **Design (agreed): daily automated *collection*, weekly human *consumption*.** Agents scan the broad signal environment daily — promised gifts, private-collection shows, estate auctions, foundation launches, museum construction/closures, deaccessioning, new private spaces, major loans, collector deaths, estate disputes, tax/regulatory moves — and *store* what's relevant. Once a week: **Nariway Signals**, ~5 genuinely important developments, not 50 links. Continuous sensing without continuous interruption.
- **Sequencing caution:** define the signal filter *first* (what counts as important) via one or two **manual** weekly cycles, then automate daily collection against that filter — otherwise the scan just accretes noise against undefined criteria. Stand it up alongside the Models candidate-universe work, not before the first case. Email delivery to the founder is a separate mechanism to arrange.

## Research method: source genealogy

For every major industry statistic, capture the chain *before* repeating it: **headline claim → citing source → original source → underlying dataset → formula / assumptions → sample → limitations → what Nariway can safely say.** The [$992B scope note](transfer-scope-note.md) is the prototype. Rigor about art-market numbers — most copied report-to-report unexamined — can become a Nariway signature, and it fits the founder's finance background.
