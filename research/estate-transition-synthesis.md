# Estate-transition synthesis — what the evidence base actually supports

The estate-facing read of Nariway's collection research, disciplined for an audience of attorneys and fiduciaries who are trained to notice imprecision.

## Read this first: what this evidence can and cannot do

The corpus is **141 significant collection transitions whose public facts are verified**, coded by pathway and outcome and tested against explicit hypotheses through repeated disconfirmation passes. It is a genuine, self-challenging body of work. It is also **curated and opportunistic, not a representative sample.** Public transitions (sales, disputes, museum openings) are far more visible than quiet family retention. So this document supports **patterns, mechanisms, precedents, recurring conditions, and counterexamples.** It does **not** support incidence rates, probabilities, or "X% of estates" claims, and nothing here should ever be stated that way.

The honest positioning of the whole thing: this is the **intellectual foundation** for Nariway's judgment, not a dataset that proves what any given estate will do. A client hires Nariway because there is a hard situation in front of them and they trust the judgment brought into it. The research is why that judgment is credible.

## Part 1 — Where significant art makes an estate more complicated

Framed as how collection transitions behave, not as a charge that professionals mishandle them.

### 1. The art decision often begins before anyone owns it
- **Confidence:** Well-supported (this is the market-gap hypothesis, tested against banks, art advisors, and state panels, none of which owns the independent strategic pre-decision).
- **Recurs in:** transitions where the collection question is handled by default across the attorney, heirs, an art advisor, and an auction house, with no single party responsible for the strategy.
- **Counterexamples:** planned bequests where someone did own it early (Charles Dee Mitchell's signed bequest to the Dallas Museum of Art; the Pearlman Foundation's staged plan; Cisneros's deliberate dispersal). These show ownership is possible and changes the outcome.
- **Mechanism:** each specialist optimizes their own slice; no one is accountable for the whole.
- **Can say:** in the cases studied, the strategic decision about a collection frequently lacks a single owner.
- **Cannot say:** a rate at which this happens.

### 2. Sale usually has an execution path already waiting; other outcomes have to be built
- **Confidence:** Emerging (the outcome is well-observed; the "why" is partly inference and should stay framed as such).
- **Recurs in:** estate and single-owner sales that had a ready commercial channel (Fisher-Landau to Sotheby's, the Herbert Lust estate contested between houses, Ganz, Blaquier, Medaugh, Joe Lewis, the Lehman liquidation).
- **Counterexamples:** intact placements that did happen (Mitchell to the DMA, Hsu-Tang to New York Historical, Kirk Edward Long to FAMSF), each of which required a relationship or a plan to be built first.
- **Mechanism:** a sale has a standing offer and an operational path; a gift, foundation, or retention structure has to be created and coordinated.
- **Can say:** sale often has an execution path already in place, while alternatives require someone to create and coordinate them.
- **Cannot say:** that estates "default to sale," as a proven or quantified claim.

### 3. A collector's wishes and an enforceable plan are not the same thing
- **Confidence:** Well-supported (tested across several cases and a named legal doctrine).
- **Recurs in:** documented instructions set aside or overridden. Robert Indiana's will directed his home become a public museum; the foundation's board later voted to sell instead, with no enforcement. Georgia O'Keeffe's "cannot be sold or broken up" gift to Fisk was modified by a court under cy-près. The Barnes indenture was altered only through decades of litigation. Valparaiso sold works over its founding director's objection.
- **Counterexamples:** narrow, honored constraints (Museo Poldi Pezzoli's terms holding for roughly 145 years).
- **Mechanism:** the cy-près doctrine lets a court approve a modified use when literal compliance becomes impractical; enforcement depends on a party with standing (a state attorney general, a willing heir) actually acting.
- **Can say:** a documented wish is only as durable as someone's standing and willingness to enforce it.
- **Cannot say:** that constraints generally fail, or generally hold.

### 4. Institutional interest cannot be assumed
- **Confidence:** Well-supported (the "insider exception," confirmed repeatedly).
- **Recurs in:** museums that readily accepted an intact collection almost always had a donor already tied to them (a trustee, board chair, or long-term partner). Cold, arm's-length offers of an intact collection rarely land as easily.
- **Counterexamples:** candidate arm's-length acceptances (Walther at the Met, Long at FAMSF, Portnoy at Syracuse) that, on closer look, each carried a prior institutional or personal-professional tie or a connector.
- **Mechanism:** board familiarity, collection fit, and the real cost of accepting and stewarding a collection make a prior relationship the usual precondition.
- **Can say:** prior institutional ties recur in successful intact placements, and a cold offer of a whole collection is harder to place than families often assume.
- **Cannot say:** that museums will not accept collections from people they do not already know.

### 5. Continuity depends on governance, not simply financial resources
- **Confidence:** Well-supported, with an important limit.
- **Recurs in:** collections that became institutions and survived tended to have an independent board and a locked succession; sole control meeting the founder's death without a structure is the recurring failure ("founder's disease"): Terra.
- **Counterexamples / limit:** the Corcoran had independent governance and still dissolved on money, so governance appears necessary but not sufficient. Multi-member boards dominated by one family across generations (Ohara, Sadberk Hanim) also avoided the sole-control collapse, so "family involvement" is not itself the risk.
- **Mechanism:** a structure that can make decisions after the founder is gone, versus a vacuum.
- **Can say:** governance design predicts institutional survival better than endowment size, though funding still constrains.
- **Cannot say:** that governance alone guarantees survival.

### 6. Different heirs can turn one collection into several decisions
- **Confidence:** Emerging (a pattern, deliberately stated without any rate).
- **Recurs in:** transitions that split across several pathways at once, which tend to co-occur with contested or distressed situations (Fisher-Landau, CAM Raleigh, the Herbert Lust estate, Valparaiso; the Thyssen-Bornemisza family conflict that ended in an outright sale to Spain).
- **Counterexamples:** branched dispositions driven by strength rather than conflict (a founder actively acquiring and expanding), which look nothing like the distressed ones.
- **Mechanism:** divergent heir intentions fragment one decision into many, each pulling toward a different outcome.
- **Can say:** when heirs diverge, one collection often becomes several separate decisions, and split dispositions recur among contested transitions.
- **Cannot say:** a success or failure rate for split dispositions.

### 7. Delay itself becomes a decision
- **Confidence:** Tentative (inference-heavy; hold loosely).
- **Recurs in:** decisions deferred until after death or through prolonged litigation, after which options narrow and the outcome often defaults to sale (Star of Hope's years of estate litigation preceding the board's sale vote; estates shopped between houses once no plan existed).
- **Mechanism:** time removes reversible options and lets the standing-offer path win.
- **Can say:** deferral tends to remove options over time.
- **Cannot say:** a causal rate.

## Part 2 — What tends to characterize transitions that went unusually well

The inverse, and probably the more valuable half. Read as the beginning of a methodology the cases reveal, not one Nariway invented. Same discipline: these are recurring characteristics of good outcomes, not guarantees.

1. **The decision had a clear owner, early.** Someone was responsible for the collection's strategy before death or a forced event (Mitchell's signed bequest; Pearlman's plan; Storm King's institutional footing).
2. **Alternatives were weighed before any sale process started,** rather than the sale channel being the only one with a plan.
3. **Institutional relationships were built in advance.** The placements that landed had a real prior tie or a cultivated fit, not a cold approach.
4. **Governance was independent and succession was locked** for the collections that became lasting institutions (the Menil, the Phillips, Chinati).
5. **Wishes were translated into operating structures,** not just stated. A governed foundation with an endowment and a workable instrument outlives a line in a will that no one is positioned to enforce.
6. **Constraints were scoped to survive.** Narrow, honorable terms (Poldi Pezzoli) held, where rigid, total control (Barnes) invited decades of litigation.
7. **Someone coordinated across the specialists.** In the cleaner outcomes there was a person, often an engaged family member, a foundation director, or an advisor, keeping the appraisal, legal, institutional, and logistics workstreams connected. This is precisely the role Nariway proposes to fill.

## Where this points

Two uses, in order. First, this is the raw material for **Nariway's intellectual point of view**, the one thing that should drive the report, LinkedIn, speaking, and referral conversations. The point of view is not "we have 141 cases." It is closer to: *the outcome for a significant collection is usually decided by whether anyone owns the decision early, and by whether wishes were turned into structures that can actually hold; sale is not chosen so much as defaulted to when neither is true.* That is a defensible, evidence-grounded position, and it is distinctly Nariway.

Second, Part 2 is the seed of an eventual **engagement methodology**, drawn from what the good outcomes had in common, to be confirmed and reshaped by real matters rather than published as doctrine now.

Open, and only answerable in the market: whether attorneys, fiduciaries, and families will pay an independent party to own this, and for what. The research is explicit that this cannot be settled at the desk.
